Topic 3 of the SPAC section

CAM Photosynthesis

The same two carboxylases as C4, separated in time instead of space: CO₂ is captured at night into malic acid, stored in the vacuole, and refixed by Rubisco behind closed stomata the next day. The result is the highest water-use efficiency in the plant kingdom, bought with ATP, succulence and slow growth.

The one-paragraph version

In crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM) the stomata open at night, when the vapour pressure deficit is low. PEP carboxylase (PEPC) fixes bicarbonate into oxaloacetate, which is reduced to malate and pumped into the vacuole as malic acid; the leaf becomes measurably sour by dawn, and the PEP comes from nocturnal breakdown of starch or soluble sugars. During the day the stomata close, malic acid leaves the vacuole and is decarboxylated (by NAD(P)-malic enzyme or PEP carboxykinase), raising internal CO₂ to 1–2.5% so that Rubisco runs saturated and almost without photorespiration while the carbon skeletons are rebuilt into storage carbohydrate by gluconeogenesis. Osmond (1978) divided the cycle into four phases: I (night, PEPC), II (dawn transition), III (day, decarboxylation behind closed stomata) and IV (late afternoon, direct C3 fixation when the acid is exhausted). Because water is lost mainly at night, CAM plants need only 50–100 g water per g CO₂ compared with 250–350 for C4 and 400–600 or more for C3. The cost is 5.5–6.5 ATP per CO₂, a vacuole large enough to hold the night's acid (hence succulence), a daily carbon gain capped by that storage capacity, and typically slow growth. CAM is highly plastic: obligate in cacti and agaves, inducible by drought or salt in facultative species, and reducible to CAM-cycling or CAM-idling when water is short. About 6–7% of vascular plant species, in at least 36 families, use it.

Examiner's lens

CAM questions test whether you can reason about a temporal separation: what is open, active, stored or depleted at each hour, and why. Expect to draw the four phases, to explain the regulation that stops PEPC and Rubisco from competing (PEPC kinase, malate feedback), to compute water-use efficiency from VPD, and to say why CAM is nearly absent from land-surface models and what a CAM module (Bartlett et al. 2014, Hartzell et al. 2018) must contain.

Osmond's four phases

The diurnal course of net CO₂ exchange in a well-watered CAM plant has a characteristic shape: a broad nocturnal plateau, a dawn burst, a daytime trough at or near zero, and an afternoon rise. Osmond (1978) [1] gave the four regions names that are still used in every CAM paper.

Night and day biochemistry

Night: PEPC, its kinase, and the vacuole

Carbonic anhydrase converts CO₂ to HCO₃⁻, and PEPC carboxylates PEP to oxaloacetate, which cytosolic NAD-malate dehydrogenase reduces to malate using NADH from glycolysis. The enzyme is the same as in C4 and is equally O₂-insensitive. What makes CAM work is regulation in time. PEPC is feedback-inhibited by malate; in the dark a circadian-clock-controlled PEPC kinase (PPCK) is transcribed and phosphorylates a serine near the N-terminus, which raises the Ki for malate about ten-fold and lets PEPC keep working while malate accumulates. At dawn PPCK expression stops, a type-2A phosphatase dephosphorylates PEPC, and the enzyme becomes malate-sensitive just as malate floods out of the vacuole, so it switches off and does not refix the CO₂ meant for Rubisco. Stomata follow the same inverted schedule: they open in the dark, probably in response to low internal CO₂ created by PEPC, and close in the light as decarboxylation raises Ci.

Malate is stored as malic acid: two protons per malate are pumped across the tonoplast by the vacuolar H⁺-ATPase (and H⁺-pyrophosphatase), and malate²⁻ follows through anion channels of the ALMT family down the electrical gradient. Overnight the vacuole reaches 100–300 mM malic acid (over 1 M in some Clusia, with citric acid), and its pH falls to 3–3.5. This storage sets several constraints. The osmotic load is large, so cells need big vacuoles and a thick, succulent mesophyll; vacuolar volume per unit leaf area therefore limits the night's carbon gain, which is why nocturnal uptake slows toward dawn even if PEPC capacity remains. The acid also has to be made from something: a hexose equivalent, taken from starch (in Kalanchoë, Mesembryanthemum) or from soluble sugars (in Agave, Ananas, Clusia), is glycolysed to two PEP, so the plant carries a daily carbohydrate reserve at least as large as its nocturnal acid pool.

Day: decarboxylation, gluconeogenesis and internal CO₂

Malic acid effluxes passively from the vacuole once light-driven consumption lowers cytosolic malate. Decarboxylation uses the same enzymes as the C4 subtypes: NADP-ME (cytosol/chloroplast) or NAD-ME (mitochondria) in Kalanchoë, Agave, cacti and most Crassulaceae, producing pyruvate that PPDK converts to PEP; or PEPCK in pineapple (Ananas), Clusia and Aloe, producing PEP directly at one ATP. The released CO₂ accumulates behind closed stomata to 1–2.5% (10,000–25,000 µbar), so Rubisco is CO₂-saturated and photorespiration is suppressed, although internal O₂ also rises above 21% and some oxygenation does occur. The PEP is returned to triose-P and hexose by gluconeogenesis (reversing glycolysis, with fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase as the key regulated step) and stored as starch or sugars for the next night. The carbon fixed by Rubisco, by contrast, is exported or stored for growth. Partitioning between "reserve for tomorrow's acid" and "growth" is itself regulated and is a major reason CAM plants grow slowly.

Energetics compared with C3 and C4

night: hexose → 2 PEP (glycolysis) ; 2 HCO₃⁻ + 2 PEP → 2 malate ; 2 malate + 4 H⁺ → vacuole (V-ATPase, ~2 H⁺ per ATP)
day: malate → CO₂ + pyruvate (ME) ; pyruvate + ATP → PEP (PPDK, ≈ 2 ATP) ; 2 PEP → hexose (gluconeogenesis, ATP + NADPH) ; CO₂ → CBB cycle (3 ATP + 2 NADPH)

Summing the bill gives roughly 5.5 ATP and 2 NADPH per CO₂ for a starch-storing NAD(P)-ME plant, rising to about 6.5 ATP for species that store soluble sugars (sucrose synthesis and hydrolysis cost extra), against 3 ATP for C3 and 5 ATP for C4 (Winter & Smith 1996 [2]). The extra ATP over C4 is the vacuolar pumping and the gluconeogenic reconstruction of the acceptor; the extra time is a whole night. CAM therefore makes sense only where the water saved is worth more than the ATP and the growth rate forgone.

Water-use efficiency: why night matters

Leaf-level water-use efficiency is set by the ratio of the CO₂ and water vapour gradients across the stomata. With A = gsc(Ca − Ci) and E = 1.6 gsc D/P,

$$\frac{A}{E} = \frac{C_a\,(1 - C_i/C_a)\,P}{1.6\,D}, \qquad D = e_s(T_{leaf}) - e_a, \qquad e_s(T) = 0.6108\,\exp\!\left(\frac{17.27\,T}{T + 237.3}\right)\ \text{kPa}$$

(the Tetens formula for saturation vapour pressure). The C4 pathway improves A/E through the numerator (Ci/Ca ≈ 0.4). CAM improves it through the denominator: at night the leaf is cooler and the air more humid, so D is typically 0.2–0.5 kPa rather than the 2–4 kPa of a desert afternoon, a factor of 5–10 in water cost per CO₂. Integrated over a growing season this yields transpiration ratios of about 50–100 g H₂O per g CO₂ for CAM, 250–350 for C4 and 400–600 or more for C3. CAM plasticity and environmental control

CAM is less a category than a continuum of expression [6, 7]. Obligate (constitutive) CAM plants, such as Opuntia, Agave, Kalanchoë daigremontiana and pineapple, perform CAM in all conditions once mature, though the size of phases II and IV still responds to water. Facultative (inducible) CAM plants are C3 when well watered and switch to CAM under drought, salinity or, in Kalanchoë blossfeldiana, short days; the ice plant Mesembryanthemum crystallinum, in which salt stress induces PEPC, PPCK and tonoplast transporter genes within days, is the classic system, and Clusia, Talinum and Portulaca are others. The switch is reversible on rewatering. CAM-cycling describes plants that take up CO₂ by C3 photosynthesis in the day but keep their stomata shut at night and refix respiratory CO₂ into malate, producing a small day–night acid fluctuation and a modest water saving. CAM-idling is the drought end-point of obligate CAM: stomata closed day and night, acid fluctuation driven entirely by recycled respiratory CO₂, zero net carbon gain but maintained metabolism.

Environmental controls act on identifiable steps. Night temperature is the strongest: nocturnal acid accumulation in Agave and Opuntia is maximal near 10–15 °C and falls steeply above 25 °C because PEPC becomes more malate-sensitive, respiration rises, and malate leaks from the vacuole, whereas day temperature matters comparatively little; this is why CAM productivity is highest in climates with warm days and cool nights (Nobel 1988, 1991 [8]). Water deficit narrows the phases as described above and induces CAM in facultative species through ABA-dependent signalling. Salinity acts like drought in M. crystallinum. Light during the preceding day sets the carbohydrate reserve for the night's PEP and controls the clock that times PPCK. Elevated CO₂ stimulates CAM plants substantially (nocturnal PEPC is not saturated at ambient Ci, and phase IV Rubisco fixation gains as in C3), so CAM crops are among the beneficiaries of rising CO₂. Nutrients, especially N and P, limit CAM in the same way as other pathways, and the productivity index approach of Nobel multiplies water, temperature, light and nutrient indices to predict yield.

Carbon isotopes in CAM plants

Night-time carbon enters through PEPC, whose effective fractionation is small and negative (b₄ ≈ −5.7‰), so carbon fixed in phase I has a tissue δ¹³C of about −11 to −13‰, indistinguishable from C4. Carbon fixed in phases II and IV enters through Rubisco in the ordinary C3 manner and carries δ¹³C ≈ −26 to −28‰. A CAM plant's bulk tissue therefore lies between these end-members in proportion to the share of carbon taken up at night (Winter & Holtum 2002 established the near-linear relationship [6]):

$$\delta^{13}\mathrm{C}_{plant} \approx f\,\delta_{night} + (1 - f)\,\delta_{day}, \qquad \delta_{night} \approx -11.5‰,\ \ \delta_{day} \approx -27‰$$

Three consequences. First, CAM δ¹³C spans −10 to −28‰, the widest range of any pathway, and a value alone cannot distinguish C4 from strong CAM; succulence, nocturnal acidification and gas exchange are needed. Second, weak and facultative CAM is invisible to bulk δ¹³C: a plant obtaining a third of its carbon at night still reads about −22‰, inside the C3 range, which is why surveys that classify CAM by a −20‰ threshold underestimate its frequency. Third, because CAM δ¹³C moves with the environment, it is a useful integrator: the same Clusia or Kalanchoë individual becomes less negative in a dry season. CAM crops, bioenergy and models

Despite slow growth per unit leaf area, well-managed CAM crops can be very productive because they keep canopies and stomatal activity going through dry seasons that stop C3 and C4 crops. Nobel (1991) [8] reported 38–43 Mg dry mass ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ for Agave mapisaga and A. salmiana and up to 47 Mg for Opuntia ficus-indica under near-optimal conditions, comparable to the best C4 crops, and 10–34 Mg under ordinary cultivation. Borland et al. (2009) [3] and Davis, Dohleman and Long (2011) [11] argued that agaves and prickly pear could supply lignocellulosic or fermentable feedstock from semi-arid land receiving 300–800 mm rainfall, land that does not compete with food crops; Owen and Griffiths (2014) mapped the potential for four CAM species across Australia using Nobel's environmental productivity index (EPI), the product of water, temperature, PAR and nutrient indices each scaled 0–1 by empirical response curves. Agave tequilana and A. fourcroydes (henequen) have established agronomy, high soluble sugar (fructan) content and ten-year rotations; Opuntia is already grown on about a million hectares for forage and fruit. Borland et al. (2014) [4] extend the argument to engineering CAM into C3 crops, which requires the nocturnal PEPC circuit, the tonoplast transport and the stomatal rhythm to be moved together.

Connections to ecosystem models

No major land-surface model (CLM, JULES, ORCHIDEE, Noah-MP) includes a CAM plant functional type: CAM species are a small share of global biomass, their phenomena are sub-daily, and the storage dynamics do not fit the instantaneous A = min(...) structure of FvCB and Collatz. Three modelling efforts fill the gap. Owen and Griffiths (2013) [10] built a system-dynamics model in which PEPC activity, PPCK expression, vacuolar malate, carbohydrate reserves and stomata are state variables with explicit feedbacks; it predicts the extent of phases I–IV from physiology and environment and reproduces the shrinking of II and IV under drought. Bartlett, Vico and Porporato (2014) [9] coupled a circadian-rhythm oscillator that gates malic acid storage and release to an FvCB bundle with a CO₂ mass balance of the cell, a stomatal model and a soil water balance, giving a tractable description of daily carbon gain and transpiration against VPD, temperature and soil moisture. Hartzell, Bartlett and Porporato (2018) [12] generalised this into Photo3, a single framework in which C3, C4 and CAM share the FvCB core and the same plant hydraulics, differing in the CO₂ supply term (ambient for C3, pumped for C4, stored malate for CAM); it is the first model that lets a dynamic vegetation or hydrology scheme compare the three pathways on equal terms. For a crop-scale CAM representation, the empirical EPI of Nobel remains the tool actually used in bioenergy assessments [11], and models such as BioCro or DSSAT have no CAM module. The key ingredients any CAM scheme needs are: a malate pool with a storage ceiling that depends on succulence; a nocturnal PEPC rate controlled by temperature and malate feedback; a daytime decarboxylation rate that sets internal CO₂ for FvCB; a stomatal rule driven by internal CO₂ rather than light; and a carbohydrate reserve that links today's light to tonight's acid.

Exam-style questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer.

1. Sketch the diurnal course of net CO₂ exchange, stomatal conductance and leaf acidity of a well-watered CAM plant, label Osmond's phases, and state what controls each transition.

Net CO₂ uptake is positive through the night (phase I, broad plateau of 3–10 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, declining toward dawn as the vacuole fills); it spikes briefly after sunrise (phase II) as Rubisco activates while stomata are still open; it falls to about zero for most of the day (phase III, stomata closed, Ci 1–2.5%); and rises again in the late afternoon (phase IV) when the acid is depleted and stomata reopen for direct C3 fixation. Stomatal conductance mirrors this: high at night, a dawn shoulder, near zero at midday, moderate in late afternoon. Titratable acidity rises through the night to a dawn maximum and falls through phase III to a minimum by mid-afternoon. Transitions are controlled by (i) the circadian clock and light: PPCK transcription stops at dawn, PEPC is dephosphorylated and becomes malate-sensitive, Rubisco is activated; (ii) metabolite feedback: malate efflux inhibits PEPC and raises internal CO₂, closing stomata; (iii) exhaustion of the vacuolar acid pool, which lets internal CO₂ fall, stomata reopen and phase IV begin; (iv) darkness and renewed PPCK expression, which restart phase I. Water status scales the whole pattern: drought removes IV, then II, then narrows I.

2. Explain how PEP carboxylase and Rubisco are prevented from competing for CO₂ in a CAM leaf, and contrast this with the solution used by C4 plants.

C4 plants separate the two carboxylases in space (mesophyll vs bundle sheath). CAM plants have both in the same cell and separate them in time by regulating PEPC. At night PEPC kinase, whose gene is transcribed under circadian control in the dark, phosphorylates PEPC, raising its Ki for malate about ten-fold so the enzyme keeps running as malate accumulates; Rubisco is inactive in the dark anyway, and the stomata are open. At dawn kinase expression ceases, a PP2A phosphatase dephosphorylates PEPC, and the enzyme becomes strongly inhibited by the malate now flooding out of the vacuole, so PEPC shuts down just as Rubisco becomes active; the stomata close as internal CO₂ rises, removing the external supply that PEPC could otherwise fix. Phase II is the brief period when both run, which is why it shows a burst of uptake. In the late afternoon the sequence reverses in phase IV. The C4 solution is anatomically expensive but allows continuous high rates; the CAM solution needs only one cell type but imposes a storage ceiling, a 24-h cycle and the extra ATP for vacuolar transport and gluconeogenesis.

3. Account for the ATP and NADPH cost of one CO₂ fixed by CAM and compare it with C3 and C4. Where does the extra energy go and when is it worth paying?

Night: half a hexose from starch is glycolysed to PEP (roughly ATP-neutral to slightly positive, and generating the NADH used to reduce OAA to malate); PEPC carboxylation is free; storing malic acid requires two H⁺ per malate pumped by the V-ATPase at about two H⁺ per ATP, ~1 ATP. Day: malate decarboxylation by NAD(P)-ME yields pyruvate (and NAD(P)H); PPDK regenerates PEP at 2 ATP equivalents (1 ATP in PEPCK species); gluconeogenesis back to hexose costs about 1 ATP and 1 NADPH per triose; the CBB cycle costs 3 ATP and 2 NADPH. Total ≈ 5.5 ATP + 2 NADPH per CO₂ for starch-storing ME types, up to 6.5 ATP for sugar-storing species that must also make and hydrolyse sucrose; compare 3 ATP + 2 NADPH for C3 (plus photorespiration in air) and 5 ATP + 2 NADPH for C4. The surplus over C4 is the tonoplast pumping and the gluconeogenic rebuild of the acceptor. Because CAM plants are light-saturated for most of phase III and have abundant ATP, energy is rarely the binding constraint; the pathway pays whenever the water saved (5–10 × less per CO₂ than C3) is limiting growth more than the ATP and the storage-capped daily carbon gain are, i.e. in semi-arid climates with cool nights and in epiphytic habitats with intermittent water.

4. Using the Tetens equation, estimate the leaf-level water-use efficiency of a C3 leaf at 32 °C and 30% RH and of a CAM leaf at 18 °C and 75% RH at night, and explain the result.

es(32 °C) = 0.6108 exp(17.27 × 32/269.3) = 4.76 kPa; Dday = 4.76 × 0.7 = 3.33 kPa. es(18 °C) = 2.06 kPa; Dnight = 2.06 × 0.25 = 0.52 kPa. With A/E = Ca(1 − Ci/Ca)P/(1.6D) and Ca = 420 µmol mol⁻¹, P = 101.3 kPa: C3 (Ci/Ca = 0.7): 420 × 0.3 × 101.3/(1.6 × 3.33) ≈ 2400 µmol mol⁻¹ ≈ 2.4 mmol CO₂ per mol H₂O, i.e. about 420 mol H₂O per mol CO₂ or 170 g H₂O per g CO₂. CAM phase I (Ci/Ca ≈ 0.5): 420 × 0.5 × 101.3/(1.6 × 0.52) ≈ 25,600 µmol mol⁻¹ ≈ 26 mmol mol⁻¹, about 39 mol H₂O per mol CO₂ or 16 g g⁻¹. The CAM leaf is roughly ten times more water-efficient, and only about 1.7 of that factor comes from the lower Ci/Ca; the rest is the six-fold lower VPD. Whole-plant transpiration ratios are 3–5 times higher than these instantaneous values because of respiration, cuticular loss and phases II and IV, which brings them to the textbook 50–100 g g⁻¹ for CAM and 400–600 for C3. If the night is as hot and dry as the day the advantage vanishes, which constrains where CAM succeeds.

5. Why do cool nights favour CAM, and what does this imply for CAM crops under climate warming?

Nocturnal acid accumulation in agaves and cacti peaks at about 10–15 °C and declines steeply above 25 °C. Mechanisms: PEPC's sensitivity to malate inhibition increases with temperature, so the enzyme shuts off at a lower malate concentration; the tonoplast becomes leakier and passive malate efflux rises, creating a futile cycle; nocturnal respiration (Q₁₀ ≈ 2) consumes more of the carbohydrate reserve needed for PEP and more of the fixed carbon; and the vapour pressure deficit is higher, so the stomata either open less or lose more water per CO₂. Daytime decarboxylation and Rubisco, in contrast, tolerate high temperatures well, so CAM productivity depends on the day–night temperature amplitude more than on the mean. Under warming, regions with mild nights (highlands, coastal deserts, subtropics with large diurnal ranges) retain their CAM advantage, while hot-night lowlands lose it; elevated CO₂ partly compensates because nocturnal PEPC fixation and phase IV Rubisco fixation both respond positively to CO₂. For bioenergy planning this means siting agave or opuntia by night temperature and rainfall rather than by mean annual temperature, which is exactly what the temperature index in Nobel's EPI and the Owen and Griffiths (2014) maps do.

6. A leaf has δ¹³C = −18‰. What can and cannot be concluded about its photosynthetic pathway, and what further measurements would you make?

−18‰ is outside the C3 range (−22 to −35‰) and slightly more negative than typical C4 (−11 to −14‰). It is consistent with (a) a CAM plant obtaining roughly 60% of its carbon at night (linear mixing between −11.5 and −27‰ gives f ≈ 0.58), (b) a C4 plant with unusually high leakiness or high Ci/Ca, or (c) in principle a C3–C4 intermediate. A value alone cannot settle it because PEPC-derived carbon has the same signature whether it is fixed in a bundle sheath at noon or a vacuole at midnight. Confirmatory measurements: diurnal titratable acidity (a dawn–dusk ΔH⁺ of > 50 µmol g⁻¹ FW indicates CAM), 24-h gas exchange to see whether uptake is nocturnal, leaf anatomy (Kranz vs succulent mesophyll with large vacuoles), and online Δ¹³C during the day and night. Also recall that a C3-range value does not exclude CAM: a facultative species with a third of its carbon from night-time fixation reads about −22‰, so weak CAM is invisible to bulk isotopes.

7. Why is CAM absent from most land-surface models, and what state variables and processes would a CAM module need? Refer to the Bartlett et al. (2014) and Hartzell et al. (2018) approaches.

Land-surface models compute photosynthesis as an instantaneous function of light, temperature, CO₂ and water (FvCB or Collatz), with stomata responding to the same drivers, and they aggregate vegetation into a few plant functional types weighted by biomass. CAM violates the instantaneous assumption: today's carbon gain depends on last night's acid, which depends on yesterday's carbohydrate, and stomata open in the dark. CAM species also hold a small share of global biomass, so the effort was not prioritised. A module needs: a vacuolar malate pool with a capacity set by succulence and a release rate in the light; a nocturnal PEPC rate limited by temperature, malate feedback, carbohydrate reserve and stomatal supply; a daytime internal CO₂ computed from decarboxylation that feeds the FvCB equations; a stomatal rule driven by internal CO₂ and the clock rather than by light; and a carbohydrate reserve linking the two halves of the day. Bartlett et al. (2014) achieved this with a circadian oscillator that gates storage and release, coupled to FvCB, a stomatal model and a soil water balance, and used it to quantify CAM WUE and productivity against VPD and soil moisture. Hartzell et al. (2018) generalised it as Photo3, in which C3, C4 and CAM share one FvCB core and one hydraulic scheme and differ only in how CO₂ reaches Rubisco; this lets a hydrological or vegetation model compare the pathways and simulate CAM crops on marginal land. Owen and Griffiths (2013) offer a more mechanistic, regulation-explicit alternative suited to predicting phase durations.

8. Compare C3, C4 and CAM as strategies for the same environmental problem, and explain why each is favoured where it occurs.

All three answer the problem that Rubisco is slow, oxygenates, and needs CO₂ delivered through stomata that lose water. C3 accepts photorespiration and water loss in return for the lowest energy cost (3 ATP per CO₂) and the fastest potential growth where water and cool temperatures permit; it dominates mesic, temperate and shaded habitats and ~85–90% of species. C4 adds a spatial CO₂ pump (2 extra ATP) that removes photorespiration, halves the water cost through low Ci/Ca, and raises N-use efficiency, at the price of poorer performance in cool and low-light conditions; it dominates hot, open, seasonally dry grasslands and the most productive crops. CAM adds a temporal pump that shifts water loss to the night, cutting water cost by another factor of 5–10 and permitting survival through long droughts by idling, at the price of 5.5–6.5 ATP per CO₂, a storage-limited daily uptake, succulence and slow growth; it dominates deserts with cool nights, rocky and saline sites and the epiphyte niche of tropical canopies, where water is intermittent even though humidity is high. The three are not discrete: C3–C4 intermediates and facultative or weak CAM fill the gaps, and many lineages have evolved C4 (> 60 origins) and CAM (dozens of origins) independently whenever the local balance of water, temperature, light and CO₂ made the extra ATP worthwhile.
