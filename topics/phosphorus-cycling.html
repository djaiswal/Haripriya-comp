Topic 6 of the Biogeochemistry section

Phosphorus Loss and Retention

Phosphorus has no gas phase and arrives only by rock weathering and dust, so its fate is decided by surfaces: whether phosphate is held on Fe and Al oxides, locked in calcium minerals, bound in organic molecules, or carried away on sediment. This page covers the chemistry, the pedogenic trajectory, the loss pathways that drive eutrophication, and how models handle P.

The one-paragraph version

Phosphorus enters ecosystems almost entirely from the weathering of apatite in parent material (plus a trickle of dust), and leaves only in water or on sediment, because, unlike nitrogen, it has no significant gaseous form. Walker and Syers [1] showed that this makes soil P a one-way clock: primary mineral P declines with soil age, is converted into organic P, labile sorbed P and occluded P buried inside Fe and Al oxide coatings, and the total slowly leaks away until an old soil is P-limited and the ecosystem survives on tight recycling. In the soil solution, orthophosphate speciation is set by pH (pKa 2.15, 7.20, 12.35); its concentration is buffered by sorption on oxide surfaces in acid soils and by precipitation with calcium in calcareous soils, so solution P is typically only 0.01–0.3 mg L⁻¹ and plants have evolved mycorrhizae, cluster roots, carboxylate exudation and phosphatases to get at the rest. Organic P (30–65% of total) is mineralised by phosphatases in response to demand, largely independent of carbon flow. Agricultural soils have been loaded with fertiliser P for a century; the resulting legacy P is lost by erosion (particulate P) and runoff (dissolved P, rising sharply above a soil-test change point), and causes freshwater eutrophication that can persist for decades. Land-surface models now add P pools with Langmuir-type sorption, and P limitation may halve the CO₂ fertilisation effect in tropical forests.

Examiner's lens

Expect to be asked to contrast N and P (gas phase, source, loss mechanism, limitation with soil age), to interpret Hedley fractions or a sorption isotherm, to explain why P moves mainly with sediment and why dissolved P loss is non-linear in soil test P, to describe plant and microbial P acquisition, and to say how a model such as ELM, CASA-CNP or ORCHIDEE-CNP represents sorption and why that matters for tropical carbon uptake.

Rock-derived P and the Walker–Syers trajectory

Phosphorus is about 0.1% of the crust, almost all as the calcium phosphate apatite, Ca₅(PO₄)₃(F,Cl,OH). Acid hydrolysis of apatite (accelerated by carbonic acid, organic acids and root and microbial exudates) releases orthophosphate at rates of 0.01–1 kg P ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹, far less than annual plant uptake (5–30 kg), so ecosystems live on recycling. Total soil P ranges from ~100 to ~2000 mg kg⁻¹ (median ~500), and because P is retained strongly by sorption, the soil's P stock is an archive of its history.

Walker and Syers [1], using New Zealand chronosequences, proposed the conceptual model every P paper cites. At time zero all P is in primary minerals. Weathering transfers it into (i) non-occluded (labile) inorganic P sorbed on surfaces, (ii) organic P in biomass and soil organic matter, which accumulates rapidly then declines, and (iii) occluded P, phosphate trapped within secondary Fe and Al oxides and hydroxides as they crystallise and coat sorbed P, which becomes the dominant form in old soils. Leaching and erosion remove P continuously, so total P declines monotonically toward a terminal steady state dominated by occluded and organic forms, at which point the ecosystem is strongly P-limited. Nitrogen follows the opposite course, accumulating from zero by fixation, so young soils are N-limited and old soils P-limited. Crews et al. [5] confirmed this on the Hawaiian chronosequence (300 yr to 4.1 Myr lava flows): N limitation and high primary P at the young site, P limitation, most P occluded, and the highest N:P at the oldest site; Vitousek (2004) and Vitousek et al. [4] generalised the picture into six mechanisms of P limitation: depletion-driven (Walker–Syers), soil barrier (sorption and occlusion), transactional (slow organic P turnover), low-P parent material, sink-driven (high demand), and anthropogenic.

Forms of P in soil and the chemistry that holds them

Solution P and speciation

Orthophosphoric acid dissociates in three steps with pKa values of 2.15, 7.20 and 12.35, so in the pH range of soils the solution contains H₂PO₄⁻ (dominant below 7.2) and HPO₄²⁻ (dominant above), with PO₄³⁻ negligible until pH > 11. Plants take up mainly H₂PO₄⁻. The fractions follow directly from the equilibria:

$$\alpha_{H_2PO_4^-} = \frac{10^{pH - pK_1}}{1 + 10^{pH - pK_1} + 10^{2pH - pK_1 - pK_2} + 10^{3pH - pK_1 - pK_2 - pK_3}}$$

Speciation matters because the different anions have different affinities for mineral surfaces and different solubility products with Ca, Fe and Al, which is why P availability is maximal near pH 6–7 and falls off on both sides.

Sorption on Fe and Al oxides

In acid and neutral soils the main sink is ligand exchange: phosphate displaces hydroxyl or water from the surface of goethite, ferrihydrite, gibbsite and allophane and forms inner-sphere bidentate complexes. This is fast (minutes to hours) and partly reversible, followed by slow diffusion into micropores and precipitation that is less reversible. Sorption is described by isotherms. The Langmuir form assumes a finite number of identical sites:

$$Q = \frac{Q_{max} K C}{1 + K C}, \qquad \text{buffer capacity } \frac{dQ}{dC} = \frac{Q_{max} K}{(1 + KC)^2},$$

where Q is sorbed P (mg kg⁻¹), C is solution P (mg L⁻¹), Qmax the sorption maximum, and K the affinity. The Freundlich form Q = Kf Cn (n ≈ 0.3–0.7) has no maximum and fits heterogeneous surfaces better at low C. Two derived quantities are central. The equilibrium P concentration at zero net sorption, EPC₀, is the solution concentration in equilibrium with the soil's current sorbed P: below it the soil releases P, above it the soil sorbs, so EPC₀ predicts whether a soil or sediment will act as a P source or sink in contact with runoff or a lake. The degree of P saturation,

$$DPS = \frac{P_{ox}}{\alpha\,(Fe_{ox} + Al_{ox})} \times 100\%, \qquad \alpha \approx 0.5,$$

uses acid ammonium oxalate extraction of amorphous Fe, Al and P (mmol kg⁻¹) to express how full the sorption capacity is; Dutch work set a critical DPS of ~25%, above which dissolved P in drainage rises steeply. Qmax scales with oxalate Fe + Al, which is why Oxisols and Andisols (allophane) fix enormous amounts of fertiliser P and sandy soils fix little.

Calcium-bound, occluded and organic P

In calcareous and alkaline soils the sink is precipitation: added phosphate reacts with Ca²⁺ to form dicalcium phosphate dihydrate, then octacalcium phosphate, then over months to years the less soluble hydroxyapatite, and it also sorbs on calcite surfaces. Availability is low despite high total P, and acidifying fertilisers or band placement help. Occluded P is phosphate physically enclosed within Fe and Al oxide coatings or inside aggregates; it is unavailable on decadal timescales and is extracted only by strong reductants or residual digestion. Organic P is 30–65% of total P in most surface soils (higher in old, weathered and in organic soils). Its main components are inositol phosphates (phytate, up to 50% of organic P; strongly sorbed on oxides and Ca, so stable), nucleic acids, phospholipids and sugar phosphates (fast-cycling), and phosphonates. McGill and Cole [6] made the distinction that organises P mineralisation: C–O–P ester bonds can be cleaved by extracellular phosphatases (phosphomonoesterases, phosphodiesterases, phytases) released in response to P demand, so organic P is mineralised biochemically and largely independently of the C flow, whereas C-bonded N and S are mineralised biologically as a by-product of C oxidation. Phosphatase activity therefore rises when P is scarce and is suppressed by phosphate, a regulatory loop visible in the ecoenzymatic ratios on the stoichiometry page. Microbial P (chloroform-labile, 5–50 kg P ha⁻¹, often as polyphosphate) is a large, fast pool relative to solution P; its turnover on drying–rewetting (the Birch effect) and freeze–thaw releases pulses of phosphate, and in P-poor tropical soils microbial P can exceed plant-available inorganic P.

Hedley fractionation

Hedley, Stewart and Chauhan [2] introduced sequential extraction to apportion soil P by availability, later refined by Tiessen and Moir: anion-exchange resin (freely exchangeable Pi), 0.5 M NaHCO₃ (labile Pi and Po), 0.1 M NaOH (Fe/Al-associated Pi and more stable Po; sometimes after sonication for aggregate-protected P), 1 M HCl (Ca-bound, mostly apatite), and residual (concentrated acid digest; occluded and recalcitrant organic). Cross and Schlesinger [3] meta-analysed 88 soils in Hedley terms and found that along weathering gradients HCl-Pi (primary) declines, NaOH-Pi and residual P increase, and organic P peaks in moderately weathered soils, exactly the Walker–Syers trajectory expressed as fractions. The method is operational, not mechanistic: the fractions are defined by the extractant, extractions can redistribute P, and NaOH-Po is a mixture, so interpret with care. Several models (ELM/E3SM, CASA-CNP) map their P pools directly onto Hedley fractions so that the fractionation data can initialise and test them.

How plants and microbes get P, and what redox and dust add

Because solution P is tiny and immobile (diffusion coefficient ~10⁻¹² to 10⁻¹⁵ m² s⁻¹ in soil; a depletion zone of only 1–2 mm forms around roots), P acquisition is about increasing contact with soil and about chemistry at the root surface. Lambers et al. [8] organise the strategies along a gradient: (i) mycorrhizal symbiosis, in which arbuscular and ectomycorrhizal hyphae extend the depletion zone by centimetres and deliver P for carbon (the dominant strategy in most ecosystems; 80% of plant families); (ii) root morphology, with fine roots, long root hairs, high specific root length, and on the most P-impoverished soils (south-western Australia, the Cape, old tropical sites) non-mycorrhizal cluster roots (Proteaceae, lupins) that release pulses of carboxylates; (iii) rhizosphere chemistry (Hinsinger [7]): citrate, malate and oxalate exudation that desorbs P by ligand exchange and complexes Fe, Al and Ca; proton or hydroxyl extrusion that shifts pH toward the availability optimum; and secretion of acid phosphatases and, with microbial help, phytases that mineralise organic P; and (iv) internal economy: high P-use efficiency, P resorption (~65%) and replacement of phospholipids by galacto- and sulpholipids. Richardson and Simpson [9] review the microbial side: phosphate-solubilising bacteria and fungi (Pseudomonas, Bacillus, Penicillium) using organic acids and phosphatases, and the mycorrhizal pathway as the main route in most plants.

Redox. Flooding and waterlogging drive reductive dissolution of Fe(III) oxides to Fe²⁺ (Eh < ~ +100 mV), releasing the phosphate sorbed on or precipitated with them. This is why paddy rice often needs less P fertiliser than upland crops, why wetland soils release P on inundation, and why lakes show internal loading: anoxic hypolimnia in summer dissolve Fe-bound P from sediments and return it to the water column, prolonging eutrophication for decades after external loads are cut. Re-oxidation re-precipitates ferric phosphate and ferrihydrite that scavenge P again, so redox oscillations pump P between forms.

Dust. In weathering-limited systems the only external P input is atmospheric. Okin et al. [10] estimated that mineral dust carries on the order of 1 Tg P yr⁻¹ to the global land surface, and Yu et al. [11] used CALIPSO lidar to estimate that ~22 Tg of Saharan dust, carrying about 22 Gg of P (~0.02 Tg), reaches the Amazon each year, a quantity comparable to the basin's hydrological P loss, which is why Bodélé Depression dust is invoked as the long-term P subsidy that keeps old Amazonian soils productive. Supply-limited versus weathering-limited: on young or eroding landscapes (mountains, glaciated terrain, volcanic ash) P supply from fresh minerals exceeds demand and N limits; on old stable surfaces weathering has exhausted the supply, P is held in occluded and organic forms, and dust and tight recycling sustain the system.

Loss pathways: erosion, runoff, leaching, and the lake

Haygarth et al. [12] describe P transfer as a continuum of sources (soil P status, fertiliser and manure, plant residues), mobilisation (solubilisation into water or detachment of particles), delivery (overland flow, subsurface flow via macropores and drains, bank erosion) and impact. In most agricultural landscapes the dominant loss is particulate P carried on eroded sediment: total P in sediment is enriched relative to the source soil (enrichment ratios 1.2–3) because fine, P-rich clay and organic particles are preferentially detached. Particulate P is only partly bioavailable, but it accumulates in sediments and can be released later. Dissolved reactive P (DRP, essentially orthophosphate) in surface runoff is the immediately algal-available fraction. Its concentration depends on soil-test P in the thin surface interaction layer, and the relationship is non-linear: Heckrath et al. [13] showed for the Broadbalk experiment at Rothamsted that P in drainage water was low and insensitive to Olsen P up to a change point around 60 mg kg⁻¹ and then rose steeply, because once sorption sites approach saturation (DPS ~25%) each additional increment of P raises EPC₀ sharply. Freshly applied fertiliser or manure on the surface before rain gives "incidental" losses that bypass the soil chemistry altogether.

Subsurface leaching was long considered negligible, but it matters in sandy soils with low Fe and Al, in organic (peat) soils, in soils with a long manure history, and where macropores and tile drains short-circuit the matrix; preferential flow carries colloid-bound P as well as DRP. Losses are small in absolute terms (0.1–2 kg P ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹) but ecologically large because lakes respond to tens of µg L⁻¹.

Critical source areas are the intersection of high-P soils with hydrologically active zones (saturated areas near streams, compacted tracks); a small fraction of a catchment usually delivers most of the P. The P Index (Lemunyon and Gilbert 1993 and state versions) ranks fields by combining source factors (soil test P, fertiliser and manure rate, method and timing) with transport factors (erosion, runoff class, distance to water, connectivity) to target management. Soil tests: Olsen (0.5 M NaHCO₃, pH 8.5; works across pH, standard in Europe and calcareous soils), Bray-1 (dilute HCl + NH₄F; acid soils), Mehlich-3 (acid, multi-element; widespread in North America); agronomic optima are roughly Olsen 20–30 mg kg⁻¹ and Mehlich-3 30–50 mg kg⁻¹, and environmental thresholds lie not far above.

Eutrophication and legacy P

Schindler's whole-lake experiments at the Experimental Lakes Area (Lake 227, from 1969) established that P, not C or N, controls algal biomass in most freshwater lakes: a lake fertilised with C and N stayed clear, while P addition produced blooms; after 37 years, Schindler et al. [14] showed that cutting N while keeping P did not control blooms because N-fixing cyanobacteria filled the gap, so P control is necessary and often sufficient. Eutrophic lakes are typically above ~30–35 µg total P L⁻¹. Because P sorbs, decades of surplus applications have built legacy P in soils, stream sediments and lake beds: Sharpley et al. [15] and Jarvie et al. [16] document lag times of years to decades between reducing inputs and improved water quality, because legacy stores keep releasing P (desorption as EPC₀ remains high, internal loading under anoxia, remobilisation of sediment). Management must therefore combine source reduction (drawing down soil P by harvest removal without fertiliser, which takes ~10 years to drop Olsen P by 20 mg kg⁻¹), transport interruption (buffers, cover, drainage control) and realistic expectations.

Global P flows, use efficiency and "peak phosphorus"

Mined phosphate rock supplies ~20 Tg P yr⁻¹ (14–20 Tg as fertiliser), two thirds of it from Morocco, China, the United States and Russia; USGS reserves are ~70 Gt of rock, with Morocco holding most. Cordell, Drangert and White [17] argued that production could peak around 2030 ("peak phosphorus") and that P security deserves the attention given to energy; subsequent reserve re-estimates pushed the peak out by a century or more, but the geopolitical concentration and the lack of substitutes remain. MacDonald et al. [18] mapped agronomic P balances at 0.5°: 71% of cropland had a surplus (Western Europe, China, parts of Brazil), while 29% (sub-Saharan Africa, parts of South America and Eastern Europe) ran deficits, mining soil P. Sattari et al. [19] showed with a residual-P model that legacy soil P can supply a large share of future crop demand in surplus regions, cutting required fertiliser substantially. Use efficiency along the chain (mine to fork) is only ~20%; the main leaks are mining losses, erosion and runoff from fields, and the one-way flow of P in food and manure to cities and the sea.

P limitation of tropical forests and the CO₂ fertilisation effect

Lowland tropical forests on old Oxisols and Ultisols have most of their P occluded or organic, extremely low available P, high phosphatase activity, high N:P and high P resorption: all signatures of P limitation, though direct fertilisation evidence is mixed (growth and litterfall respond in some experiments, not others, and co-limitation by N and base cations is common). The question matters globally because the tropics hold the largest potential CO₂ fertilisation response. Fleischer et al. [20] ran 14 vegetation models for the AmazonFACE site: models without a P cycle predicted ~46% biomass gain under +200 ppm CO₂, whereas models with P limitation predicted about half that, and the spread among P-models reflected different assumptions about plant P flexibility, phosphatase up-regulation and increased P uptake by roots and mycorrhizae under elevated CO₂, i.e. exactly the acquisition strategies above. Terrer et al. (2019) likewise found that the CO₂ effect on biomass declines with decreasing soil P in ectomycorrhizal systems.

Connections to ecosystem models

Century P submodel. Parton et al. (1988) gave Century parallel P pools for each organic C pool with flexible C:P bounds, plus a mineral P chain: labile (solution + sorbed) P in rapid equilibrium with a secondary (sorbed) pool, which slowly transfers to an occluded pool, and a parent-material pool that weathers at a fixed rate. Organic P mineralisation follows the McGill–Cole logic, with a separate biochemical mineralisation flux depending on labile P. This structure, labile ⇌ secondary → occluded ← parent, is the template for most later models.

CASA-CNP (Wang, Law and Pak [21]) added N and P to CASA for global simulations with fixed plant C:P, Hedley-based initialisation and a Langmuir-type labile–sorbed equilibrium; it found P limitation of NPP in tropical and old-soil regions and reduced global NPP by ~25% relative to C-only runs. JSBACH-CNP (Goll et al. [22]) and ELM/E3SM (Yang et al. [23]) followed; Yang et al. represent sorption dynamically with Langmuir kinetics and map pools to Hedley fractions, using the global Hedley dataset of Yang and Post (2011) and a soil-order-based Qmax and K. ORCHIDEE-CNP (Goll et al. 2017) includes weathering as a function of lithology and runoff, flexible plant stoichiometry and phosphatase-driven mineralisation. QUINCY carries P with explicit microbial demand. The crop model QUEFTS (Janssen et al. 1990) takes the opposite, empirical approach: it estimates potential N, P and K supply from soil tests (Olsen P, organic C, pH) and computes yield from the most limiting pair of nutrients with substitution, and is the backbone of fertiliser recommendation in many tropical systems. Reed, Yang and Thornton [24] reviewed the state of P in models and listed the uncertain processes: weathering rates, sorption parameters by soil type, biochemical mineralisation and phosphatase response, plant P flexibility and mycorrhizal uptake, and dust inputs. Where P models differ most is how much extra P plants can acquire when CO₂ rises, which is the Fleischer et al. result in another form.

Exam-style questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer.

1. Contrast the N and P cycles in terms of source, loss pathways, and the change in limitation with soil age. Why does this contrast matter for ecosystem models?

Source: N enters from an effectively infinite atmospheric pool through biological fixation (and now Haber–Bosch and deposition), so inputs respond to biology; P enters by weathering of apatite in parent material plus dust, so inputs are set by geology and decline as the mineral is exhausted. Losses: N has gaseous pathways (denitrification, volatilisation, fire) in addition to leaching of the mobile nitrate anion; P has no gas phase, is retained by sorption and precipitation, and leaves mainly on sediment or in small dissolved amounts. Soil age: N accumulates from zero (young soils N-limited); P declines and is progressively occluded (old soils P-limited), the Walker–Syers trajectory confirmed on the Hawaiian chronosequence. Consequences for models: N cycles need fixation, denitrification and leaching formulations and respond to climate on annual timescales; P cycles need weathering, sorption (Langmuir with soil-type parameters), occlusion and biochemical mineralisation, and their long-term state depends on soil order, so initialisation (Hedley data) dominates. N limitation is widespread in temperate and boreal zones, P limitation in the tropics; so CO₂ fertilisation projections depend on N in the north and P in the tropics (Fleischer et al. 2019).

2. Derive the Langmuir isotherm's buffer capacity and explain the meaning and use of EPC₀ and the degree of P saturation.

Langmuir: Q = QmaxKC/(1 + KC). Differentiating, dQ/dC = [QmaxK(1 + KC) − QmaxKC·K]/(1 + KC)² = QmaxK/(1 + KC)². At low C the buffer capacity approaches QmaxK (maximal), and it declines as sites fill, so each added unit of P raises solution concentration more as the soil saturates. EPC₀ is the solution P concentration at which net sorption is zero for the soil's existing sorbed P; inverting the isotherm, C = Q₀/[K(Qmax − Q₀)]. If runoff or lake water has a concentration below EPC₀, the soil or sediment releases P (acts as a source); above it, sorbs (sink). It is measured by equilibrating soil with a series of P solutions and finding the zero-crossing. DPS = Pox/[α(Feox + Alox)] × 100 with α ≈ 0.5 expresses how much of the sorption maximum (proportional to amorphous Fe and Al) is occupied; above ~25% EPC₀ and dissolved P losses rise sharply, so DPS is an environmental risk index that is transferable across soils because it normalises for capacity, unlike a bare soil-test value.

3. Explain, with the McGill and Cole (1981) framework, why organic P mineralisation can be decoupled from carbon mineralisation and what this implies for stoichiometric models.

McGill and Cole distinguished elements bonded directly to carbon (C–N, C–S in amino acids and amino sulphur) from those attached through ester linkages (C–O–P in phosphate esters, C–O–S in sulphate esters). C-bonded elements are released only when the carbon skeleton is oxidised: mineralisation is "biological", a by-product of microbial use of the substrate for energy, and so follows C flow and the C:N threshold logic. Ester-bonded P can be cleaved by extracellular phosphatases without degrading the carbon backbone: mineralisation is "biochemical", driven by demand for P, with phosphatase synthesis induced by low phosphate and repressed by high phosphate. Therefore P mineralisation responds to P scarcity rather than to C supply, and organic P can be mined from otherwise stable organic matter. Implications: models cannot compute P release purely from C decomposition with fixed C:P; they need a separate biochemical mineralisation term that depends on labile P and on microbial or plant P demand (Century, ORCHIDEE-CNP, ELM include such a term). It also predicts that under elevated CO₂ plants may increase phosphatase investment, which is one of the processes separating P-models in Fleischer et al. (2019), and that ecoenzymatic ratios (phosphatase relative to glucosidase) indicate P stress.

4. Why is dissolved P in runoff a non-linear function of soil test P, and why does P loss persist for decades after fertiliser inputs are cut?

Dissolved reactive P in runoff equilibrates with the thin surface soil layer. At low sorbed P the soil has abundant high-affinity sites and strong buffer capacity (dQ/dC ≈ QmaxK), so EPC₀ and runoff DRP are low and change little as soil-test P rises. As sorbed P approaches a substantial fraction of Qmax (DPS above ~25%), remaining sites are weak and buffer capacity falls as 1/(1 + KC)², so EPC₀ rises steeply with each increment: the change-point or two-slope relationship of Heckrath et al. (1995) at Olsen P ≈ 60 mg kg⁻¹ for Broadbalk. Persistence: decades of surplus applications have stored legacy P in soils (desorbing slowly as EPC₀ remains above stream concentrations), in stream and ditch sediments (acting as sources when water P falls below their EPC₀), and in lake sediments (internal loading by reductive dissolution of Fe-P under anoxia). Drawing down soil-test P by crop removal without fertiliser is slow (roughly 10 years per 20 mg kg⁻¹ Olsen P), and sediment stores can supply lakes for decades (Sharpley et al. 2013; Jarvie et al. 2013). Hence lag times and the need to combine source and transport management.

5. Describe the Hedley fractionation and how Cross and Schlesinger used it to test Walker and Syers. What are its limitations?

Sequential extraction of a soil sample: anion-exchange resin (freely exchangeable Pi), 0.5 M NaHCO₃ (labile Pi and Po, including microbial P after fumigation), 0.1 M NaOH (Fe/Al-oxide sorbed Pi and humic Po), 1 M HCl (Ca-bound primary P), and residual digest (occluded and recalcitrant P). Cross and Schlesinger (1995) compiled 88 soils of different weathering status and found that with increasing weathering the HCl (primary) fraction declined, NaOH-Pi and residual P increased, and organic P peaked in intermediate soils; they also found that labile fractions correlated with organic P in unfertilised soils, supporting biological control of availability in older soils. This matches Walker and Syers' predicted sequence of primary → labile/organic → occluded. Limitations: fractions are operationally defined and overlap chemically; extractions can hydrolyse organic P (NaOH) or redistribute P among phases; NaOH-Po is heterogeneous; microbial P must be handled separately; the method is laborious and results vary with soil:solution ratio and shaking time. It is best used comparatively and to initialise model pools (ELM, CASA-CNP) rather than as a mechanistic measure of availability.

6. List the strategies plants use to acquire P from P-poor soils, and relate them to the soil chemistry that makes P unavailable.

The constraint is that phosphate is immobile (diffusion-limited depletion zones of 1–2 mm), sorbed on Fe/Al oxides (acid soils) or precipitated with Ca (alkaline soils), and partly organic (phytate strongly sorbed). Strategies (Lambers et al. 2008; Hinsinger 2001): (i) increase soil volume explored: mycorrhizal hyphae (AM and ECM) extend beyond the depletion zone and have high P affinity; fine roots, root hairs and high specific root length; topsoil foraging. (ii) Chemically mobilise sorbed and precipitated P: exudation of carboxylates (citrate, malate, oxalate), especially in pulses from cluster roots of non-mycorrhizal Proteaceae and lupins, which displace phosphate from oxides by ligand exchange and complex Al, Fe and Ca; rhizosphere acidification or alkalinisation to move pH toward 6–7; reductant release. (iii) Mineralise organic P: secretion of acid phosphatases and phytases (also by rhizosphere microbes and mycorrhizae). (iv) Reduce demand: high P-use efficiency, photosynthate per unit P, replacement of phospholipids with sulpho- and galactolipids, long leaf lifespan, and ~65% P resorption before leaf fall. (v) Microbial partnerships beyond mycorrhizae: phosphate-solubilising bacteria and fungi. Trade-offs: carboxylate strategies cost up to 20% of photosynthate but work on the oldest soils where mycorrhizal P is simply absent.

7. How do land-surface models represent P sorption and why does it matter for projections of tropical carbon uptake?

Most follow the Century template: solution/labile P exchanges rapidly with a sorbed (secondary) pool, the sorbed pool slowly transfers to an occluded pool, and parent material weathers into labile P. CASA-CNP uses a Langmuir equilibrium between labile and sorbed P; ELM/E3SM (Yang et al. 2014) uses Langmuir kinetics with Qmax and K assigned by soil order from the Hedley dataset so that sorption capacity differs between Oxisols and Mollisols; ORCHIDEE-CNP links weathering to lithology and runoff. Organic P mineralisation includes a biochemical term dependent on labile P. Plant P demand follows tissue C:P (fixed or flexible), and uptake is limited by labile P and root or mycorrhizal capacity. It matters because in the tropics the labile pool is tiny and the sorbed/occluded pools large; whether plants can increase P supply under elevated CO₂ (through phosphatase up-regulation, more root and mycorrhizal uptake, desorption by exudates) decides the size of the CO₂ fertilisation effect. Fleischer et al. (2019) found that adding P limitation roughly halved the simulated AmazonFACE biomass response (from ~+46% to ~+23%), and that the spread among P models came mainly from those acquisition assumptions. Without a P cycle, Earth system models likely overestimate the tropical land sink.

8. Explain the redox control of P availability and its consequences in paddy soils and eutrophic lakes.

Phosphate in acid and neutral soils is largely sorbed on or co-precipitated with ferric oxides and hydroxides (goethite, ferrihydrite, strengite). When soils are flooded, O₂ is consumed, Eh falls below ~+100 to +200 mV, and iron-reducing bacteria use Fe(III) as an electron acceptor, dissolving the oxides to Fe²⁺ and releasing the associated phosphate into solution. Simultaneously pH converges toward 6.5–7 in flooded soils, improving availability. In paddy rice, this reductive release is why flooded rice often shows a smaller P response than upland crops on the same soil and why P fertiliser recommendations differ. On drainage, Fe²⁺ re-oxidises to fresh, highly sorptive ferrihydrite that recaptures P. In stratified eutrophic lakes, summer hypolimnetic anoxia drives the same reaction in sediments: Fe-bound P is released to the water column ("internal loading") and mixed into the epilimnion at turnover, sustaining blooms even after catchment inputs are reduced; sulphate reduction makes it worse by sequestering Fe as FeS, removing the P sink. Remedies include hypolimnetic oxygenation, alum or lanthanum-modified clay additions to bind P independently of redox, and, ultimately, reducing legacy P delivery.
