Topic 7 of the Biogeochemistry section

The Carbon Cycle

Where the carbon is, how fast it moves, what the Keeling curve and isotopes tell us, how the land and ocean sinks respond to CO₂ and warming, and how a leaf-level photosynthesis model ends up setting the size of the global land sink in an Earth system model.

The one-paragraph version

Carbon sits in four big reservoirs: the atmosphere (~880 Pg C at 420 ppm), the land biosphere (vegetation ~450–650 Pg, soils ~1500–2400 Pg to 1–2 m plus ~1300–1700 Pg in permafrost), the ocean (~38,000 Pg, mostly dissolved inorganic carbon), and the lithosphere (fossil fuels ~1000–2000 Pg extractable; carbonate and organic rocks ~10⁸ Pg). The fast cycle exchanges ~120 Pg C yr⁻¹ between atmosphere and land (GPP) and ~80 Pg each way with the ocean; these gross fluxes are nearly balanced, so the atmospheric trend is set by the small residual. Humans add ~10 Pg C yr⁻¹ from fossil fuels and ~1–1.5 Pg from land-use change; about 44% stays in the atmosphere (the airborne fraction), ~30% is taken up by land and ~26% by the ocean (Global Carbon Budget, Friedlingstein et al. [1]). Both sinks exist because CO₂ is higher than before (the carbon–concentration feedback, β) and both are weakened by warming (the carbon–climate feedback, γ). The size of a carbon stock is input times residence time (X = u τ), so projections depend on how photosynthesis responds to CO₂ (β) and how turnover responds to temperature (γ), and the land models that carry this in CMIP6 disagree by a factor of two or more. Because warming is close to proportional to cumulative emissions (TCRE ≈ 1.65 °C per 1000 Pg C), the whole problem can be expressed as a remaining carbon budget.

Examiner's lens

You will be asked to reproduce the global budget to within round numbers and to explain the concepts behind them: the GPP → NPP → NEP → NBP hierarchy, turnover time, β and γ, the airborne fraction, TCRE, and the matrix form of a pool model. The connecting question is always "how does a parameter you measure at the leaf or in a soil core change the global sink in a model?".

Pools and fluxes: the global carbon budget

The Global Carbon Project publishes an annual budget that balances fossil emissions (EFOS) and land-use change emissions (ELUC) against atmospheric growth (GATM), the ocean sink (SOCEAN) and the land sink (SLAND), with a budget imbalance that measures what we do not understand:

$$E_{FOS} + E_{LUC} = G_{ATM} + S_{OCEAN} + S_{LAND} + B_{IM}.$$

For 2013–2022 the decadal means were EFOS 9.6 ± 0.5, ELUC 1.3 ± 0.7, GATM 5.2 ± 0.02, SOCEAN 2.8 ± 0.4, SLAND 3.3 ± 0.8 Pg C yr⁻¹ (Friedlingstein et al. [1]). The atmospheric growth is measured directly (1 ppm = 2.124 Pg C), the fossil emissions from energy statistics, the ocean sink from observation-based products and models, the land sink from dynamic global vegetation models (the TRENDY ensemble, Sitch et al. [17]) checked against atmospheric inversions. ELUC and SLAND are the least certain. Gross fluxes dwarf these: GPP is 120–130 Pg C yr⁻¹ (Beer et al. [9] estimated 123 ± 8 from flux-tower upscaling), terrestrial NPP ~60, heterotrophic respiration ~60, fire ~2, and ocean–atmosphere exchange ~80 each way. The sinks are therefore small differences between large numbers, which is why inverting them from atmospheric data works and why bottom-up models struggle.

From GPP to NBP: the carbon-flux hierarchy

Chapin et al. [4] standardised the vocabulary. GPP is gross photosynthesis. Subtracting autotrophic respiration Ra (growth plus maintenance) gives NPP; the ratio NPP/GPP, the carbon-use efficiency, is 0.4–0.5 in most forests and lower in old or hot ones. Subtracting heterotrophic respiration Rh (decomposers, herbivores) gives NEP = NPP − Rh = GPP − Reco, the quantity an eddy-covariance tower measures as −NEE. Subtracting non-respiratory losses, fire, harvest, herbivore removal, erosion and leaching of DOC and DIC, volatile organic carbon, gives NBP (net biome productivity), the change in carbon storage of a region over years to decades; it is NBP, not NEP, that the global budget calls the land sink. The distinction matters because a forest with NEP of +2 t C ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ can have NBP near zero once disturbance is averaged over the landscape.

Eddy covariance measures NEE as the covariance of vertical wind speed and CO₂ concentration fluctuations plus a storage term, half-hourly, over a footprint of hundreds of metres. Partitioning NEE into GPP and Reco uses either the night-time method (Reichstein et al. [7]: fit a temperature response to night-time NEE, when GPP = 0, in short moving windows, extrapolate to daytime, GPP = Reco − NEE) or the daytime method (Lasslop et al. [8]: fit a light-response hyperbola with a VPD-dependent saturation and a temperature-dependent respiration term to daytime data). The two differ systematically, by up to 10% in GPP, because respiration under light may be lower than the night-time extrapolation (Kok effect) and because night-time data are filtered for low turbulence (u* threshold). FLUXNET syntheses of these partitioned fluxes are the basis of GPP upscaling (Beer et al. [9]) and of model benchmarking.

Turnover times and the matrix form of carbon models

At steady state a pool's size equals its input times its mean residence time, X = u τ, and τ = X/F for any pool with stock X and throughput F. Vegetation carbon (~550 Pg) with NPP of 60 gives τ ≈ 9–10 years; soil carbon (~1500–2400 Pg) with ~60 Pg input gives 25–40 years as a bulk average. But bulk averages hide enormous heterogeneity: fine roots and leaves turn over in months, wood in decades to centuries, and soil carbon ranges from days (microbial biomass) to millennia (mineral-associated, deep, permafrost). Carvalhais et al. [6] combined global biomass and soil maps with GPP to estimate whole-ecosystem turnover of 23 (+7, −4) years, ranging from ~15 years in the tropics to > 250 years in cold northern soils, with a temperature sensitivity across biomes weaker than most models assume; the models disagreed with the data mainly on turnover, not on GPP. This is the origin of the "models get the fluxes right for the wrong reasons" criticism.

Luo et al. [14, 15] showed that virtually all land carbon models, from Century to CLM to JULES, can be written in a common matrix form:

$$\frac{d\mathbf{X}}{dt} = \mathbf{B}\,u(t) - \mathbf{A}\,\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\,\mathbf{K}\,\mathbf{X}(t),$$

where X is the vector of pool sizes (leaves, wood, roots, litter, soil pools), u(t) is the carbon input (GPP or NPP), B the allocation vector, K a diagonal matrix of baseline turnover rates, ξ(t) a diagonal matrix of environmental scalars (temperature, moisture, texture, nutrient modifiers), and A the transfer matrix with −1 on the diagonal and the fractions transferred between pools below it (the rest is respired). Writing a model this way exposes its properties: the steady state is X* = (AξK)⁻¹Bu, i.e. input times a matrix of residence times; the transient response is a sum of exponentials with time constants from the eigenvalues of AξK; and the carbon storage at any time can be decomposed into carbon storage capacity (X*) and carbon storage potential (the disequilibrium). Xia et al. [16] built the traceability framework on this: differences in simulated carbon storage between models trace to differences in NPP, in baseline residence time (A, K, B), and in environmental scalars, which turns model intercomparison from "which is right" into "which component differs". The matrix form also allows semi-analytic spin-up, cutting the thousands of years of spin-up a soil model needs to reach steady state.

Fast and slow cycles, the ocean, and the atmospheric record

The slow cycle. Over 10⁵–10⁶ years CO₂ is removed by silicate weathering (CaSiO₃ + 2 CO₂ + H₂O → Ca²⁺ + 2 HCO₃⁻ + SiO₂), the bicarbonate is precipitated as marine carbonate, and CO₂ returns through volcanism and metamorphism; carbonate weathering is CO₂-neutral on these timescales. Because weathering accelerates with temperature and runoff, this forms a negative feedback that stabilises climate on million-year timescales but is far too slow to matter for anthropogenic CO₂, roughly 20–30% of which remains in the atmosphere after 1000 years.

The ocean. CO₂ dissolves and reacts: CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ ⇌ 2 H⁺ + CO₃²⁻; at seawater pH ~8.1 about 90% is bicarbonate, 9% carbonate and 1% dissolved CO₂. The Revelle factor (∂ln pCO₂/∂ln DIC ≈ 10) expresses that a 10% rise in pCO₂ raises DIC by only 1%, because added CO₂ consumes carbonate ion; it rises as the ocean acidifies, weakening the sink. The solubility pump (cold, dense water carrying CO₂ down at high latitudes) and the biological pump (sinking organic matter and calcium carbonate shells) keep deep-ocean DIC above surface values; the ocean sink's rate is limited by mixing of surface water into the deep ocean (centuries), not by chemistry.

The Keeling curve. Charles Keeling began continuous measurements at Mauna Loa in 1958 at 315 ppm (Keeling et al. [13]); the record passed 420 ppm in 2023 and now rises ~2.4 ppm yr⁻¹. The seasonal cycle, ~6–7 ppm peak-to-peak at Mauna Loa and ~15–16 ppm at Barrow, is the breathing of Northern Hemisphere vegetation: CO₂ falls from May to September as GPP exceeds respiration and rises through winter. Graven et al. [10] showed from aircraft data that the amplitude above 45° N increased by ~50% between 1958–61 and 2009–11, more than models reproduce, implying a large increase in the seasonal exchange of northern ecosystems (longer growing seasons, CO₂ fertilisation, agricultural intensification).

Isotopic and O₂ constraints. Fossil carbon is ¹⁴C-free and depleted in ¹³C (δ¹³C ≈ −28‰), so burning it lowers atmospheric δ¹³C (the Suess effect, from −6.5‰ pre-industrial to below −8.5‰ now) and diluted ¹⁴C before bomb testing doubled it in the 1960s; the subsequent decline of bomb ¹⁴C as it mixed into the ocean and biosphere is a tracer of exchange timescales and of soil carbon turnover. Fossil combustion consumes O₂ at a stoichiometric ratio of ~1.4 O₂ per CO₂, while ocean uptake of CO₂ does not consume O₂ and land uptake releases it (ratio ~1.1); Keeling's atmospheric O₂/N₂ record therefore separates the land and ocean sinks independently of models, confirming each at ~2–3 Pg C yr⁻¹. Together with the fact that the ¹³C signature of the land sink differs from the ocean's (photosynthetic discrimination of ~18‰ vs ~2‰), these constrain the global budget partition.

Feedbacks, TCRE and the remaining budget

Friedlingstein et al. [5] (C4MIP) defined the two feedback parameters that organise the problem. The carbon–concentration feedback β (Pg C per ppm) is the change in land (or ocean) carbon storage per unit rise in CO₂ at fixed climate: CO₂ fertilisation of photosynthesis, water-use-efficiency gains, and for the ocean simple solubility. The carbon–climate feedback γ (Pg C per °C) is the change in storage per degree of warming at fixed CO₂: for land, faster decomposition and respiration, drought, fire and permafrost thaw against longer growing seasons; for the ocean, lower solubility and stronger stratification. β is positive and γ negative in every model. C4MIP (2006) gave land β 0.2–2.8 (mean 1.4) and γ −20 to −177 (mean −79); Arora et al. [2] for CMIP6 found land β 0.97 ± 0.40 Pg C ppm⁻¹ and γL −45 ± 50 Pg C °C⁻¹, ocean β 0.91 ± 0.09 and γ −17 ± 5: the spread is dominated by the land, and within land by the representation of nutrient limitation (models with N cycles have lower β) and of soil carbon turnover (γ). The feedbacks are defined by three runs: fully coupled, biogeochemically coupled only (BGC: the carbon cycle sees rising CO₂ but the climate does not), and radiatively coupled only (RAD); β = ΔCBGC/ΔCO₂ and γ = (ΔCCOU − ΔCBGC)/ΔT.

Other feedbacks and sinks: the permafrost carbon feedback (Schuur et al. [11]): ~1300–1700 Pg C in frozen soils, of which 5–15% (~130–160 Pg by 2100 under high emissions) is expected to be released gradually as CO₂ and some CH₄, a feedback outside most CMIP6 models; fire (~2 Pg C yr⁻¹, mostly re-sequestered, but net loss in deforestation and peat fires); the "missing sink" of the 1980s–1990s, the gap between emissions and atmospheric growth plus ocean uptake that was eventually attributed to the Northern Hemisphere land sink (forest regrowth, CO₂ and N fertilisation), identified through O₂/N₂ and ¹³C constraints; and the detection of CO₂ fertilisation itself: Walker et al. [12] reviewed evidence from FACE experiments, tree rings, inventories, satellite greening, atmospheric inversions and models and concluded that a CO₂-driven increase in GPP and biomass is detectable and probably accounts for a large share of the land sink, but that its persistence depends on nutrient and water constraints and on turnover.

TCRE and budgets. Matthews et al. [19] showed that global warming is nearly proportional to cumulative CO₂ emissions regardless of pathway, the transient climate response to cumulative emissions, because the declining airborne fraction (ocean uptake saturates slowly) and the declining radiative efficiency of CO₂ (logarithmic forcing) roughly cancel. AR6 gives TCRE = 1.65 °C per 1000 Pg C (likely 1.0–2.3), i.e. 0.45 °C per 1000 Gt CO₂. A warming target then becomes a budget: for 1.5 °C with 50% likelihood, AR6 estimated ~500 Gt CO₂ remaining from 2020, now below 200 Gt; for 2 °C (67%), ~1150 Gt from 2020. Budgets shrink with non-CO₂ forcing and with Earth-system feedbacks such as permafrost. Nature-based solutions (afforestation, soil carbon, restoration) can contribute 1–3 Pg C yr⁻¹ at most, with questions of permanence (fire, drought, land-use reversal), saturation (stocks approach a new steady state after decades; X = uτ), additionality and leakage, and the asymmetry that a tonne stored in biomass is at risk while a tonne of fossil carbon left underground is not.

Methane briefly. CH₄ is ~1930 ppb, rising again since 2007; sources ~575 Tg CH₄ yr⁻¹, with natural wetlands ~150–200 Tg (the largest single source and a climate feedback), agriculture (ruminants, rice), fossil fuels and waste; the sink is OH oxidation (lifetime ~9 yr). GWP₁₀₀ is 27–30 (AR6), GWP₂₀ ~80. Wetland CH₄ emission is modelled from NPP or soil carbon, water-table depth, temperature and transport pathway (diffusion, ebullition, aerenchyma), and is a key reason that peatland and permafrost carbon matter beyond their CO₂.

Connections to ecosystem models

From leaf to globe. The CMIP land components (CLM5 in CESM2, JULES in UKESM1, ORCHIDEE in IPSL-CM6, JSBACH in MPI-ESM, LPJ-GUESS and others in TRENDY) all begin with FvCB photosynthesis and a stomatal conductance model at the leaf (the C3 page), scaled through the canopy by sun/shade or multi-layer schemes with LAI and a vertical profile of Vcmax that follows leaf N. GPP is then split by allocation rules into leaves, wood and roots (fixed fractions, allometric, or resource-dependent), tissues turn over at prescribed rates into litter and soil pools (Century-type cascades in CLM and ORCHIDEE; RothC in JULES; microbial-explicit options such as MIMICS exist), and disturbance (fire models such as SPITFIRE or Li et al., harvest, land-use transitions) removes carbon. The global sink that comes out is therefore a product of: Vcmax and its response to CO₂ and temperature (sets β of GPP), the CO₂ response of stomatal conductance (water-use efficiency), the fraction of GPP respired (CUE), the allocation to long-lived wood versus short-lived leaves and roots (vegetation τ), the soil turnover and its Q₁₀ and moisture functions (soil τ and γ), nutrient limitation (lowers β), and disturbance (NBP vs NEP). Each of these appears on another page of this site.

Benchmarking. ILAMB (Collier et al. [18]) scores models against dozens of datasets, including biomass, soil carbon, GPP from FLUXNET upscaling, burned area, the seasonal cycle of CO₂ at monitoring stations, and functional relationships (e.g. GPP versus precipitation), producing the familiar coloured scorecard. TRENDY (Sitch et al. [17]) runs ~15–20 DGVMs with common forcing in factorial experiments (CO₂ only; CO₂ + climate; + land use) to attribute the historical land sink and to supply the land-sink term and its uncertainty to the Global Carbon Budget; the ensemble mean matches inversions, but the spread among models (±1 Pg C yr⁻¹) and the disagreement on regional attribution persist. In CMIP6 the land carbon uptake projected for 2100 under SSP5-8.5 ranges over several hundred Pg C across models, most of it traceable to β and γ differences and hence to nutrient cycling and turnover.

Matrix models and traceability. Luo et al. [14, 15] re-wrote CLM, CABLE, ORCHIDEE and others in matrix form, which makes semi-analytic spin-up fast, allows storage to be decomposed into NPP × residence time × environmental modifiers, and makes data assimilation of pool sizes tractable. The practical payoff for an exam answer is simple: if two models disagree on soil carbon, ask whether it is the input (NPP), the baseline residence time (K, A, B) or the environmental scalar (ξ) that differs, and which observation (Carvalhais turnover, ¹⁴C, soil carbon maps) could tell.

Exam-style questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer.

1. Write down the global carbon budget equation with current values and explain how each term is estimated and which are least certain.

EFOS + ELUC = GATM + SOCEAN + SLAND + BIM. For 2013–2022: 9.6 + 1.3 = 5.2 + 2.8 + 3.3 (+ imbalance −0.4) Pg C yr⁻¹. EFOS comes from energy statistics and cement production (±5%). ELUC from bookkeeping models combining land-cover change data with carbon densities and decay curves (±0.7, the most uncertain emission). GATM from the global network of CO₂ stations, converted at 2.124 Pg C per ppm (very precise). SOCEAN from global ocean biogeochemistry models and observation-based pCO₂ products (SOCAT), ±0.4. SLAND from the TRENDY DGVM ensemble (CO₂ + climate runs minus the land-use effect), checked with atmospheric inversions and O₂/N₂ and ¹³C constraints (±0.8, the most uncertain sink). The budget imbalance (~0.3–0.4) shows the residual mismatch; in individual years it reflects ENSO-driven variability of the land sink that the models capture imperfectly. Over 1850–2022, cumulative emissions ≈ 700 Pg C (fossil ~480, LUC ~210), of which ~40% remains in the atmosphere.

2. Define GPP, NPP, NEP and NBP, and explain why a tower that measures NEP of +3 t C ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ may not imply a regional sink of that size.

GPP: gross photosynthesis. NPP = GPP − Ra (autotrophic respiration), the biomass produced. NEP = NPP − Rh = GPP − Reco, the net ecosystem exchange of CO₂ with the atmosphere by metabolism, measured by eddy covariance as −NEE. NBP = NEP minus non-respiratory losses: fire, harvest and removal, herbivory export, erosion, leaching of DOC/DIC, VOC emission; it equals the change in regional carbon storage over years to decades. A tower sees one footprint at one successional stage, typically a maturing stand chosen for fetch, where NEP is positive; the region contains recently disturbed stands (sources), harvested areas and fires, and lateral losses. Averaged over a landscape with a disturbance return interval, NBP is the long-term mean of NEP minus disturbance fluxes and is much smaller, near zero in steady-state mosaics. In addition, towers lose data at night under low turbulence (u* filtering) and partition fluxes with assumptions (night-time vs day-time methods) that bias annual sums by up to tens of g C m⁻². Scaling from NEP to NBP therefore needs inventories, disturbance records and bookkeeping, which is why the global land sink is reported as NBP from models and inversions, not from towers.

3. Derive the steady-state carbon stock of a two-pool vegetation–soil model and the time constants of its response to a step change in NPP. What does this imply about where model uncertainty lies?

Let dV/dt = u − V/τv and dS/dt = V/τv − S/τs. At steady state V* = uτv and S* = (V*/τv)τs = uτs, so total C* = u(τv + τs): stock equals input times residence time. For a step u → u(1 + δ) at t = 0, the vegetation deviation obeys v' = uδ − v/τv, giving v(t) = uδτv(1 − e−t/τv); the soil receives v/τv and relaxes with τs, giving a two-exponential response with time constants τv and τs (for τv ≠ τs): s(t) = uδτs[1 − (τse−t/τs − τve−t/τv)/(τs − τv)]. In the matrix form dX/dt = Bu − AξKX these constants are the eigenvalues of AξK, and X* = (AξK)⁻¹Bu. Implications: the equilibrium gain from CO₂ fertilisation is proportional to total residence time, and the time to realise it is ~3τs; thus models with long soil turnover store more carbon but more slowly; and uncertainty in projected storage is dominated by τ (and its climate sensitivity, γ) rather than by NPP, which models agree on better. Carvalhais et al. (2014) found exactly this: models reproduced GPP but disagreed with observed turnover and its temperature dependence.

4. Explain the carbon–concentration (β) and carbon–climate (γ) feedback parameters, how they are diagnosed from model experiments, and why their land values vary so much between models.

β (Pg C ppm⁻¹) is the sensitivity of land or ocean carbon storage to atmospheric CO₂ with climate held fixed: on land, CO₂ fertilisation of photosynthesis and improved water-use efficiency; in the ocean, solubility and carbonate chemistry. γ (Pg C °C⁻¹) is the sensitivity of storage to global temperature at fixed CO₂: on land, faster decomposition and autotrophic respiration, drought and fire, and permafrost thaw, partly offset by longer growing seasons at high latitudes; in the ocean, reduced solubility and stratification. Diagnosis (C4MIP/CMIP6 1% yr⁻¹ CO₂ experiments): a fully coupled run (COU), a biogeochemically coupled run in which only the carbon cycle sees rising CO₂ (BGC), and a radiatively coupled run in which only the radiation code sees it (RAD). β = ΔCBGC/ΔCO₂; γ = (ΔCCOU − ΔCBGC)/ΔT (or ΔCRAD/ΔT; the two differ by nonlinearity). Arora et al. (2020): land β 0.97 ± 0.40, γ −45 ± 50; ocean 0.91 ± 0.09, −17 ± 5. The land spread arises because (i) models with coupled N (and P) cycles have smaller β, since nutrient limitation caps the CO₂ response; (ii) turnover formulations and their Q₁₀ and moisture functions set γ, and soil carbon stocks differ by a factor of two; (iii) treatment of fire, permafrost and vegetation dynamics differs; (iv) allocation and CUE differ. Because β and γ act on the same stocks, a model with large soil carbon tends to have both large β and large |γ|.

5. Why is the airborne fraction roughly constant at ~0.44 even though emissions have grown? What would make it change?

If sinks scale with the CO₂ excess above pre-industrial (S ≈ k(C − C₀)) and emissions grow exponentially, then for an exponentially growing forcing the atmospheric excess also grows exponentially at the same rate and the ratio of growth to emissions is constant: the sinks keep pace because they are driven by the same excess that the emissions create. Formally, with dC/dt = E − k(C − C₀) and E = E₀ert, the particular solution has C − C₀ ∝ ert and airborne fraction r/(r + k). Thus a constant AF is expected for exponential emissions growth with linear sinks, and does not imply that sinks are not saturating. AF would rise if emissions growth accelerated (sinks lag), if sinks weakened relative to the excess (ocean Revelle factor increasing, warming-driven γ, nutrient limitation of β, land-use loss of sink capacity), or if emissions fell to zero (AF is then undefined, and the sinks draw CO₂ down). Detecting a trend in AF is hard because interannual variability (ENSO, volcanic eruptions) is large relative to the signal; studies find no robust trend over 1959–2020 after accounting for LUC uncertainty.

6. What is TCRE, why is warming proportional to cumulative emissions, and how does this translate into a remaining carbon budget?

TCRE is the transient climate response to cumulative CO₂ emissions: the global mean warming per unit of cumulative CO₂ emitted, ~1.65 °C per 1000 Pg C (AR6 likely range 1.0–2.3), equivalently 0.45 °C per 1000 Gt CO₂. Matthews et al. (2009) and Allen et al. (2009) found it nearly independent of emission pathway and timing. The proportionality arises from two cancelling nonlinearities: as cumulative emissions rise, the airborne fraction tends to increase (ocean uptake per unit emission falls because the carbonate buffer saturates and mixing is slow), which would steepen warming per tonne, while the radiative forcing per ppm falls logarithmically with concentration, which would flatten it; in addition the ocean heat uptake that delays warming declines over time. The remaining budget for a target T is (T − Talready)/TCRE minus adjustments for non-CO₂ forcing (methane, aerosols), Earth-system feedbacks not in the models (permafrost), and the chosen probability; AR6 gave ~500 Gt CO₂ for a 50% chance of 1.5 °C from 2020, which at ~40 Gt CO₂ yr⁻¹ is consumed in roughly a decade, and ~1150 Gt for a 67% chance of 2 °C. TCRE also implies that net-zero CO₂ emissions roughly stabilise temperature (zero emissions commitment near zero), so that every tonne matters equally whenever it is emitted.

7. Describe the chain of processes and parameters through which a land model converts leaf-level photosynthesis into a global land sink, and identify three places where the uncertainty is largest.

Leaf: FvCB photosynthesis with Vcmax (from PFT tables, leaf N via LUNA, or optimality), Jmax, temperature functions and acclimation, coupled to a stomatal model (Ball–Berry or Medlyn) which sets Ci and transpiration. Canopy: integration over LAI with sun/shade or multi-layer radiative transfer and a Vcmax profile; LAI itself is prognostic from leaf carbon and specific leaf area, and phenology sets its timing. Plant: GPP minus maintenance and growth respiration (CUE) gives NPP, allocated to leaves, wood and roots by fixed or dynamic rules; nutrient limitation may down-regulate growth or impose costs (FUN). Turnover: tissue lifespans move carbon to litter; litter and soil cascades (Century-type or RothC, or microbial-explicit) with temperature and moisture scalars release CO₂; vertical soil discretisation and permafrost matter at high latitudes. Disturbance: fire, mortality, harvest, land-use transitions, vegetation dynamics. Globally, NBP summed over grid cells is the land sink, and in a coupled ESM the resulting CO₂ feeds back on climate and on the photosynthesis at the start of the chain. Largest uncertainties: (1) the CO₂ response of GPP and its nutrient constraint (β), including Vcmax acclimation and down-regulation; (2) soil and vegetation turnover and their climate sensitivity (γ), including soil carbon stocks that differ twofold among models and the permafrost feedback; (3) disturbance and land use (fire, mortality, LUC emissions), which convert NEP to NBP and are the least constrained flux in the budget. Benchmarking (ILAMB) and traceability analysis locate which link differs between models.

8. What do the ¹³C, ¹⁴C and O₂/N₂ records each tell us about the carbon cycle that CO₂ concentration alone cannot?

CO₂ concentration gives the net atmospheric budget but cannot separate sources and sinks. ¹³C: fossil fuels and biomass are depleted (δ¹³C ≈ −28‰ for C3-derived carbon) relative to the atmosphere (−6.5‰ pre-industrial), so fossil emissions drive the atmosphere lighter (the Suess effect, now below −8.5‰); because exchange with the land involves strong photosynthetic discrimination (~18‰) while ocean exchange involves little (~2‰), the rate of ¹³C decline relative to CO₂ rise partitions the uptake between land and ocean ("double deconvolution"). ¹⁴C: fossil carbon is ¹⁴C-free, so fossil CO₂ dilutes atmospheric Δ¹⁴C (the Suess effect in ¹⁴C), which is used to quantify the fossil fraction of CO₂ regionally; the 1960s bomb spike, which doubled atmospheric ¹⁴C, then decayed as it mixed into the ocean and biosphere, provides exchange timescales (the e-folding of ~16 years) and, in soils and plants, a clock for carbon turnover and the age of respired CO₂ that directly tests model τ. O₂/N₂: fossil combustion consumes O₂ (~1.4 mol O₂ per mol CO₂); ocean CO₂ uptake does not change O₂ (apart from small thermal outgassing), while land uptake releases O₂ (~1.1 per CO₂); the measured decline in O₂/N₂ against the CO₂ rise therefore yields independent estimates of the land and ocean sinks (each ~2–3 Pg C yr⁻¹), the key evidence that resolved the "missing sink". Together these constrain the partition in the global budget and identify the northern land sink.
