Topic 2 of the Biogeochemistry section

Microbial-Explicit Models

What changes when the decomposer is a state variable: Michaelis–Menten kinetics, enzymes, carbon-use efficiency, necromass, and the family of models (AWB, MIMICS, CORPSE, MEND, DAMM, Millennial) that replaced fixed rate constants with microbial physiology, together with the instabilities and identifiability problems that came with them.

The one-paragraph version

First-order models treat decomposition as a property of the substrate: dC/dt = −kC, with k modified by climate. Microbial-explicit models treat it as a property of the decomposer: the rate depends on microbial biomass B (or the enzymes it produces), typically through Michaelis–Menten kinetics, Vmax·B·C/(Km + C), and the biomass itself grows on the carbon it takes up with a carbon-use efficiency (CUE) of 0.2–0.6 and dies at a rate that returns necromass to the soil. Three consequences follow that first-order models cannot produce: fresh inputs stimulate biomass and therefore the decomposition of old carbon (priming); the steady-state stock depends on physiology (CUE, turnover) as much as on inputs; and the response to warming depends on whether CUE and biomass acclimate. Because 30–60% of mineral-associated carbon is microbial residue, these models also connect to the mineral-stabilisation story of the next page. The costs are real: the simplest versions oscillate after perturbation, parameters are poorly identifiable from the data normally available, and the extra process detail widens rather than narrows projections of soil carbon. The field's answer has been models of intermediate complexity (MIMICS, Millennial) with measurable pools and traits.

Examiner's lens

Expect four moves: (i) write the kinetic forms (first order, forward MM, reverse MM) and say in which limit each applies; (ii) define CUE, give its range, and explain both the temperature debate and the stoichiometric threshold; (iii) draw a 3–4 pool microbial model (AWB or MIMICS) and explain priming, necromass and the oscillation problem from its structure; (iv) say what these models changed in warming projections and why parameter identifiability limits them.

What first-order models leave out

In Century or RothC the decomposer is implicit: the "active" or "BIO" pool is a carbon reservoir, not an agent, and decomposition of each pool proceeds at kiCi regardless of how much microbial biomass exists or what it is doing. Three observations contradict this. Priming: adding labile carbon (root exudates, glucose, fresh litter) increases the mineralisation of old SOM by 10–100% for days to months, and the magnitude scales with microbial growth, not with the amount of old carbon (Kuzyakov 2010); independent first-order pools cannot interact by construction. Biomass feedback: in long-term incubations without inputs, respiration declines faster than substrate because the microbial population shrinks; in fertilised or warmed soils, respiration often returns toward control values within years (thermal "acclimation") because biomass, community composition or CUE adjust. First-order models can only mimic this with ad hoc rate changes. Physiological efficiency: how much of a unit of decomposed carbon ends up as new SOM rather than CO₂ is set by microbial CUE and by how necromass is stabilised, not by the fixed "respired fraction" of a pool. The response, beginning with Schimel & Weintraub (2003) [1] and Allison, Wallenstein & Bradford (2010) [2], was to make the microbial biomass a state variable and let it control decomposition.

Microbial biomass as a pool

Microbial biomass carbon (MBC) is 0.5–5% of SOC (typically 1–3% in mineral topsoils; 200–2000 µg C g⁻¹ soil), with a C:N of 6–10. It is measured by chloroform fumigation–extraction (the flush of extractable C after lysing cells, divided by a recovery factor kEC ≈ 0.45), by phospholipid fatty acids (PLFA, which also fingerprint broad groups and turn over within days of cell death so they index living biomass), and by qPCR of 16S/ITS gene copies (abundance of bacteria and fungi, though copy number per cell varies). Biomass turns over in weeks to a year (0.5–3 yr⁻¹ in most models, faster for bacteria than fungi); because it is small and fast, it functions as a catalyst and a conduit, not a store. Its ratio to SOC (the microbial quotient, qmic) and the respiration per unit biomass (metabolic quotient, qCO₂) are the classic indices of substrate availability and stress.

Decomposition kinetics: first order, Michaelis–Menten and its reverse

Think of decomposition as an enzyme-catalysed reaction between a decomposer (biomass B or enzymes E) and a substrate C. Three forms are in use:

$$\text{first order:}\quad \frac{dC}{dt} = -k\,C \qquad\qquad \text{forward MM:}\quad \frac{dC}{dt} = -V_{max}\,B\,\frac{C}{K_m + C}$$
$$\text{reverse MM:}\quad \frac{dC}{dt} = -V_{max}\,C\,\frac{B}{K_B + B}$$

Forward Michaelis–Menten (Schimel & Weintraub 2003; AWB) treats the microbe or its enzymes as the catalyst and the substrate as the thing that saturates: at C ≫ Km the rate is VmaxB, independent of C, and proportional to biomass; at C ≪ Km the rate is (Vmax/Km)·B·C, bilinear. The reverse form (Schimel & Weintraub's alternative; used in MEND and in Wieder's MIMICS variants) treats the substrate as abundant and the decomposers as the saturating agent: more biomass yields diminishing returns because enzymes compete for a limited number of accessible binding sites. The two give opposite answers to "what happens if biomass doubles": forward MM doubles decomposition, reverse MM barely changes it when B ≫ KB. Tang & Riley (2013, 2015) [20] derived a unifying equilibrium chemistry approximation (ECA) that reduces to forward MM when enzymes are scarce and to reverse MM when substrates are scarce, and that handles many substrates competing for many enzymes (and minerals competing with enzymes for substrate); in their land-model implementation it reproduced the acclimation and priming behaviours without the instability of pure forward MM. In the limit where B is constant, forward MM with C ≪ Km collapses to first order with k = VmaxB/Km, which is why first-order models work in steady, undisturbed soils and fail during transitions.

Enzymes, carbon-use efficiency and stoichiometry

Exo-enzymes and the AWB model

Most SOM is polymeric and must be depolymerised outside the cell before uptake, so the real catalyst is the pool of extracellular enzymes. Allison (2005) argued that enzyme production is a costly investment that microbes make in proportion to biomass and expected return, and that enzymes persist for days to months (longer when sorbed to minerals) before being degraded. The AWB model (Allison, Wallenstein & Bradford 2010 [2]) has four pools: SOC, dissolved organic carbon (DOC), microbial biomass (MIC) and enzymes (ENZ). Enzymes depolymerise SOC into DOC by forward Michaelis–Menten kinetics (Vmax·ENZ·SOC/(Km + SOC)); microbes take up DOC by a second MM term; a fraction CUE of uptake becomes biomass and the rest is respired; biomass dies at a constant rate, returning to SOC and DOC, and produces enzymes at a constant fraction of biomass; enzymes decay to DOC. Temperature acts on Vmax (Arrhenius, Ea ≈ 47 kJ mol⁻¹), on Km (increasing linearly with T, as for many enzymes) and on CUE (declining linearly, −0.016 °C⁻¹ in the original). The headline result was that if CUE declines with warming, biomass and enzymes shrink and SOC losses are much smaller than a Q10 model predicts; if CUE is constant, warming stimulates biomass and losses are large. The structure, not the parameter values, generates this: decomposition is proportional to the decomposer, so anything that limits the decomposer limits the loss.

Carbon-use efficiency

CUE is the fraction of carbon taken up (or assimilated) that becomes microbial biomass:

$$\text{CUE} = \frac{\text{growth}}{\text{growth} + \text{respiration}} = \frac{\text{growth}}{\text{uptake}}$$
$$R_h = (1-\text{CUE})\,U, \qquad \text{necromass production} = \text{CUE}\,U - \frac{dB}{dt}$$

Typical values are 0.2–0.6 (Manzoni et al. 2012 [11]); the thermodynamic maximum for aerobic growth on sugars is about 0.6–0.8 because biosynthesis itself requires ATP generated by respiring part of the substrate. CUE is measured by ¹³C-labelled substrate incorporation into biomass versus ¹³CO₂ (substrate-specific and biased toward labile compounds), by ¹⁸O-water incorporation into DNA (growth-based, substrate-independent; Spohn, Geyer et al. 2016 [15] discuss the definitions at cell, population and ecosystem scales and why they differ), and by calorespirometry. Three controls matter for models.

Temperature. Allison et al. (2010) assumed CUE declines with temperature (maintenance respiration rises faster than growth), and experiments with glucose supported a decline of ~0.01–0.02 per °C. Frey et al. (2013) [14] measured CUE in a 20-year warming experiment and found the decline for glucose but no decline for phenol and a smaller one for complex substrates, with evidence of acclimation of the warmed community. Sinsabaugh et al. (2013) [13] argued from meta-analysis that CUE converges on ~0.3 across ecosystems and is controlled by stoichiometry more than by temperature. The practical consequence: whether warming reduces CUE (buffering soil C loss) or not (amplifying it) is a model switch with a large effect, and the data support both answers depending on substrate and acclimation time.

Stoichiometry. Microbes have a narrow C:N (≈ 7–9) and must balance carbon and nitrogen intake. If substrate C:N is above the threshold element ratio TER = (C:N)B/CUEmax (roughly 20–30 for N), there is more carbon than the microbes can use with the nitrogen available, so the excess is respired ("overflow") or not taken up, and CUE declines in proportion: CUE ≈ (C:N)B/(C:N)S × (1 + fraction of N obtained by immobilisation from inorganic pools). Below TER, nitrogen is in surplus, CUE is at its maximum and net N mineralisation occurs. This is the basis of the Manzoni (2012, 2017) and Sinsabaugh "ecoenzymatic stoichiometry" framework, and of the MIMICS-CN and Millennial N schemes; it produces the familiar rule that residues with C:N > 25–30 immobilise N and those below mineralise it.

Substrate quality and community. CUE is higher on labile, reduced substrates (sugars, amino acids: 0.5–0.7) than on aromatic or oxidised ones (lignin, phenols: 0.1–0.3); fungi tend to have higher CUE and slower turnover than bacteria; drought and salinity lower CUE by raising maintenance costs. Because necromass formation is CUE × uptake, these differences propagate to SOM stabilisation: Cotrufo et al. (2013) [18] made this the heart of the MEMS framework (Microbial Efficiency–Matrix Stabilisation): labile litter is used with high CUE, generates more necromass, and so contributes more to stable, mineral-associated SOM than recalcitrant litter does, which inverts the old view that lignin-rich litter builds humus.

Necromass and the microbial carbon pump

Dead microbial cells, cell-wall fragments, and extracellular products are collectively necromass. Amino-sugar biomarkers (glucosamine from fungal chitin, muramic acid from bacterial peptidoglycan) scaled by conversion factors suggest that necromass makes up 30–60% of SOC in temperate grassland and cropland topsoils and 20–40% in forests, most of it in the mineral-associated fraction (Liang, Schimel & Jastrow 2017 [16]; Liang et al. 2019 [17]). Liang's microbial carbon pump is the idea that microbes transform labile plant carbon into stable soil carbon through two routes: the in vivo turnover pathway (uptake → biomass → necromass → stabilisation on minerals, controlled by CUE and turnover) and the ex vivo modification pathway (extracellular enzymes partially transform plant compounds that are then sorbed without passing through a cell). The entombing effect (necromass is preserved by mineral association faster than it is decomposed) explains why soils with high clay and high CUE accumulate microbial carbon, and why the MEMS prediction holds: labile litter, high CUE, more necromass, more MAOM. The counter-argument is that necromass is also the most accessible SOM (C:N ≈ 8, soluble fragments), so its persistence depends entirely on the mineral matrix and not on anything microbial; the two views are reconciled on the MAOM page by treating stabilisation as sorption with finite capacity.

MIMICS deserves the most detail because it is the microbial model most often run in land-surface frameworks (CLM, E3SM tests, the TRENDY-style comparisons). Litter is split into metabolic and structural pools by lignin:N as in Century. Two microbial pools represent copiotrophs (r strategists: fast growth, high Vmax, low CUE on structural litter, fast turnover) and oligotrophs (K strategists: slow growth, high affinity, higher CUE on structural litter, slow turnover). Each decomposes each litter and the available SOM pool with forward MM kinetics (Vmax and Km temperature-dependent, scaled by litter quality and by clay for the SOM pools). Dead microbes are partitioned among the three SOM pools (physically protected, chemically recalcitrant, available) with fractions that depend on clay (protected) and litter chemistry (recalcitrant); the protected pool desorbs slowly back to the available pool, which is the only one microbes can consume. Wieder et al. (2015) showed MIMICS reproduces global litter-decomposition data and the spatial pattern of SOC as well as Century-like models while producing distinctly different responses to warming and to changes in inputs; its weakness, shared by all the family, is that the functional-group parameters are not directly measurable and were tuned.

Oscillations, stability and what the models do to projections

Making decomposition proportional to the decomposer creates a predator–prey structure: biomass grows on substrate, substrate is depleted by biomass. Wang et al. (2014) [19] showed analytically that models with forward MM kinetics and linear (first-order) microbial turnover have steady states with complex eigenvalues over much of parameter space, so any perturbation produces damped oscillations with periods of years to decades; some configurations have undamped limit cycles. In a land model, a seasonal litter pulse can excite these oscillations every year, which is unphysical. Three remedies are used: (i) density-dependent turnover, death ∝ Bβ with β ≈ 1.5–2 (Georgiou et al. 2017 [21]), which represents predation, viral lysis and competition and which also makes steady-state SOC respond sub-linearly to inputs, matching long-term fertilisation experiments better than linear turnover does; (ii) reverse MM or ECA kinetics, which weaken the biomass feedback; (iii) explicit dormancy or substrate-limited enzyme production. The steady state of the simplest two-pool microbial model also illustrates why physiology matters: with dS/dt = I + rB − VmaxBS/(Km+S) and dB/dt = CUE·VmaxBS/(Km+S) − rB,

$$S^* = \frac{K_m\,r}{\text{CUE}\,V_{max} - r}, \qquad B^* = \frac{I\,\text{CUE}}{r\,(1-\text{CUE})}$$

so SOC at steady state does not depend on inputs at all (inputs only change biomass) and decreases with CUE, because efficient microbes build more biomass that decomposes more. This is the Allison (2010) counter-intuition. It disappears when necromass is stabilised in a pool that microbes cannot immediately re-consume (MIMICS protected pool, MAOM): then higher CUE means more necromass transfer to protection and SOC can rise with CUE, which is the Cotrufo/MEMS intuition. Which regime applies depends on mineral capacity and on turnover form; lab (c) lets you switch between them.

Projections

Wieder, Bonan & Allison (2013) [3] put a MIMICS-precursor into a global framework and found it matched present-day SOC as well as the CLM4 Century-type model but projected a very different future: with CUE declining under warming the microbial model lost less carbon, with constant CUE it lost more, and under increased NPP it gained less because biomass rose and primed existing SOM. Sulman et al. (2014) [6] used CORPSE to show that elevated CO₂ could either increase or decrease SOC depending on clay content: in low-clay soils rhizosphere priming dominated and SOC fell, in high-clay soils protection of new inputs dominated and SOC rose. Sulman et al. (2018) [7] compared five models (Century-type, MIMICS, CORPSE, MEND and others) at long-term experimental sites and found that no model class was consistently better, that the microbial models disagreed with each other as much as with the first-order ones, and that the spread of responses to warming and N addition was wider with microbial models. The conclusion has been stated by Luo et al. (2016) and Todd-Brown et al. (2012) [23]: microbial models add structural realism and the right qualitative behaviours (priming, acclimation, saturation), but their benefit for projection depends on constraining their parameters.

Parameter identifiability, data constraints and scaling

A four-pool microbial model has 10–15 parameters (two Vmax, two Km, their temperature sensitivities, CUE and its slope, turnover, enzyme production and loss, partitioning of necromass), and the data usually available (bulk SOC, respiration time series, occasionally biomass) constrain only a few combinations of them. Hararuk, Smith & Luo (2015) [22] fitted a microbial model and a Century-type model to global SOC data with Bayesian calibration and found both could match the stocks, but the microbial model's posterior still spanned a wide range of warming responses; the extra structure was not constrained by the stock data. Marschmann et al. (2024) showed with identifiability analysis that most microbial-model parameters are structurally or practically non-identifiable from incubation data unless biomass, enzymes and isotope-labelled fluxes are all measured, and that different parameter sets fitted equally well while diverging in projection. The remedies are the same as for first-order models (measurable pools, radiocarbon, long-term experiments, isotope tracers) plus trait data: CUE from ¹⁸O methods, growth rates, enzyme kinetics measured at the site, and biomass by fumigation.

Scaling from microbes to the globe. No model simulates taxa. The options are functional groups (MIMICS's r and K strategists; bacteria and fungi in ORCHIMIC) with trait trade-offs (growth rate vs affinity vs CUE vs turnover), continuous trait distributions, or a single aggregated biomass with emergent parameters. Dormancy matters: often more than half of biomass is inactive, so active biomass, not total, should drive kinetics (MEND and ORCHIMIC separate them). Moisture enters through diffusion rather than through an empirical curve: in DAMM, substrate supply to enzymes scales with the water-filled pore space (solute diffusion) while O₂ supply scales with air-filled pore space, so the product is humped; Manzoni et al. (2012) [11] (and Manzoni & Katul) showed that the sharp decline of respiration below about −1 MPa matric potential is consistent with the loss of hydraulic connectivity cutting substrate diffusion, and that microbial osmoregulation costs reduce CUE under drought. Sinsabaugh's ecoenzymatic stoichiometry uses the ratios of C-, N- and P-acquiring enzyme activities (β-glucosidase : N-acetylglucosaminidase + leucine aminopeptidase : phosphatase ≈ 1:1:1 globally) as a trait-level indicator of which element limits microbial growth, and connects enzyme allocation to CUE via the threshold element ratio.

Connections to ecosystem models

  • CLM has run MIMICS as an alternative soil module (CLM-MIMICS) alongside the default Century-type CLM5 BGC soil; results differ most in the sign of the response to increased productivity and in high-latitude warming responses.
  • ORCHIDEE's ORCHIMIC replaces the Century pools with litter, enzymes, bacteria and fungi; JULES and the ECOSSE lineage remain first order with RothC pools; E3SM (ELM) includes ECA kinetics for nutrient competition, which is the same mathematics as the decomposition kinetics above.
  • DayCent and BioCro remain first order; their microbial biomass is implicit in the active pool, which is why they cannot represent rhizosphere priming of SOM by root exudates (the Root–microbe page) except by prescribing it.
  • Millennial v2 and MEMS 2.0 keep a microbial pool but put most of the dynamics into measurable POM and MAOM fractions with sorption; this is the current compromise between microbial realism and identifiability (next page).
  • In any coupled model the microbial module determines three things the first-order module could not: how NPP changes translate into SOC (priming vs stabilisation), how warming affects decomposition after acclimation, and how N limitation of decomposition emerges from stoichiometry rather than from fixed pool C:N.

Exam-style questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer.

1. Write the first-order, forward Michaelis–Menten and reverse Michaelis–Menten expressions for decomposition, state the limiting cases of each, and explain which behaviours of real soils each can and cannot reproduce.

First order: dC/dt = −kC; the rate depends only on substrate and climate scalars. Forward MM: dC/dt = −VmaxB·C/(Km+C); at C ≪ Km it is bilinear, (Vmax/Km)BC, and at C ≫ Km it saturates at VmaxB, proportional to biomass. Reverse MM: dC/dt = −VmaxC·B/(KB+B); linear in substrate, saturating in biomass, so when B ≫ KB it reduces to first order with k = Vmax. First order cannot produce priming (pools are independent), biomass feedback or acclimation, but is stable and parsimonious. Forward MM produces priming (more biomass → more decomposition), input-independent steady-state SOC, and strong sensitivity to CUE; it also produces damped or sustained oscillations after perturbation because of the predator–prey structure. Reverse MM damps the feedback: doubling biomass barely changes decomposition when enzymes saturate accessible sites, so priming is weaker and stability better, and steady-state SOC depends on inputs. ECA kinetics (Tang & Riley 2013) interpolates between them depending on whether enzymes or substrates are scarce. In steady, undisturbed soils with roughly constant biomass all three agree, which is why first-order models fit present-day stocks; they differ during transitions (warming, CO₂, land-use change).

2. Define carbon-use efficiency, give its typical range and methods of measurement, and explain why its temperature dependence is both important and contested.

CUE = growth/(growth + respiration) = growth/uptake, the fraction of assimilated carbon that becomes biomass. Typical range 0.2–0.6, mean around 0.3 in meta-analyses; thermodynamic maximum ~0.6–0.8. Measured by ¹³C-substrate incorporation into biomass (fumigation) versus ¹³CO₂ (substrate-specific, biased to labile compounds), ¹⁸O-water incorporation into DNA to measure growth independent of substrate, and calorespirometry. Importance: necromass production equals CUE × uptake, so CUE sets how much processed carbon can be stabilised; and in microbial models decomposition scales with biomass, so CUE sets decomposer abundance. If CUE declines with warming (maintenance costs rise faster than growth; Allison 2010 assumed −0.016 °C⁻¹), biomass and enzymes shrink and warming-induced SOC losses are buffered, possibly to near zero. If CUE is constant, warming increases decomposition and biomass and losses are amplified. Contested because: glucose-based measurements show declines, but complex substrates show smaller or no declines (Frey 2013); long-term warmed communities acclimate; meta-analysis suggests stoichiometry, not temperature, dominates CUE (Sinsabaugh 2013); and growth-based (¹⁸O) measurements give different values from substrate-based ones. The sign and size of the CUE–temperature slope is therefore one of the largest single sources of structural uncertainty in soil carbon feedback projections.

3. Derive the steady state of a two-pool microbial model (substrate S, biomass B) with forward MM kinetics and linear turnover, and discuss the two counter-intuitive results.

dS/dt = I + rB − VmaxB·S/(Km+S); dB/dt = CUE·VmaxB·S/(Km+S) − rB. Setting dB/dt = 0 with B > 0 gives CUE·VmaxS/(Km+S) = r, hence S* = Kmr/(CUE·Vmax − r), requiring CUE·Vmax > r for a viable population. Setting dS/dt = 0 and substituting uptake U = rB/CUE: I + rB = rB/CUE → B* = I·CUE/(r(1 − CUE)). Results: (i) S* is independent of input I. More litter produces more biomass, which decomposes faster, until the substrate is drawn down to the level at which growth just balances death. Observed SOC increases with inputs in long-term experiments contradict this, which motivated density-dependent turnover (death ∝ Bβ), for which B* ∝ (I·CUE/(1−CUE))1/β and S* then rises with I sub-linearly. (ii) S* decreases with CUE: efficient microbes maintain more biomass per unit substrate, so the substrate is depleted further. This conflicts with the MEMS intuition that high CUE builds SOM; the reconciliation is that necromass in this model returns to the same consumable pool, whereas in reality a fraction is stabilised on minerals where microbes cannot immediately re-consume it. Adding a protected pool M with dM/dt = fprB − kpM gives M* = fprB*/kp, increasing with CUE, so total carbon can become non-monotonic or increasing in CUE depending on fp and kp.

4. Explain the threshold element ratio and use it to predict whether a residue with C:N = 60 will immobilise or mineralise nitrogen, and how its CUE will compare with a residue of C:N = 15.

Microbes maintain a biomass C:N of about 8 and grow with a maximum CUE of about 0.5–0.6. Per unit C taken up, they retain CUEmax units of C and therefore need CUEmax/(C:N)B units of N. The substrate supplies 1/(C:N)S units of N per unit C. Equating the two defines the threshold element ratio TER = (C:N)B/CUEmax ≈ 8/0.55 ≈ 15 (20–30 when inorganic N uptake and N-use efficiency are included). For C:N = 15 the substrate supplies exactly the N required: CUE is at its maximum and net mineralisation is near zero. For C:N = 60, the substrate supplies 1/60 N per C but 0.55/8 ≈ 0.069 is needed; either inorganic N must be immobilised from the soil (net immobilisation, the classic "N tie-up" by straw) or, if none is available, growth is N-limited and CUE falls to (C:N)B/(C:N)S = 8/60 ≈ 0.13 with the surplus carbon respired (overflow) or left undecomposed. The C:N = 60 residue therefore decomposes more slowly, immobilises N, contributes less necromass to SOM, and in models with stoichiometric CUE (MIMICS-CN, Millennial, Manzoni's framework) this emerges without prescribing pool C:N ratios as Century does.

5. Describe the structure of MIMICS, and explain how it produces responses to warming and to elevated inputs that differ from a Century-type model.

MIMICS: metabolic and structural litter pools (split by lignin:N); two microbial functional groups, r (copiotroph: high Vmax, low Km affinity trade-off, lower CUE on structural litter, faster turnover) and K (oligotroph: slower, higher CUE on poor substrates, slower turnover); three SOM pools: physically protected (desorbs slowly, fraction set by clay), chemically recalcitrant (fraction set by litter chemistry) and available (the only pool microbes consume). All decomposition fluxes are forward MM in microbial biomass, with Vmax and Km temperature-dependent and modified by litter quality and clay. Dead microbes are partitioned among the SOM pools. Warming: Vmax rises, but so does Km; biomass responds, and the net SOC change depends on whether increased decomposition is offset by increased necromass flow to protected pools and on the CUE–temperature assumption; outcomes range from loss to little change, and the spatial pattern differs from a Q₁₀ model (largest effects where biomass responds most, not where stocks are largest). Elevated inputs: biomass increases, decomposition of available SOM increases (priming), so SOC rises much less than proportionally and can fall in low-clay soils where little necromass is protected; a Century model would increase SOC in proportion to inputs. These are the Wieder 2013 and Sulman 2014 results. Weaknesses: functional-group parameters are tuned, not measured; the protected pool is conceptual; and early versions oscillated, fixed by density-dependent turnover.

6. Why do forward-MM microbial models oscillate, and what modifications stabilise them? Relate your answer to the eigenvalues of the linearised system.

In a model where substrate is consumed in proportion to biomass and biomass grows in proportion to substrate consumption, the two state variables form a consumer–resource (predator–prey) pair. Linearising around the steady state gives a Jacobian whose off-diagonal terms have opposite signs (substrate reduces biomass growth's limitation, biomass reduces substrate), which produces complex eigenvalues whenever the diagonal damping (substrate loss rate and microbial death rate) is weak relative to the coupling; the real part is negative in most cases (damped oscillation with period set by the geometric mean of the turnover times, years to decades) but can be positive for high CUE and low death rates, giving limit cycles (Wang et al. 2014). Perturbations such as a litter pulse or warming then produce biomass overshoot followed by substrate depletion and biomass collapse, repeating with decaying amplitude. Stabilisers: (i) density-dependent turnover (death ∝ Bβ, β > 1) increases the diagonal damping as biomass grows and removes the oscillations for β ≈ 2 (Georgiou 2017); (ii) reverse MM or ECA kinetics make decomposition insensitive to biomass when biomass is high, weakening the coupling; (iii) a dormant pool that absorbs biomass changes; (iv) explicit enzyme pools with slow turnover add a lag that can worsen or improve damping depending on rates. Beyond stability, density-dependent turnover also fixes the input-independence of steady-state SOC.

7. A reviewer argues that microbial-explicit models should replace first-order models in Earth system models. Make the case for and against, with evidence.

For: first-order models cannot represent priming, acclimation, stoichiometric control of decomposition or CUE-dependent stabilisation, all of which are observed and all of which matter for the response to CO₂, warming and N deposition; microbial models reproduce present-day stocks and litter decomposition as well as first-order models (Wieder 2015) while producing qualitatively correct behaviours in FACE (rhizosphere priming, Sulman 2014) and warming experiments (acclimation, Allison 2010); necromass dominance of stable SOM (Liang 2019) implies the microbial route is the main stabilisation pathway. Against: the parameters (Vmax, Km, CUE slopes, turnover, functional-group traits) are mostly non-identifiable from available data (Hararuk 2015; Marschmann 2024), so added structure adds uncertainty rather than skill: Sulman 2018 found microbial models disagreed with each other as much as with first-order models at long-term sites and widened the projection spread; early microbial models oscillate and have input-independent steady states; and computational cost and tuning effort are high for a module whose sign of response to warming depends on one poorly known slope. A balanced position: adopt intermediate-complexity structures with measurable pools and few microbial parameters (MIMICS with density-dependent turnover, Millennial, MEMS 2.0), constrain them with fraction, radiocarbon, ¹⁸O-CUE and warming-experiment data, run them alongside first-order models as a structural ensemble, and report both.

8. Explain the DAMM model and how it reconciles constant intrinsic activation energy with variable apparent temperature and moisture sensitivities.

DAMM (Davidson et al. 2012) computes heterotrophic respiration as a dual Arrhenius–Michaelis–Menten function: Rh = Vmax(T) × [Sx]/(KM,Sx + [Sx]) × [O₂]/(KM,O₂ + [O₂]), with Vmax = α exp(−Ea/RT) and Ea constant. The soluble substrate concentration at the enzyme [Sx] is the product of total soluble carbon, a diffusion coefficient and the water content raised to a power (solute diffusion through water films increases with moisture), and [O₂] is proportional to air-filled porosity raised to 4/3 (gas diffusion decreases with moisture). Consequences: (i) in dry soil respiration is substrate-limited and the apparent temperature sensitivity is low because [Sx] ≪ KM and KM itself rises with temperature; (ii) in wet soil O₂ limits, giving the humped moisture response without an empirical curve; (iii) at intermediate moisture the full Arrhenius sensitivity is expressed. Seasonal changes in soil moisture therefore change apparent Q₁₀ even though Ea is constant, which is Davidson & Janssens's intrinsic–apparent distinction made quantitative. DAMM is a flux model (no explicit pools) and is used as the kinetic core in several microbial models and as a diagnostic for eddy-covariance and chamber data.
