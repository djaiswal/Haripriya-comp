Topic 10 of the SPAC section

Transpiration and Penman–Monteith

How the energy budget of a leaf or canopy sets its evaporation, why one algebraic trick lets us compute latent heat flux without knowing the surface temperature, and what the resulting equation says about which controls on evapotranspiration matter where.

The one-paragraph version

Evaporation needs energy and a way to carry vapour away. The energy balance Rn = H + λE + G says that net radiation is spent on sensible heat H, latent heat λE and ground heat flux G. Both H and λE are diffusive fluxes driven by gradients between the surface and the air: H by the temperature difference through an aerodynamic resistance ra, λE by the vapour pressure difference through ra plus a surface resistance rs that for vegetation is the inverse of canopy conductance. Both depend on the surface temperature, which is unknown. Penman (1948) eliminated it by linearising the saturation vapour pressure curve around air temperature (slope Δ), and Monteith (1965) added rs, giving λE = [Δ(Rn − G) + ρcpD/ra]/[Δ + γ(1 + rs/ra)]. The equation has two limits. When ra is large (short, smooth canopies, low wind), evaporation approaches the equilibrium rate set by available energy; when ra is small (tall, rough forests), it approaches the imposed rate ρcpD/(γrs) set by VPD and stomata. The decoupling coefficient Ω says where a canopy sits between the two, and therefore how much stomatal control it has over its own water loss. Priestley–Taylor (λE = 1.26 × equilibrium) and FAO-56 reference ET are special cases. The same algebra, applied to a single leaf, gives leaf temperature; applied to two layers (Shuttleworth–Wallace) it partitions transpiration from soil evaporation; and in one form or another it is the latent heat scheme of every land-surface and crop model.

Examiner's lens

Be able to derive Penman–Monteith on the board from the energy balance and the two flux-gradient equations, naming every assumption. Know the definitions and magnitudes of Δ, γ, ra, rs and Ω, and be able to say in one sentence why a forest's transpiration responds to stomata while a wet grassland's responds to radiation. Expect follow-ups on the energy-balance closure problem at flux towers and on how ET is partitioned into transpiration and evaporation.

The surface energy balance

For a leaf, a canopy or a landscape, conservation of energy over a time step reads

$$R_n = H + \lambda E + G + S, \qquad R_n = (1 - \alpha)\,S_\downarrow + \varepsilon\,L_\downarrow - \varepsilon\sigma T_s^4,$$

where Rn is net radiation (W m⁻²; absorbed shortwave with albedo α ≈ 0.1–0.25 for vegetation, plus absorbed longwave from the sky minus emitted longwave at surface temperature Ts, emissivity ε ≈ 0.97), H the sensible heat flux, λE the latent heat flux (λ = 2.45 MJ kg⁻¹ at 20 °C, so 1 mm day⁻¹ of evaporation = 28.4 W m⁻²), G the heat conducted into soil (≈ 5–15% of Rn by day under a canopy, more over bare soil) and S the storage in biomass and canopy air plus the energy used in photosynthesis (small; often neglected except for tall forests on sub-daily scales). Over a day G and S nearly cancel, and Rn − G is the available energy.

H and λE are written as flux-gradient relations with resistances (s m⁻¹) or conductances (m s⁻¹; or mol m⁻² s⁻¹, dividing by the molar volume 0.0245 m³ mol⁻¹):

$$H = \rho c_p\,\frac{T_s - T_a}{r_a}, \qquad \lambda E = \frac{\rho c_p}{\gamma}\,\frac{e_s(T_s) - e_a}{r_a + r_s}, \qquad \gamma = \frac{c_p P}{0.622\,\lambda} \approx 0.066\ \text{kPa K}^{-1}.$$

ρcp ≈ 1200 J m⁻³ K⁻¹ is the volumetric heat capacity of air. The psychrometric constant γ converts a vapour pressure difference into the temperature difference that would carry the same energy flux; it appears because λE is written in pressure units rather than in specific humidity (λE = λρ(qs − qa)/(ra + rs) with q ≈ 0.622e/P). The vapour source is assumed saturated at Ts: inside the leaf's substomatal cavities this is true; for a canopy it defines an effective "big-leaf" surface. The surface resistance rs is the resistance between that saturated surface and the air just outside the leaves. For a closed, dry canopy it is approximately the parallel combination of all leaf stomatal resistances:

$$\frac{1}{r_s} = G_c \approx \sum_{\text{leaves}} g_{s,i}\,\Delta\!L_i \approx \bar g_s\,\mathrm{LAI}_{eff}, \qquad \text{FAO-56: } r_s = \frac{r_l}{0.5\,\mathrm{LAI}} = \frac{100}{0.5 \times 2.88} \approx 70\ \text{s m}^{-1},$$

with LAIeff the "active" sunlit leaf area, roughly half the total for dense canopies because shaded leaves have low conductance. For a wet canopy or open water rs = 0; for partly vegetated or drying soil rs includes a soil resistance. Typical magnitudes: rs ≈ 30–70 s m⁻¹ for a well-watered crop at midday (Gc ≈ 15–30 mm s⁻¹), 100–300 s m⁻¹ for forests, rising to > 1000 s m⁻¹ under drought or at night.

Aerodynamic resistance, Δ and γ

Momentum, heat and vapour are carried from the surface to a reference height z by turbulence. For neutral stratification the mean wind profile above a canopy of height h is logarithmic,

$$u(z) = \frac{u_*}{k}\ln\!\left(\frac{z - d}{z_{0m}}\right), \qquad r_a = \frac{\ln\!\left(\dfrac{z_m - d}{z_{0m}}\right)\ln\!\left(\dfrac{z_h - d}{z_{0h}}\right)}{k^2\,u(z_m)},$$

with von Kármán's constant k = 0.41, zero-plane displacement d ≈ 0.67h, roughness length for momentum z0m ≈ 0.123h, and roughness length for heat and vapour z0h ≈ 0.1z0m (the "excess resistance" kB⁻¹ ≈ 2, because scalars lack the pressure-drag pathway that momentum has). For the FAO reference grass (h = 0.12 m, measurements at 2 m) this reduces to ra = 208/u2 s m⁻¹, about 100 s m⁻¹ at 2 m s⁻¹; for a 20 m forest at the same wind speed ra ≈ 5–15 s m⁻¹. Under unstable (daytime) or stable (night) conditions the profiles depart from logarithmic and Monin–Obukhov stability functions Ψm, Ψh correct the logarithms; unstable conditions reduce ra, stable conditions increase it, and land-surface models iterate on this because the stability depends on H.

Two thermodynamic quantities complete the toolkit. The slope of the saturation vapour pressure curve Δ = des/dT follows from differentiating Tetens,

$$\Delta = \frac{4098\,e_s(T)}{(T + 237.3)^2}\ \text{kPa K}^{-1},$$
$$\Delta(10\,°\text{C}) = 0.082,\quad \Delta(20) = 0.145,\quad \Delta(25) = 0.189,\quad \Delta(30) = 0.243\ \text{kPa K}^{-1},$$

and the ratio Δ/γ (0.9 at 5 °C, 2.2 at 20 °C, 3.7 at 30 °C) is the number that decides how available energy is split between H and λE at equilibrium: warm air evaporates more per unit energy because the same temperature excess carries a much larger vapour pressure excess.

From Penman to Penman–Monteith

The obstacle to computing λE from standard weather data is Ts: it enters both H and es(Ts) and is rarely measured. Penman [1], working on open water and wet grass (rs = 0), noticed that Ts can be eliminated by one approximation. Monteith [2] generalised it to a dry vegetated surface. The derivation, as an examiner expects it:

Derivation

  1. Energy balance: H = Rn − G − λE.
  2. Sensible heat: Ts − Ta = H ra/(ρcp) = (Rn − G − λE) ra/(ρcp).
  3. Latent heat: λE = (ρcp/γ)[es(Ts) − ea]/(ra + rs).
  4. Penman's linearisation: es(Ts) ≈ es(Ta) + Δ(Ts − Ta), with Δ evaluated at Ta (or at the mean of Ta and Ts). Then es(Ts) − ea = D + Δ(Ts − Ta), where D = es(Ta) − ea is the VPD of the air.
  5. Substitute step 2 into step 4: es(Ts) − ea = D + Δ(Rn − G − λE) ra/(ρcp).
  6. Substitute into step 3 and multiply out: λE γ (ra + rs) = ρcpD + Δ(Rn − G) ra − Δ λE ra.
  7. Collect λE: λE [Δ ra + γ(ra + rs)] = Δ(Rn − G) ra + ρcpD. Divide by ra:
$$\lambda E = \frac{\Delta\,(R_n - G) + \rho c_p\,D/r_a}{\Delta + \gamma\left(1 + r_s/r_a\right)} \qquad \text{(Penman–Monteith)}$$

With rs = 0 this is Penman's equation for a wet surface, λE = [Δ(Rn − G) + ρcpD/ra]/(Δ + γ); Penman wrote the second term as a wind function f(u)·D. The surface temperature has disappeared; it can be recovered afterwards from step 2. The only approximation is the linearisation, which is exact as Ts → Ta and introduces errors of a few percent when |Ts − Ta| exceeds ~5 K (Lab 1). The structural assumptions are: one source height for heat and vapour (the big leaf), the same ra for both, a saturated source, steady state, and neutral or corrected stability.

Equilibrium, imposed evaporation and decoupling

Rearranging Penman–Monteith as a weighted sum of two limiting rates is the most useful thing to know about it. Jarvis and McNaughton [4] wrote

Decoupling coefficient

$$\lambda E = \Omega\,\lambda E_{eq} + (1 - \Omega)\,\lambda E_{imp}, \qquad \Omega = \frac{\Delta/\gamma + 1}{\Delta/\gamma + 1 + r_s/r_a},$$
$$\lambda E_{eq} = \frac{\Delta}{\Delta + \gamma}(R_n - G), \qquad \lambda E_{imp} = \frac{\rho c_p\,D}{\gamma\,r_s}.$$

The equilibrium rate is what a wet surface would evaporate in a closed box: as the air above it saturates and the surface temperature adjusts, λE tends to Δ(Rn − G)/(Δ + γ) regardless of ra. The imposed rate is what the surface would lose if it were perfectly coupled to the free air (ra → 0): the air's own D acting through the stomata alone. Ω → 1 when ra ≫ rs (short, smooth, well-watered canopies in light wind: grass, irrigated crops, Ω ≈ 0.8–0.9), so λE follows radiation and stomata have little influence; Ω → 0 when ra ≪ rs (tall, rough, aerodynamically well-coupled forests, especially conifers, Ω ≈ 0.1–0.3), so λE follows D and gs. The sensitivity of λE to stomata is ∂ln λE/∂ln gs = 1 − Ω.

Priestley–Taylor [5] found that over large, well-watered surfaces λE ≈ α λEeq with α = 1.26, the excess over equilibrium being supplied by entrainment of dry air at the top of the boundary layer; α falls toward 1 and below as soil dries and is ≈ 0.7–0.9 for forests, making the ratio λE/λEeq a convenient index of water stress. The Bowen ratio β = H/λE is another: for equilibrium evaporation β = γ/Δ (≈ 0.45 at 20 °C); β ≈ 0.1–0.3 over wet crops, 0.5–1 for forests, > 5 over deserts; it is the basis of the Bowen-ratio energy-balance method, which measures ΔT/Δe between two heights and partitions Rn − G without any resistances. The evaporative fraction EF = λE/(Rn − G) = 1/(1 + β) is roughly constant through the day and is used to scale instantaneous satellite retrievals to daily totals.

FAO-56 reference evapotranspiration [6] applies Penman–Monteith to a hypothetical grass surface (h = 0.12 m, rs = 70 s m⁻¹, α = 0.23, ra = 208/u2), which with daily data in MJ m⁻² day⁻¹ gives the familiar

$$ET_0 = \frac{0.408\,\Delta(R_n - G) + \gamma\,\dfrac{900}{T + 273}\,u_2\,(e_s - e_a)}{\Delta + \gamma(1 + 0.34\,u_2)} \quad \text{mm day}^{-1}.$$

Crop evapotranspiration is then ETc = Kc ET0, with the crop coefficient Kc (0.3–1.2 through the season) absorbing differences in albedo, roughness, rs and ground cover, and a water-stress coefficient Ks ≤ 1 multiplying it under soil-water deficit. The dual-coefficient form Kc = Kcb + Ke separates transpiration (basal) from soil evaporation. It is the standard in irrigation scheduling and in many crop models.

Leaf temperature and the isothermal form

For a single leaf the same balance is written per unit leaf area with molar conductances, radiation absorbed on both sides, and longwave emitted from both sides:

$$R_{abs} - 2\varepsilon\sigma T_l^4 = c_p\,g_H\,(T_l - T_a) + \lambda\,g_v\,\frac{e_s(T_l) - e_a}{P},$$

with cp = 29.3 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, λ = 44 kJ mol⁻¹, gH the boundary-layer conductance for heat summed over both sides (gHa ≈ 0.135√(u/d) mol m⁻² s⁻¹ per side for a leaf of characteristic dimension d in forced convection), and gv the series combination of stomatal and boundary-layer conductance for vapour (gva ≈ 0.147√(u/d)). Linearising the longwave term about Ta defines a radiative conductance gr = 4εσTa³/cp (≈ 0.2 mol m⁻² s⁻¹ per side at 25 °C) and the isothermal net radiation Rni = Rabs − 2εσTa⁴, the net radiation the leaf would have if it were at air temperature. Applying Penman's linearisation to es(Tl) gives the leaf-temperature analogue of Penman–Monteith (Campbell and Norman [9]):

$$T_l - T_a = \frac{\gamma^*}{s + \gamma^*}\left[\frac{R_{ni}}{c_p\,(g_H + g_r)} - \frac{D}{P\,\gamma^*}\right], \qquad \gamma^* = \gamma\,\frac{g_H + g_r}{g_v}, \qquad s = \frac{\Delta}{P}.$$

The bracket contains the two competing terms: radiative heating (positive, large for big leaves in still air, since gH is small) and evaporative cooling (negative, proportional to D and, through γ*, to gv). Leaves are warmer than air when radiation load is high and gs or D is low, and cooler than air when stomata are open in dry air; well-watered crops are typically 1–3 K below air temperature at midday, droughted ones several degrees above, which is the basis of the crop water stress index from infrared thermometry. Small leaves (small d, large gH) track air temperature closely, which is why desert plants have small leaves. The nonlinear balance is what land-surface models solve, usually by Newton iteration on Tl jointly with the stomatal model, because D in the stomatal equation depends on Tl.

Big leaf versus two sources, partitioning and closure

The big-leaf model collapses the canopy to one source at height d + z0h. Raupach and Finnigan [13] summarised the status with their title: single-layer models are incorrect but useful, multilayer models correct but useless. They are incorrect because heat and vapour sources are at different heights (soil evaporation, understory, sunlit upper leaves), because the within-canopy turbulence does not obey gradient-diffusion, and because the "canopy resistance" fitted by inverting Penman–Monteith is a flux-weighted average that depends on conditions. They are useful because at the scale of a flux footprint the errors are small compared with those in rs itself, and because the equation is linear enough to invert. Shuttleworth and Wallace [7] built the standard two-source extension for sparse canopies: a soil source and a canopy source, each with its own surface resistance (rss, rsc) and aerodynamic resistance (ras soil-to-canopy-air, rac leaf boundary layer), both feeding a canopy air space that exchanges with the reference height through raa. λE = λEsoil + λEcanopy is obtained from two coupled Penman–Monteith expressions, and the partitioning between them follows LAI, soil wetness and the resistances. Land-surface models use essentially this structure, with sunlit and shaded leaves as separate canopy sources.

ET partitioning. Transpiration T, soil evaporation Es and canopy interception evaporation Ei sum to ET. Globally the ratio T/ET is now put at roughly 0.6: Jasechko et al. [10] used lake-catchment isotope mass balances to argue for 80–90%, which prompted a re-examination; Schlesinger and Jasechko [11] compiled 81 studies and found T/ET = 61 ± 15%, highest in tropical rainforests (~70%) and temperate grasslands and lowest in deserts and croplands with bare-soil periods, and most land-surface models at the time were lower still (≈ 0.4–0.5), under-estimating transpiration relative to soil evaporation. Methods: isotopes (δ¹⁸O, δ²H, because transpiration is non-fractionating at steady state while evaporation fractionates), sap flow scaled to the stand plus eddy-covariance ET, lysimeters, chambers, and the ratio of GPP-derived to total ET using water-use-efficiency relations (underlying WUE methods). The partition matters because T is coupled to CO₂ uptake and responds to stomata and leaf area, whereas Es responds to surface soil moisture and litter; getting T/ET right is a precondition for a model to get the CO₂ and VPD responses of ET right.

Energy-balance closure. At eddy-covariance sites the measured turbulent fluxes typically fall short of the available energy: H + λE ≈ 0.7–0.9 (Rn − G). Wilson et al. [12] found a mean imbalance of about 20% across 22 FLUXNET sites, worse at night and in stable conditions, and attributed it to sampling-footprint mismatch between Rn (a few hundred m²) and the flux footprint (hectares), systematic instrument bias, neglected storage terms (canopy air, biomass, soil above the heat-flux plates), and loss of low-frequency and large-eddy (secondary circulation) flux contributions at the half-hour averaging scale. The gap is largely attributed today to the last cause over heterogeneous landscapes. Practical consequences: ET from towers is probably under-estimated unless corrected; corrections either preserve the measured Bowen ratio and scale H and λE together, or assign the residual to one flux; and comparing a model's λE (which closes the balance by construction) with uncorrected tower λE builds in a ~10–20% bias. Inverting Penman–Monteith for canopy conductance from tower data inherits all of this.

Connections to ecosystem and land-surface models

Land-surface models do not use the closed-form Penman–Monteith equation. CLM, JULES, Noah-MP, ORCHIDEE and JSBACH solve the leaf and ground energy balances numerically each time step: the canopy (sunlit and shaded big leaves, or multiple layers) exchanges heat and vapour with a canopy air space through leaf boundary-layer conductances; the ground exchanges through a within-canopy aerodynamic resistance and a soil resistance; the canopy air space exchanges with the atmosphere through ra from Monin–Obukhov similarity, iterated for stability; and Tl, Tcanopy-air, Tground and the fluxes are solved simultaneously with the stomatal–photosynthesis model, which supplies gs as a function of A and D(Tl). The Penman–Monteith form is what you get when you linearise this system, and it is the right mental model for interpreting the results: a model's ET sensitivity to g1 depends on its simulated Ω, which depends on its roughness parameterisation; wet-canopy evaporation after rain is computed with rs = 0 for the wet fraction; and Noah-MP offers a Jarvis or a Ball–Berry stomatal option behind the same energy balance.

Crop and ecosystem models mostly use the closed form or simpler. DSSAT defaults to Priestley–Taylor for potential ET with FAO-56 Penman–Monteith as an option; APSIM uses a Priestley–Taylor-type potential ET with crop factors; BioCro computes leaf-level transpiration with a Penman–Monteith-style energy balance layer by layer in its canopy, coupled to Collatz photosynthesis and a Ball–Berry conductance; DayCent and Century use a Penman-type potential ET from temperature and radiation and partition it to soil layers and transpiration by water availability; MIMICS and other soil-only models take soil moisture as input. The hierarchy matters when coupling: a soil carbon model driven by a crop model's soil water will inherit whatever that model's ET scheme does under rising VPD and CO₂, which for Priestley–Taylor is nothing (no stomatal term) and for FAO-56 only what a prescribed Kc allows.

Sensitivity summary. From the equation: ∂λE/∂Rn = Δ/[Δ + γ(1 + rs/ra)], near 0.7 for a crop and 0.3 for a forest; ∂λE/∂D = ρcp/[ra(Δ + γ(1 + rs/ra))], large for forests, small for crops, and reduced further once gs responds to D; ∂ln λE/∂ln gs = 1 − Ω; and ∂λE/∂u has either sign, pushing λE toward λEimp. Under climate change the leading-order ET changes come from Rn (cloud, aerosol), D (warming at roughly constant RH), gs (CO₂, drought) and LAI (greening), and the equation shows that their relative weights differ by vegetation type and are set by Ω.

Exam-style questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer.

1. Derive the Penman–Monteith equation from the surface energy balance, stating each assumption. What was Penman's contribution and what was Monteith's?

Start with Rn − G = H + λE; H = ρcp(Ts − Ta)/ra; λE = (ρcp/γ)[es(Ts) − ea]/(ra + rs). The unknown Ts appears in both fluxes. Linearise the saturation curve about air temperature: es(Ts) ≈ es(Ta) + Δ(Ts − Ta), so es(Ts) − ea = D + Δ(Ts − Ta). Replace Ts − Ta by Hra/(ρcp) = (Rn − G − λE)ra/(ρcp). Substituting into the latent heat equation and multiplying through: λEγ(ra + rs) = ρcpD + Δ(Rn − G)ra − ΔλEra; collecting λE and dividing by ra gives λE = [Δ(Rn − G) + ρcpD/ra]/[Δ + γ(1 + rs/ra)]. Assumptions: steady state; one effective source height for heat and vapour (big leaf) with the same ra for both; the vapour source is saturated at Ts; Δ is constant between Ta and Ts (the linearisation, accurate to a few percent for |Ts − Ta| < 5 K); ra known (neutral log profile or stability-corrected); G and storage known or negligible. Penman (1948) introduced the linearisation and the combination of the energy-balance and aerodynamic approaches for a wet surface (rs = 0), writing the aerodynamic term with an empirical wind function. Monteith (1965) inserted the surface (canopy) resistance in series with ra for the vapour path only, turning it into an equation for a transpiring canopy with a physiological control, and showed that rs can be interpreted as the parallel sum of stomatal resistances.

2. Define the decoupling coefficient Ω. Explain why transpiration from a coniferous forest is controlled by stomata while transpiration from irrigated pasture is controlled by net radiation, and give typical values.

Rewriting Penman–Monteith as λE = ΩλEeq + (1 − Ω)λEimp, with λEeq = Δ(Rn − G)/(Δ + γ) and λEimp = ρcpD/(γrs), gives Ω = (Δ/γ + 1)/(Δ/γ + 1 + rs/ra). Ω measures how far the canopy surface is decoupled from the free atmosphere. A tall, rough conifer forest has very small ra (5–10 s m⁻¹) and large rs (100–300 s m⁻¹), so rs/ra ≈ 20–50 and Ω ≈ 0.1–0.2: the canopy air is essentially the free air, the VPD at the leaf is imposed by the atmosphere, and λE ≈ ρcpD/(γrs) is proportional to canopy conductance; the sensitivity ∂ln λE/∂ln gs = 1 − Ω ≈ 0.85. A short, smooth, well-watered pasture has ra ≈ 50–100 s m⁻¹ and rs ≈ 30–60 s m⁻¹, so rs/ra ≈ 0.5–1 and Ω ≈ 0.8–0.9: the canopy air humidifies and cools toward the surface, the local VPD adjusts until λE approaches the equilibrium rate set by available energy, and stomatal changes are largely compensated by changes in the local gradient (if stomata close, the canopy air dries and warms, pulling more vapour per unit conductance). Consequences: forests respond to VPD and drought through gs and show strong CO₂-induced reductions in transpiration; crops respond to radiation and show weak transpiration responses to gs changes; and in models the simulated Ω, set by roughness and LAI, governs how much a stomatal parameter such as g1 matters for ET.

3. A well-watered maize canopy (h = 2 m) at midday: Rn = 600 W m⁻², G = 60 W m⁻², Ta = 30 °C, RH = 40%, u = 2.5 m s⁻¹ at 4 m, Gc = 20 mm s⁻¹. Estimate λE, H, the Bowen ratio and Ω.

es(30) = 4.24 kPa, ea = 1.70 kPa, D = 2.55 kPa. Δ = 4098 × 4.24/267.3² = 0.243 kPa K⁻¹; γ = 0.066 kPa K⁻¹; Δ/γ = 3.7. ra: d = 1.34 m, z0m = 0.246 m, z0h = 0.0246 m; ln((4 − 1.34)/0.246) = ln 10.8 = 2.38; ln((4 − 1.34)/0.0246) = ln 108 = 4.68; ra = 2.38 × 4.68/(0.168 × 2.5) = 26.5 s m⁻¹. rs = 1/0.020 = 50 s m⁻¹. ρcpD/ra = 1200 × 2.55/26.5 = 115 W m⁻² kPa⁻¹·kPa, i.e. 115 in the numerator's units. Numerator: 0.243 × 540 + 115 = 131 + 115 = 246; denominator: 0.243 + 0.066 × (1 + 50/26.5) = 0.243 + 0.190 = 0.433. λE = 568 W m⁻² (≈ 0.83 mm h⁻¹), H = 540 − 568 = −28 W m⁻² (slight downward sensible heat: advection-assisted evaporation, the "oasis" condition). Bowen ratio ≈ −0.05. Ω = (3.7 + 1)/(3.7 + 1 + 1.89) = 0.71. Check: λEeq = 0.787 × 540 = 425, λEimp = 1200 × 2.55/(0.066 × 50) = 927; ΩλEeq + (1 − Ω)λEimp = 302 + 269 = 571 ✓. Priestley–Taylor would give 1.26 × 425 = 535 W m⁻². The surface is about 0.6 K cooler than the air (Ts − Ta = Hra/ρcp = −28 × 26.5/1200).

4. Explain the energy-balance closure problem at eddy-covariance sites: its magnitude, its likely causes, and its consequences for model evaluation and for estimating canopy conductance.

At most flux towers the sum of measured turbulent fluxes falls short of the measured available energy: H + λE ≈ 0.7–0.9 (Rn − G), a mean gap near 20% in the FLUXNET synthesis of Wilson et al. (2002), larger at night and in stable, low-turbulence conditions and over tall or heterogeneous vegetation. Causes: (i) mismatch of source areas, Rn and G sampling a few metres while the flux footprint covers hectares; (ii) unmeasured storage in the canopy air column, biomass and the soil layer above the heat-flux plates, important at sub-daily scales for forests; (iii) systematic sensor errors (sonic anemometer angle-of-attack, gas-analyser high-frequency attenuation) that reduce covariances; (iv) loss of low-frequency flux contributions from large eddies and secondary circulations that are not captured in 30-min averaging at a single point, now thought to be the main cause over heterogeneous landscapes. Consequences: tower ET is biased low unless corrected, and the correction is ambiguous (scale H and λE preserving the Bowen ratio, the usual choice, or assign the residual to λE); models close the balance by construction, so model–data comparisons carry a 10–20% structural discrepancy; annual ET and water-use-efficiency estimates, and canopy conductance obtained by inverting Penman–Monteith with the measured λE, inherit the bias, which propagates into g1 estimated from towers (one reason tower g1 exceeds leaf g1). Good practice: report the closure ratio, test sensitivity to both correction methods, and use the Bowen-ratio-preserving correction when comparing with models.

5. What is the Priestley–Taylor equation, under what conditions does it hold, and why is α = 1.26 greater than 1? When would you expect it to fail?

λE = αΔ(Rn − G)/(Δ + γ) with α = 1.26: evaporation is a fixed multiple of the equilibrium rate, with no explicit humidity, wind or stomatal term. It was derived empirically for extensive, well-watered surfaces (open water, saturated land, well-watered crops) under minimal advection. Equilibrium evaporation is the rate approached by a wet surface under a closed, saturating boundary layer; real boundary layers entrain dry, warm air from the free troposphere at their top, which keeps the surface layer below saturation and sustains an aerodynamic (VPD) contribution; averaged over days this adds ≈ 26% over large wet areas. Failures: it over-predicts as soil dries or stomata close (α falls to 0.7–0.9 for forests and < 0.5 in drought), under conditions of strong regional advection (irrigated fields in deserts, where α can exceed 1.5), for small or rough surfaces with small Ω where λE follows D, and in winter or at high latitude where Δ/γ is small and the aerodynamic term dominates. In models it is used as a potential-ET reference (DSSAT, APSIM, some hydrological models), with soil-water stress functions reducing actual ET; this makes those models insensitive to VPD and CO₂ effects on stomata, which the Penman–Monteith form with a dynamic rs can represent.

6. Describe how a land-surface model such as CLM or JULES actually computes transpiration, and relate it to the Penman–Monteith equation. Why do these models not simply use the closed-form equation?

The model solves a coupled system each time step: a leaf energy balance for sunlit and shaded leaves (absorbed shortwave and longwave from the radiative transfer scheme, emission, sensible heat to the canopy air through a leaf boundary-layer conductance, latent heat through stomatal and boundary-layer conductances in series for dry leaves and boundary layer only for the wet fraction); a ground energy balance (with soil resistance to evaporation and heat conduction into the soil column); a canopy-air-space budget that connects leaf and ground fluxes to the atmosphere through an aerodynamic resistance from Monin–Obukhov similarity theory, iterated because stability depends on H; and the photosynthesis–stomatal model (FvCB + Ball–Berry or Medlyn) that gives gs as a function of A, Ca and leaf-to-air D, where D depends on the leaf temperature being solved for. The solution is by Newton or fixed-point iteration on Tl (and Tcanopy-air, Tground). Transpiration is the sum over leaf classes of gv(es(Tl) − ecanopy)/P; soil water uptake is distributed over layers by root fraction and stress. Linearising this system about air temperature recovers Penman–Monteith with an effective rs = 1/ΣGc and an ra that includes the within-canopy path, so the closed form is a good diagnostic (e.g. for Ω) but is not used because: the model needs leaf temperature itself (for photosynthesis, respiration, D, snow and interception physics); there are multiple sources at different heights with different resistances; the stomatal conductance depends on the solution; the linearisation error matters for hot, dry surfaces; and the fluxes must be consistent with the atmospheric model's stability iteration. Crop and ecosystem models that lack an atmospheric coupling (DSSAT, DayCent) use the closed form or Priestley–Taylor instead.

7. How is evapotranspiration partitioned into transpiration, soil evaporation and interception, what is the current estimate of T/ET globally, and why does it matter for carbon-cycle modelling?

Methods: stable isotopes (transpiration returns soil water unfractionated at steady state, whereas evaporation enriches the residual in ¹⁸O and ²H, so the isotopic composition of vapour, stream or lake water constrains the partition); sap-flow measurements scaled by sapwood area to stand T, compared with eddy-covariance ET; lysimeters and chambers for soil evaporation; interception from throughfall and stemflow gauges; and flux-based methods using water-use efficiency (GPP × √D/ET relations at times when soil evaporation is negligible, extrapolated). Jasechko et al. (2013) used lake-catchment isotope balances to argue T/ET ≈ 0.8–0.9, but reanalysis and the Schlesinger and Jasechko (2014) synthesis of 81 studies gave 61 ± 15%, with tropical forests ≈ 70%, temperate forests and grasslands 55–65%, croplands and deserts lower, and interception 10–25% in wet forests. Most land models at the time simulated T/ET of 0.4–0.5, too low, because of excessive soil evaporation (too little litter/soil resistance), too-low LAI or canopy conductance, or too much interception loss. It matters because only T is coupled to CO₂ uptake: the response of ET to rising CO₂ (stomatal closure), to greening (LAI) and to VPD is a response of T, while soil evaporation responds to surface wetness and radiation. A model with too little T will under-estimate the CO₂ and VPD sensitivity of the water cycle and mis-attribute runoff trends, and water-use efficiency (GPP/T) derived from tower data depends directly on the partition assumed.
