Logistic Regression

The workhorse model for a yes/no outcome against continuous drivers: species present or absent, tree alive or dead, pixel forest or not. A generalised linear model with a Bernoulli likelihood and a logit link, fitted by Newton's method, read through odds ratios, and judged by ROC curves and calibration.

The one-paragraph version

A binary outcome \(y_i \in \{0,1\}\) is modelled as a Bernoulli trial whose success probability \(p_i\) depends on covariates through the logit link: \(\log\frac{p_i}{1-p_i} = \mathbf{x}_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta\), equivalently \(p_i = \sigma(\mathbf{x}_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta)\) with the sigmoid \(\sigma(z) = 1/(1+e^{-z})\). The log-odds are linear in the predictors, so each coefficient is a log odds ratio: a one-unit increase in \(x_j\) multiplies the odds by \(e^{\beta_j}\), whatever the other covariates. The log-likelihood is concave with gradient \(\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{p})\) and Hessian \(-\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}\); it has no closed-form maximiser, so it is fitted by Newton–Raphson, which for a GLM is iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS). The model is a GLM with the canonical link of the Bernoulli family, a one-layer neural network with sigmoid output, and a linear classifier whose decision boundary \(\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta = 0\) is a hyperplane. Its quality is judged on discrimination (ROC, AUC), calibration (reliability, Brier score), and likelihood (deviance, log-loss, AIC), never on accuracy alone. In ecology it underlies species distribution models, mortality and fire-occurrence models, and remote-sensing classification.

Examiner's lens

Three things get asked almost every time: derive the gradient and Hessian from the Bernoulli log-likelihood and explain why the MLE is unique; interpret a coefficient correctly (odds, not probability); and explain why accuracy is a bad metric for a rare event such as tree death and what to use instead. The fourth, for a modelling student, is how a statistical presence/absence model relates to a process model of the same phenomenon.

Why not ordinary least squares?

Suppose \(y_i\) is 1 if a seedling survived a drought and 0 otherwise, and \(x_i\) is soil water. Regressing \(y\) on \(x\) by OLS fits \(E[y\mid x] = p(x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\). Three things go wrong. First, a line is unbounded, but \(p\) must lie in \([0,1]\); the fit predicts probabilities below zero for dry sites and above one for wet ones. Second, a Bernoulli variable has variance \(p(1-p)\), which depends on the mean, so the residuals are heteroscedastic by construction and the OLS standard errors are wrong. Third, the errors cannot be normal: for a given \(x\) the residual takes only the two values \(1-p\) and \(-p\). Linear probability models still appear in economics as a rough approximation near \(p \approx 0.5\), but for ecological thresholds, which are the interesting part, they fail exactly where they are needed.

The fix is to model the probability on a scale where a straight line makes sense. Any monotone map from \(\mathbb{R}\) to \((0,1)\) will do; the logit is the one with the cleanest interpretation and the cleanest mathematics.

The model: Bernoulli likelihood, logit link, odds

Likelihood

For independent observations with \(y_i \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_i)\),

$$L(\boldsymbol\beta) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p_i^{\,y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i}, \qquad \ell(\boldsymbol\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[y_i\log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\Big].$$

With \(p_i = \sigma(\eta_i)\), \(\eta_i = \mathbf{x}_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta\), the log-likelihood simplifies because \(\log\frac{p_i}{1-p_i} = \eta_i\) and \(\log(1-p_i) = -\log(1+e^{\eta_i})\):

$$\ell(\boldsymbol\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[y_i\,\eta_i - \log\!\big(1+e^{\eta_i}\big)\Big].$$

The negative of this, divided by \(n\), is the log-loss or binary cross-entropy used throughout machine learning. The two names describe one object.

Logit, sigmoid, odds and odds ratios

The odds of an event are \(p/(1-p)\): a probability of 0.8 is odds of 4 to 1. The logit is the log of the odds, and the sigmoid is its inverse:

$$\operatorname{logit}(p) = \log\frac{p}{1-p}, \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} = \frac{e^{z}}{1+e^{z}},$$
$$\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\big(1-\sigma(z)\big), \qquad 1-\sigma(z) = \sigma(-z).$$

Because \(\log\mathrm{odds} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots\), increasing \(x_j\) by one unit adds \(\beta_j\) to the log-odds and therefore multiplies the odds by \(e^{\beta_j}\), the odds ratio. This holds at any baseline and for any values of the other covariates, which is what makes the logit the natural scale to report on. The effect on the probability is not constant: the marginal effect is

$$\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_j} = \beta_j\,p\,(1-p),$$

largest at \(p = 0.5\) (where it equals \(\beta_j/4\), the "divide by four" rule) and vanishing in the tails. A coefficient of \(\beta = 0.7\) on VPD (per kPa) means an odds ratio of \(e^{0.7} \approx 2.0\): each extra kPa doubles the odds of mortality. If the baseline mortality is 2%, doubling the odds moves it to about 3.9%; if the baseline is 50%, to 67%. Report odds ratios with confidence intervals \(\exp(\hat\beta \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SE})\), which are asymmetric on the probability scale.

Logistic regression as a GLM

A GLM has three parts: a response distribution from the exponential family, a linear predictor \(\eta = \mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta\), and a link \(g(\mu) = \eta\). The Bernoulli density in exponential-family form is

$$f(y;\theta) = \exp\!\big[y\theta - \log(1+e^{\theta})\big], \qquad \theta = \log\frac{p}{1-p},$$
$$b(\theta) = \log(1+e^\theta),\qquad b'(\theta) = p = \mu,\qquad b''(\theta) = p(1-p) = \operatorname{Var}(y).$$

The natural parameter is the logit, so the logit is the canonical link. Canonical links make the score equations \(\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\mu) = \mathbf{0}\) especially simple, make the observed and expected information coincide, and make the log-likelihood concave in \(\boldsymbol\beta\). Two common alternatives are the probit, \(p = \Phi(\eta)\), which arises from a latent normal variable crossing a threshold and gives coefficients about \(1/1.6\) to \(1/1.8\) the size of logit ones with almost identical fitted probabilities; and the complementary log-log, \(p = 1-\exp(-e^{\eta})\), which is asymmetric and arises when the event is "at least one occurrence" of a Poisson process, hence natural for mortality over an interval or for presence as at least one individual. The cloglog is also the link that makes a binary model consistent with a continuous-time hazard, which matters when census intervals differ between plots.

Fitting: gradient, Hessian, Newton–Raphson and IRLS

Score and Hessian

Differentiate \(\ell = \sum_i [y_i\eta_i - \log(1+e^{\eta_i})]\) with respect to \(\boldsymbol\beta\), using \(\partial\eta_i/\partial\boldsymbol\beta = \mathbf{x}_i\) and \(\frac{d}{d\eta}\log(1+e^{\eta}) = \sigma(\eta) = p\):

$$\nabla\ell = \sum_{i}(y_i - p_i)\,\mathbf{x}_i = \mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{p}).$$

Differentiating again, \(\partial p_i/\partial\boldsymbol\beta = p_i(1-p_i)\mathbf{x}_i\), so

$$\mathbf{H} = \nabla^2\ell = -\sum_i p_i(1-p_i)\,\mathbf{x}_i\mathbf{x}_i^\top = -\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}, \qquad \mathbf{W} = \operatorname{diag}\big(p_i(1-p_i)\big).$$

Since every weight \(p_i(1-p_i) > 0\), \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}\) is positive definite whenever \(\mathbf{X}\) has full column rank, so \(\mathbf{H}\) is negative definite and \(\ell\) is strictly concave. Any stationary point is the unique global maximum. The score equations \(\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{p}) = \mathbf{0}\) are nonlinear in \(\boldsymbol\beta\) (because \(\mathbf{p}\) is), so there is no closed form, unlike OLS. Note also that for the canonical link the Hessian does not involve \(y\), so observed and expected (Fisher) information coincide: Newton–Raphson and Fisher scoring are the same algorithm here. The asymptotic covariance of \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) is \((\mathbf{X}^\top\hat{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\), and Wald standard errors are the square roots of its diagonal.

Newton–Raphson is iteratively reweighted least squares

The Newton update \(\boldsymbol\beta^{(t+1)} = \boldsymbol\beta^{(t)} - \mathbf{H}^{-1}\nabla\ell\) becomes

$$\boldsymbol\beta^{(t+1)} = \boldsymbol\beta^{(t)} + (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{p}) = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{W}\,\mathbf{z},$$

where the working response is \(\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta^{(t)} + \mathbf{W}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{p})\), i.e. \(z_i = \eta_i + \dfrac{y_i - p_i}{p_i(1-p_i)}\). To see the identity, multiply out: \((\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta^{(t)} = \boldsymbol\beta^{(t)}\) and \(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{-1} = \mathbf{I}\). Each Newton step is therefore a weighted least-squares regression of \(\mathbf{z}\) on \(\mathbf{X}\) with weights \(\mathbf{W}\): a first-order Taylor expansion of the link around the current fit, \(g(y_i) \approx g(p_i) + g'(p_i)(y_i-p_i)\) with \(g'(p) = 1/[p(1-p)]\). Convergence is quadratic and usually takes 4–8 iterations from \(\boldsymbol\beta = \mathbf{0}\). This is how glm() works, and the general GLM case is on the Newton–Raphson page.

Gradient descent instead takes \(\boldsymbol\beta \leftarrow \boldsymbol\beta + \alpha\,\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{p})\), avoiding the \(O(k^3)\) solve of a \(k\times k\) system but needing many more iterations and a step size. It is the method of choice when \(n\) or \(k\) is large or when the model is one layer of something bigger (see the gradient descent page). Because \(\ell\) is concave and its gradient is Lipschitz (each \(\sigma'\le 1/4\), so \(\alpha < 4/\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})\) is safe), plain gradient descent converges to the same unique optimum.

Separation

If some hyperplane perfectly separates the ones from the zeros (complete separation), the likelihood can be increased without bound by scaling \(\boldsymbol\beta\) along the separating direction: \(p_i \to 1\) for every \(y_i = 1\) and \(\to 0\) for every \(y_i = 0\), so \(\ell \to 0\) and the MLE does not exist (Albert & Anderson 1984 [9]). Quasi-complete separation (a hyperplane separates with ties on the boundary) behaves the same way for some coefficients. Software reports huge coefficients, enormous standard errors and a warning about fitted probabilities of 0 or 1. It is common in ecology: a species that is never found above 1,800 m, a categorical predictor with a level that has no deaths, or a small dataset with many predictors. The remedies are Firth's penalised likelihood (below), a Bayesian weakly informative prior, or recognising that the covariate is a deterministic rule rather than a probabilistic effect.

Firth's correction (Firth 1993 [8]) maximises \(\ell^*(\boldsymbol\beta) = \ell(\boldsymbol\beta) + \tfrac12\log\det\mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol\beta)\), where \(\mathbf{I} = \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}\). The Jeffreys-prior penalty removes the first-order bias of the MLE, which in small samples is away from zero, and always gives finite estimates. The modified score is \(\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y} + \mathbf{h}/2 - \mathbf{p}(1+\mathbf{h}))\) with \(h_i\) the leverages of the weighted regression, so it can be fitted with a tiny change to IRLS: each observation is split into fractional pseudo-observations that pull toward \(p = 0.5\).

Regularisation and the multinomial extension

Penalised logistic regression

Adding a penalty to the negative log-likelihood shrinks coefficients, stabilises the fit under separation or collinearity, and trades a little bias for a lot of variance (see the bias–variance page):

$$\min_{\boldsymbol\beta}\; -\ell(\boldsymbol\beta) + \lambda\,\|\boldsymbol\beta_{-0}\|_2^2 \;\;\text{(ridge, L2)}, \qquad \min_{\boldsymbol\beta}\; -\ell(\boldsymbol\beta) + \lambda\,\|\boldsymbol\beta_{-0}\|_1 \;\;\text{(lasso, L1)}.$$

The intercept is not penalised, and predictors must be standardised first or the penalty depends on units. L2 keeps every coefficient but shrinks them (and makes the Hessian \(-\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X} - 2\lambda\mathbf{I}\) strictly negative definite even under separation, so a finite optimum always exists); L1 sets some exactly to zero and so selects variables. Both have a Bayesian (MAP) interpretation: minimising \(-\ell + \lambda\|\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2\) is maximising the posterior under a Gaussian prior \(\beta_j \sim N(0, 1/(2\lambda))\), and the L1 penalty corresponds to a Laplace prior. Choose \(\lambda\) by cross-validated log-loss or deviance, not by accuracy, and in ecology use blocked rather than random folds when the data are spatially or temporally clustered.

Multinomial (softmax) regression and cross-entropy

With \(K\) classes (for example land-cover types from spectral bands), give each class its own linear predictor \(\eta_k = \mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta_k\) and use the softmax

$$p_k = \frac{e^{\eta_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{\eta_j}}, \qquad \ell = \sum_{i}\sum_{k} y_{ik}\log p_{ik}, \qquad -\ell/n = \text{categorical cross-entropy},$$

where \(y_{ik}\) is a one-hot indicator. The parameters are identified only up to a shared constant added to every \(\boldsymbol\beta_k\), so one class is fixed as the reference (\(\boldsymbol\beta_K = \mathbf{0}\)) and the remaining coefficients are log odds ratios relative to it; for \(K = 2\) this is exactly binary logistic regression. To derive the gradient, note \(\partial p_k/\partial\eta_j = p_k(\delta_{kj} - p_j)\), so

$$\frac{\partial\ell_i}{\partial\eta_{ij}} = \sum_k y_{ik}\,\frac{1}{p_{ik}}\,p_{ik}(\delta_{kj}-p_{ij}) = y_{ij} - p_{ij}\sum_k y_{ik} = y_{ij} - p_{ij},$$
$$\nabla_{\boldsymbol\beta_j}\ell = \mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y}_j - \mathbf{p}_j),$$

the same "observed minus expected" form as the binary case, which is why softmax with cross-entropy is the standard output layer of a classification network: the gradient is clean and never saturates the way a squared-error loss on a sigmoid does. The Hessian blocks are \(-\mathbf{X}^\top\operatorname{diag}(p_j(\delta_{jk}-p_k))\mathbf{X}\), again negative semidefinite, so the multinomial likelihood is concave too.

Evaluating a probabilistic classifier

A logistic model produces probabilities; a decision rule turns them into classes by thresholding at some \(t\). Keep the two separate. Discrimination asks whether cases get higher \(p\) than non-cases; calibration asks whether \(p = 0.3\) really happens 30% of the time; a proper scoring rule rewards both at once (Steyerberg et al. 2010 [18]).

Confusion matrix and threshold-dependent metrics

At threshold \(t\), predicted positives are \(\hat p_i \ge t\). The 2×2 table of true positives (TP), false positives (FP), false negatives (FN) and true negatives (TN) gives

$$\text{accuracy} = \frac{TP+TN}{n},\qquad \text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP},\qquad \text{recall (sensitivity, TPR)} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN},$$
$$\text{specificity} = \frac{TN}{TN+FP},\qquad F_1 = \frac{2\,\text{precision}\cdot\text{recall}}{\text{precision}+\text{recall}}.$$

Accuracy fails under class imbalance. If 3% of trees die in a census, the model "nobody dies" is 97% accurate and useless. Precision and recall ignore TN and so are informative for rare positives; \(F_1\) is their harmonic mean. Sensitivity and specificity condition on the true class and so do not depend on prevalence; precision does. In species distribution modelling the same quantities appear as the true skill statistic (TSS = sensitivity + specificity − 1) and Cohen's kappa.

ROC curve and AUC

Sweeping \(t\) from 1 to 0 traces the receiver operating characteristic: TPR against FPR (= 1 − specificity). A random classifier gives the diagonal; a perfect one goes through (0,1). The area under the curve has a direct probabilistic meaning (Hanley & McNeil 1982 [11]): it is the probability that a randomly chosen positive receives a higher score than a randomly chosen negative,

$$\mathrm{AUC} = \Pr(\hat p_{i} > \hat p_{j} \mid y_i = 1, y_j = 0) = \frac{1}{n_1 n_0}\sum_{i:y_i=1}\sum_{j:y_j=0}\mathbb{1}[\hat p_i > \hat p_j] \;(+\tfrac12\,\text{ties}),$$

i.e. the Mann–Whitney U statistic scaled to [0,1]. AUC is invariant to any monotone transformation of the scores, so it measures ranking only: a model can have AUC 0.95 and be badly calibrated. Rules of thumb: 0.7 acceptable, 0.8 good, 0.9 excellent; in SDMs AUC inflates as the modelled range shrinks relative to the background, so compare models only on the same extent (Fawcett 2006 [13]; Elith & Leathwick 2009 [14]). For rare events the precision–recall curve is more informative than ROC because it does not reward the easy true negatives.

Calibration and proper scores

A reliability diagram bins predictions by \(\hat p\) and plots the observed event fraction in each bin against the mean predicted probability; a calibrated model lies on the 1:1 line. Summaries: the calibration intercept and slope from regressing \(y\) on logit(\(\hat p\)) (slope < 1 means overconfident, typical of an overfitted model), and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, \(\chi^2 = \sum_{g=1}^{G}\frac{(O_g - E_g)^2}{E_g(1-E_g/n_g)}\) over \(G \approx 10\) groups, which is widely used and widely criticised for depending on the grouping and for low power. The Brier score, \(\frac1n\sum(\hat p_i - y_i)^2\), and the log-loss, \(-\frac1n\sum[y_i\log\hat p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat p_i)]\), are both strictly proper: they are minimised in expectation only by the true probabilities, so they cannot be gamed by hedging. The Brier score decomposes as reliability − resolution + uncertainty (Murphy), separating calibration from discrimination. Log-loss punishes confident errors without bound; the Brier score caps the penalty at 1.

Choosing a threshold

If a false negative costs \(c_{FN}\) and a false positive \(c_{FP}\), predicting "positive" minimises expected cost when \(p\,c_{FN} > (1-p)\,c_{FP}\), i.e. at

$$t^* = \frac{c_{FP}}{c_{FP}+c_{FN}}.$$

Missing a tree that will die (and so not thinning around it) is usually costlier than a false alarm, so \(t^*\) is well below 0.5 for rare events. Other common choices, the threshold maximising TSS or Youden's \(J\), or the one matching predicted to observed prevalence, are implicit cost assumptions and should be stated as such. Setting \(t = 0.5\) is a choice, not a default.

Deviance, likelihood-ratio tests, pseudo-\(R^2\), AIC

The deviance is twice the log-likelihood gap to the saturated model; for binary data the saturated log-likelihood is 0, so \(D = -2\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})\). Nested models are compared by the likelihood-ratio test: \(D_0 - D_1 = 2(\ell_1 - \ell_0) \sim \chi^2_{k_1 - k_0}\) under the smaller model. With ungrouped binary data the residual deviance itself is not a goodness-of-fit statistic (it does not follow a \(\chi^2\) distribution); it is only useful for comparisons. The null deviance \(D_{\text{null}}\) comes from the intercept-only model, \(\hat p = \bar y\), and McFadden's pseudo-\(R^2\) is \(1 - \ell_1/\ell_0 = 1 - D_1/D_{\text{null}}\); values of 0.2–0.4 are considered excellent, so do not read it as an OLS \(R^2\). Tjur's \(R^2\), the mean \(\hat p\) among ones minus the mean among zeros, is a more intuitive alternative. For non-nested comparison use \(\mathrm{AIC} = D + 2k\). Wald tests (\(\hat\beta/\mathrm{SE}\)) are quicker than LRTs but misbehave near separation, where the SE explodes and the Wald statistic shrinks to zero even though the effect is huge (the Hauck–Donner effect).

Residuals, assumptions and diagnostics

Raw residuals \(y_i - \hat p_i\) are useless to plot: they form two bands. Use Pearson residuals \((y_i-\hat p_i)/\sqrt{\hat p_i(1-\hat p_i)}\), deviance residuals \(\operatorname{sign}(y_i-\hat p_i)\sqrt{d_i}\) with \(d_i = -2[y_i\log\hat p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat p_i)]\), binned residuals (average residual in bins of \(\hat p\) or of a covariate, with ±2 SE bands; Gelman & Hill), or simulation-based quantile residuals as in the DHARMa package (Hartig 2022 [17]): simulate new \(y\) from the fitted model many times, find where each observation falls in its simulated distribution, and plot the resulting quantiles, which should be uniform for a correctly specified model and which work equally well for binomial, Poisson and mixed models. The assumptions are: independent observations given covariates (violated by spatial clustering, repeated plots, nested designs; fix with mixed models or GEE); correct link and linearity in the logit (check with partial residuals, splines or a Box–Tidwell test; fix with polynomials or a GAM, \(\operatorname{logit}p = \beta_0 + f_1(x_1) + f_2(x_2)\)); no influential points (Cook's distance, leverage from the IRLS hat matrix); no severe multicollinearity (variance inflation factors; inflates SEs and makes odds ratios uninterpretable one at a time). Interactions are included as products on the logit scale, and their odds ratios are ratios of odds ratios; on the probability scale an additive logit already implies interaction, so test interactions on the scale on which the model is linear.

Geometry, neural-network view and comparisons

The decision boundary is linear. Predicting the class with \(p \ge 0.5\) is predicting 1 when \(\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta \ge 0\): a hyperplane in feature space, with \(\boldsymbol\beta\) its normal vector and \(|\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta|/\|\boldsymbol\beta\|\) the distance from it. Curved boundaries come only from engineered features (polynomials, splines, interactions) or from nonlinear models. Changing the threshold shifts the hyperplane parallel to itself. Logistic regression is a one-layer neural network: a single unit with sigmoid activation trained with cross-entropy and (stochastic) gradient descent is exactly this model, and softmax regression is the output layer of every classification network; what deep networks add is a learned nonlinear feature map in front of it. The backpropagation gradient of that final layer is \((\hat p - y)\,\mathbf{h}\), with \(\mathbf{h}\) the hidden features, the same "observed minus expected" as above.

Ecological applications

Species distribution models. Presence/absence surveys against climate, soil and topography are the canonical use (Guisan & Zimmermann 2000 [16]; Elith & Leathwick 2009 [14]), usually as a logistic GAM or GLM with quadratic terms so that responses can be unimodal in temperature. Presence-only data (herbarium records, GBIF) have no zeros; MaxEnt (Phillips et al. 2006 [15]) fits them by contrasting presences with background points. Renner & Warton (2013 [6]) showed that MaxEnt is equivalent to an inhomogeneous Poisson point process model, and that logistic regression of presences against background points estimates the same slope coefficients up to an intercept that depends on the number of background points; the intercept, and hence absolute probabilities, are not identifiable from presence-only data. That is why MaxEnt's "suitability" output should not be read as a probability of presence.

Tree mortality. Annual mortality of individual trees in inventory plots is modelled as \(\operatorname{logit}p = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{DBH} + \beta_2\,\text{growth} + \beta_3\,\text{drought index} + \dots\), often with a cloglog link and an offset of log census interval so that unequal intervals are handled consistently. The random-forest page compares this with tree-based mortality models. Germination and phenology thresholds: the probability that a seed has germinated, or a bud has burst, by thermal time \(x\) is a sigmoid in \(x\); the logistic fit gives the median threshold \(-\beta_0/\beta_1\) and the spread \(1/\beta_1\), a dose–response problem (the context in which Berkson [2] introduced the logit in 1944). Fire occurrence: ignition probability per cell per day vs fuel moisture, wind, lightning and human access, with heavy class imbalance (most cells never burn), where case–control sampling of non-fire days with an intercept correction \(\beta_0 + \log(\pi_1/\pi_0)\) is standard. Land-cover classification: multinomial logistic regression on spectral bands and indices is the baseline against which random forests and neural networks are compared; it loses on accuracy but gives calibrated class probabilities.

Sample size. The rule of thumb from Peduzzi et al. (1996 [12]) is at least 10 events (observations in the rarer class) per candidate predictor; with fewer, coefficients are biased away from zero, confidence intervals are unreliable and separation becomes likely. Thirty dead trees support about three predictors, not thirty. Later work (van Smeden et al. 2016) argues the rule is crude and depends on prevalence and effect size, but it remains the examiner's expected answer.

Connections to ecosystem modelling

  • Mortality in vegetation demographic models. ED2, FATES and LPJ-GUESS use annual mortality probabilities that are logistic functions of carbon starvation, hydraulic stress or growth efficiency; the empirical logistic fits from inventories are what these functions are calibrated against, and the cloglog/hazard view links an annual probability to the daily time step.
  • Phenology and fire modules. Budburst and leaf-fall probabilities in CLM and ORCHIDEE, and ignition probability in SPITFIRE, are sigmoid functions of accumulated temperature or fuel moisture: logistic regressions with a mechanistic story attached.
  • Statistical vs process SDMs. A logistic SDM predicts occurrence from climate; a process model predicts it from carbon balance. Both are compared on AUC and calibration, and hybrid approaches use process-model outputs (GPP, water stress) as covariates in the logistic model.
  • Emulation and classification of model behaviour. Logistic regression on parameter samples classifies which combinations make a model crash, exceed a threshold or switch state (forest to grassland), giving an interpretable sensitivity analysis of a binary outcome.
  • Remote-sensing products that feed models. Land-cover maps, burned-area masks and disturbance flags are classifier outputs; their per-pixel probabilities, where provided, propagate uncertainty into model forcing.
  • Likelihood for binary observations in model–data fusion. When assimilating presence/absence or alive/dead observations into a process model, the Bernoulli log-likelihood of this page is the data-model term in the Bayesian calibration, with the process model providing \(p\).

Exam-style questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer.

1. Starting from the Bernoulli likelihood, derive the gradient and Hessian of the logistic log-likelihood and show the MLE is unique when it exists. Why is there no closed-form solution?

With \(p_i = \sigma(\mathbf{x}_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta)\), \(\ell = \sum[y_i\log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)] = \sum[y_i\eta_i - \log(1+e^{\eta_i})]\) because \(\log(p/(1-p)) = \eta\) and \(\log(1-p) = -\log(1+e^\eta)\). Chain rule: \(\partial\ell/\partial\boldsymbol\beta = \sum(y_i - \sigma(\eta_i))\mathbf{x}_i = \mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{p})\), using \(d\log(1+e^\eta)/d\eta = \sigma(\eta)\). Differentiating once more with \(\partial\sigma(\eta_i)/\partial\boldsymbol\beta = \sigma(1-\sigma)\mathbf{x}_i\) gives \(\mathbf{H} = -\sum p_i(1-p_i)\mathbf{x}_i\mathbf{x}_i^\top = -\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}\). For any \(\mathbf{v} \ne 0\), \(\mathbf{v}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v} = \sum p_i(1-p_i)(\mathbf{x}_i^\top\mathbf{v})^2 > 0\) if \(\mathbf{X}\) has full column rank, so \(\mathbf{H}\) is negative definite everywhere, \(\ell\) is strictly concave, and a stationary point is the unique global maximum. Setting the gradient to zero, \(\sum(y_i - \sigma(\mathbf{x}_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta))\mathbf{x}_i = 0\), is a system of nonlinear equations in \(\boldsymbol\beta\) because \(\sigma\) is nonlinear; in OLS the analogous equations \(\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta) = 0\) are linear and solvable. The maximum may fail to exist (separation), in which case the likelihood increases monotonically along a direction and the estimates diverge.

2. Show that Newton–Raphson for logistic regression is an iteratively reweighted least-squares algorithm, and define the working response.

Newton: \(\boldsymbol\beta^{+} = \boldsymbol\beta - \mathbf{H}^{-1}\nabla\ell = \boldsymbol\beta + (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{p})\). Write \(\boldsymbol\beta = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta\) and \(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{p})\); factor: \(\boldsymbol\beta^{+} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{W}[\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta + \mathbf{W}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{p})] = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{z}\). This is the weighted least-squares estimator for regressing the working response \(z_i = \eta_i + (y_i-p_i)/[p_i(1-p_i)]\) on \(\mathbf{X}\) with weights \(w_i = p_i(1-p_i)\). Interpretation: \(z_i\) is the first-order Taylor expansion of the link applied to \(y_i\) around \(p_i\), \(g(y_i) \approx g(p_i) + g'(p_i)(y_i-p_i)\) with \(g' = 1/[p(1-p)]\), so each step linearises the model and solves the resulting weighted linear regression. Weights and working response are recomputed from the new \(\boldsymbol\beta\) and the process repeats until the deviance changes by less than a tolerance; convergence is quadratic. For the canonical link observed and expected information coincide, so this is also Fisher scoring. Starting values \(\boldsymbol\beta = 0\) (so \(p_i = 0.5\), \(w_i = 0.25\), \(z_i = 4(y_i - 0.5)\)) work for logistic regression.

3. A mortality model gives β = 0.9 for a drought index (per unit). Interpret it on the odds and probability scales for a tree with baseline mortality 2% and one with 40%. Why do ecologists misreport odds ratios as relative risks?

Odds ratio \(e^{0.9} \approx 2.46\): a one-unit increase in the index multiplies the odds of dying by 2.46 regardless of other covariates. At 2% baseline the odds are 0.0204; new odds 0.0502; new probability \(0.0502/1.0502 = 4.8\%\), a 2.8-percentage-point increase (relative risk 2.4, close to the OR). At 40% baseline the odds are 0.667; new odds 1.64; new probability 62%, a 22-point increase but a relative risk of only 1.55. The marginal-effect approximation \(\beta p(1-p)\) gives 0.018 and 0.216, reasonable for small changes. Odds ratios approximate relative risks only when the outcome is rare (\(p \lesssim 0.1\)); for common outcomes the OR is always further from 1 than the RR, so reading "OR = 2.5" as "2.5 times as likely to die" overstates the effect when mortality is high. Odds ratios should be reported with \(\exp(\hat\beta \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SE})\) intervals, and predicted probabilities at representative covariate values should accompany them.

4. Only 3% of trees in your inventory died. Your logistic model is 97% accurate. Is it good? What would you report instead and how would you choose a threshold?

Not necessarily: predicting "alive" for every tree also gives 97% accuracy. Accuracy is dominated by the majority class. Report threshold-free measures: AUC (probability a dead tree is ranked above a live one), ideally with the precision–recall curve because the rare positives are what matter; and calibration (reliability diagram, Brier score relative to the baseline \(\bar y(1-\bar y) = 0.029\), log-loss). For a decision, state the costs. If missing a tree that will die is 5 times as costly as a false alarm, the Bayes-optimal threshold is \(t^* = c_{FP}/(c_{FP}+c_{FN}) = 1/6 \approx 0.17\), far below 0.5; at 0.5 almost no tree would be predicted to die and recall would be near zero. Then report sensitivity, specificity, precision and \(F_1\) (or TSS) at that threshold, and the confusion matrix. Also check events per variable: 3% of, say, 1,000 trees is 30 deaths, which supports about three predictors; use penalisation or Firth's correction otherwise. Evaluate on held-out plots (spatial blocking), not on the training trees.

5. What is complete separation, why does it break maximum likelihood, and how does Firth's method or ridge regularisation fix it?

Complete separation: there exists \(\boldsymbol\beta^*\) with \(\mathbf{x}_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta^* > 0\) for all \(y_i = 1\) and \(< 0\) for all \(y_i = 0\). Then along \(c\boldsymbol\beta^*\), as \(c \to \infty\), every \(p_i \to y_i\) and \(\ell \to 0\), its supremum; the likelihood has no maximum, estimates diverge, and Wald SEs become infinite. Quasi-complete separation (some points on the boundary) gives the same failure for a subset of coefficients. It arises from small samples, many predictors, categorical predictors with empty cells, or genuinely deterministic thresholds. Firth's penalised likelihood adds \(\tfrac12\log\det(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X})\) (the Jeffreys prior). As \(\|\boldsymbol\beta\| \to \infty\) the weights \(p_i(1-p_i) \to 0\) and the penalty \(\to -\infty\) faster than \(\ell\) approaches 0, so a finite maximiser always exists; the correction also removes the \(O(1/n)\) bias of the MLE. Ridge adds \(-\lambda\|\boldsymbol\beta\|^2\), which likewise dominates at large \(\|\boldsymbol\beta\|\) and makes the Hessian \(-\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X} - 2\lambda\mathbf{I}\) strictly negative definite, guaranteeing a unique finite solution, equivalent to a Gaussian prior. Both shrink toward zero; neither recovers an "effect size" for a deterministic rule, which should be reported as such.

6. Derive the gradient of the softmax cross-entropy loss with respect to the linear predictors, and explain why logistic regression is a one-layer neural network.

With \(p_k = e^{\eta_k}/\sum_j e^{\eta_j}\), \(\partial p_k/\partial\eta_j = p_k(\delta_{kj} - p_j)\) (differentiate the quotient: for \(k = j\) get \(p_k - p_k^2\); for \(k \ne j\) get \(-p_kp_j\)). The per-observation loss is \(L = -\sum_k y_k\log p_k\), so \(\partial L/\partial\eta_j = -\sum_k (y_k/p_k)\,p_k(\delta_{kj}-p_j) = -y_j + p_j\sum_k y_k = p_j - y_j\) since the one-hot \(y\) sums to 1. Summed over observations and chained through \(\eta_j = \mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta_j\), \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol\beta_j}L = \mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{p}_j - \mathbf{y}_j)\). For \(K = 2\) with \(\boldsymbol\beta_2 = 0\) this collapses to the binary gradient \(\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{y})\). A neural network's output unit computes \(\sigma(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{h} + b)\) and is trained by gradient descent on cross-entropy; with \(\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{x}\) (no hidden layer) that is exactly logistic regression, and the backpropagated error at the output is \(\hat p - y\). The deep network differs only in learning \(\mathbf{h}\) as a nonlinear function of \(\mathbf{x}\); the final layer is still a (multinomial) logistic regression on learned features. Consequences: the loss surface of the one-layer model is convex, deep models' are not; and the final-layer probabilities of a network are only as calibrated as those of a logistic regression trained on its features.

7. Explain the relationship between MaxEnt, Poisson point-process models and logistic regression for presence-only species data. What can and cannot be estimated?

Presence-only data are locations where a species was recorded, with no absences. MaxEnt models the distribution of presence locations over the landscape as the Gibbs distribution \(\pi(\mathbf{x}) \propto \exp(\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta)\) that maximises entropy subject to matching feature means at presences. Renner & Warton (2013) showed this is the same likelihood as an inhomogeneous Poisson point process with intensity \(\lambda(s) = \exp(\beta_0 + \mathbf{x}(s)^\top\boldsymbol\beta)\), and that logistic regression of presences (1) against background points (0) with enough background points estimates the same slopes \(\boldsymbol\beta\), with an intercept that shifts by \(\log(n_{\text{bg}}/|A|)\). The slopes, and therefore relative suitability and the shape of the response curves, are estimable; the intercept, and therefore absolute probability of presence or expected abundance, is not, because the sampling intensity of presences is unknown. MaxEnt's "logistic output" fixes the intercept by assuming prevalence 0.5 at typical sites, an arbitrary choice. Practical implications: evaluate presence-only models by ranking (AUC against background, which is inflated), state that outputs are relative, and account for sampling bias in presences (target-group background), which the point-process view makes explicit as a thinning of the intensity.

8. Compare logistic regression with LDA and with a random forest for classifying land cover from two spectral bands. When would each be preferred?

Logistic regression models \(P(y\mid\mathbf{x})\) directly with a linear logit; LDA models \(P(\mathbf{x}\mid y)\) as Gaussian with a common covariance and applies Bayes' rule, which yields a posterior of exactly logistic-linear form, so the two have the same boundary family. LDA is more statistically efficient when its Gaussian assumption is true (Efron: logistic loses roughly 30% efficiency), but spectral reflectance within a class is skewed and multimodal, so LDA's assumption fails and logistic regression is more robust; LDA is also pulled by outliers. Both give linear (or quadratic, with QDA or engineered features) boundaries and calibrated probabilities. A random forest partitions the band space into many axis-aligned rectangles, captures interactions and thresholds automatically, usually wins on accuracy for remote-sensing classification, and handles dozens of bands and indices without feature engineering. Its probabilities (vote fractions) are compressed toward the middle and need recalibration, it is a black box relative to odds ratios, and it cannot extrapolate beyond the training spectra. Prefer logistic/multinomial regression when the goal is inference about how reflectance relates to class, when training data are few (10 events per variable), or when calibrated probabilities feed a downstream model; prefer the forest when prediction accuracy is paramount, predictors are many, and the training sample covers the prediction domain.
