k-Means Clustering

The simplest useful answer to "are there groups in these data?": partition points so that the squared distance to their group's mean is as small as possible, and iterate assignment and averaging until nothing moves. Everything else on this page is about what that objective assumes, when it fails, and how ecologists use it.

The one-paragraph version

Unsupervised learning looks for structure in inputs \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) without any labels: groups (clustering), low-dimensional structure (PCA), or the density itself (mixture models). k-means is the workhorse of the first task. Given a user-chosen \(k\), it seeks \(k\) centres \(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_k\) and a hard assignment of every point to one centre that minimise the within-cluster sum of squares (also called distortion or inertia). The global problem is NP-hard, so in practice we run Lloyd's algorithm: assign each point to its nearest centre, replace each centre by the mean of its points, repeat. Each step cannot increase the objective, so the algorithm converges, but only to a local minimum that depends on the starting centres; k-means++ seeding and multiple restarts tame that. The method implicitly assumes clusters are roughly spherical, similar in size and spread, and separable by straight (Voronoi) boundaries in whatever feature space and units you give it. When those assumptions fail, soft, elliptical (Gaussian mixture), density-based (DBSCAN) or hierarchical alternatives exist, and the next two pages (GMM and PCA) are its natural companions.

Examiner's lens

Examiners use k-means to test three things: can you write down an objective function and show that an algorithm decreases it (the Lloyd proof); do you know what a method assumes (spherical, equal-variance clusters; scaled features; a known \(k\)); and can you connect it to probabilistic modelling (k-means is the \(\sigma^2 \to 0\) limit of EM for an isotropic Gaussian mixture). Have one ecological example ready for each: climate classification, plant functional types from traits, and phenology regimes from NDVI time series.

The objective: within-cluster sum of squares

Let \(x_n \in \mathbb{R}^d\) for \(n = 1,\dots,N\) and introduce binary indicators \(r_{nk} \in \{0,1\}\) with \(\sum_k r_{nk} = 1\) (each point belongs to exactly one cluster). k-means minimises the distortion

$$J(\{r_{nk}\},\{\mu_k\}) \;=\; \sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K} r_{nk}\,\lVert x_n - \mu_k\rVert^2 ,$$

the total squared Euclidean distance from every point to the centre of its cluster. Two facts about this objective carry the whole method.

Why the centroid is the right centre

Fix the assignments and consider one cluster \(C_k\) with \(N_k\) points. Its contribution to \(J\) is \(S(\mu) = \sum_{n \in C_k} \lVert x_n - \mu\rVert^2\). Differentiating with respect to \(\mu\) and setting to zero,

$$\frac{\partial S}{\partial \mu} = -2\sum_{n\in C_k}(x_n - \mu) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mu_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_{n\in C_k} x_n = \bar x_k .$$

Because \(S\) is a convex quadratic in \(\mu\) (its Hessian is \(2N_k I\), positive definite) the stationary point is the unique minimiser. An equivalent identity makes the point without calculus: for any \(\mu\),

$$\sum_{n\in C_k}\lVert x_n - \mu\rVert^2 = \sum_{n\in C_k}\lVert x_n - \bar x_k\rVert^2 + N_k\,\lVert \bar x_k - \mu\rVert^2 ,$$

so moving the centre anywhere away from the mean adds a non-negative penalty. The mean is also why k-means is tied to squared Euclidean distance: with absolute distance the minimiser would be the coordinate-wise median (k-medians), and with an arbitrary dissimilarity there is no closed form at all, which is why k-medoids restricts the centre to be one of the data points.

Why the assignment is a Voronoi partition

Now fix the centres. Each term of \(J\) involves one point and the centre it is assigned to, so \(J\) is minimised point by point by choosing \(r_{nk} = 1\) for the nearest centre, \(k = \arg\min_j \lVert x_n - \mu_j\rVert^2\). Geometrically every centre owns the region of space closer to it than to any other centre, its Voronoi cell \(V_k = \{x : \lVert x - \mu_k\rVert \le \lVert x - \mu_j\rVert\ \forall j\}\). The boundary between two cells is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining the centres, a straight line in 2-D and a hyperplane in general. k-means can therefore only ever produce convex, linearly separated clusters; a crescent or ring is unreachable no matter how the algorithm is initialised. The playground below draws these cells so that you can watch them move.

A useful decomposition for the model-selection section: the total sum of squares about the grand mean \(\bar x\) splits exactly into within- and between-cluster parts,

$$\underbrace{\sum_n \lVert x_n - \bar x\rVert^2}_{T} \;=\; \underbrace{\sum_k \sum_{n\in C_k}\lVert x_n - \bar x_k\rVert^2}_{W(k)} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_k N_k \lVert \bar x_k - \bar x\rVert^2}_{B(k)} ,$$

so minimising \(W\) is the same as maximising \(B\), and \(W(k)\) is non-increasing in \(k\) (with \(W(N) = 0\)), which is why "pick the \(k\) with the lowest SSE" is meaningless and why the elbow method exists.

Lloyd's algorithm: assign, average, repeat

The objective is a mixed combinatorial–continuous problem: \(K^N\) possible assignments, each with a closed-form optimal set of centres. Lloyd (1957, published 1982) proposed alternating the two easy sub-problems, and MacQueen (1967) gave the method its name; Steinhaus had stated the problem in 1956. Written out:

initialise μ₁…μ_K (random points, or k-means++) repeat assign: r_nk ← 1 if k = argmin_j ‖x_n − μ_j‖², else 0 update: μ_k ← Σ_n r_nk x_n / Σ_n r_nk until no assignment changes

Monotone decrease and finite convergence

Let \((r^{(t)}, \mu^{(t)})\) be the state after iteration \(t\). The assignment step chooses, for fixed \(\mu^{(t)}\), the assignments minimising \(J\), so \(J(r^{(t+1)}, \mu^{(t)}) \le J(r^{(t)}, \mu^{(t)})\). The update step chooses, for fixed \(r^{(t+1)}\), the centres minimising \(J\) (the centroid derivation above), so \(J(r^{(t+1)}, \mu^{(t+1)}) \le J(r^{(t+1)}, \mu^{(t)})\). Chaining, \(J\) is non-increasing and bounded below by zero. Because there are finitely many partitions and \(J\) strictly decreases whenever an assignment changes (ties broken consistently), the algorithm must stop after finitely many iterations at a state where no point wants to move: a partition-stable local minimum. Nothing in the argument says it is the global minimum, and in practice different starts give different answers on the same data. Theoretical worst cases need exponentially many iterations (Vattani 2011) but real data converge in tens of iterations, and each iteration costs \(O(NKd)\).

Two readings of the same algorithm

Coordinate descent. \(J\) is a function of two blocks of variables, \(r\) and \(\mu\); Lloyd's algorithm minimises exactly over one block while holding the other fixed, i.e. block coordinate descent on a non-convex objective. That is why it converges but not globally.

Expectation–maximisation. Take a mixture of \(K\) isotropic Gaussians with equal weights \(\pi_k = 1/K\) and shared variance \(\sigma^2 I\). The E-step responsibility of component \(k\) for point \(n\) is

$$\gamma_{nk} = \frac{\exp\!\big(-\lVert x_n-\mu_k\rVert^2/2\sigma^2\big)}{\sum_j \exp\!\big(-\lVert x_n-\mu_j\rVert^2/2\sigma^2\big)} \;\xrightarrow{\;\sigma^2\to 0\;}\; r_{nk} \in\{0,1\},$$

because the smallest distance dominates the sum. The expected complete-data log-likelihood becomes \(-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_n\sum_k r_{nk}\lVert x_n-\mu_k\rVert^2 + \text{const} = -J/2\sigma^2 + \text{const}\), so the M-step (maximise over \(\mu\)) is exactly the centroid update. k-means is "hard EM" for a spherical equal-variance mixture, which is simultaneously its justification and a list of its assumptions. The GMM page develops the soft version.

NP-hardness and initialisation

Finding the global minimum of \(J\) is NP-hard even for \(K = 2\) in general dimension (Aloise et al. 2009) and even in the plane for general \(K\) (Mahajan, Nimbhorkar & Varadarajan 2009). Lloyd's algorithm is a heuristic, and its weakest point is the start. Random seeding (MacQueen's choice of \(K\) random data points, or Forgy's) frequently places two seeds in one true cluster and none in another; the algorithm then splits one group and merges two, and no local move can fix that. The standard cure is k-means++ (Arthur & Vassilvitskii 2007):

  1. Choose the first centre uniformly at random from the data.
  2. For every point compute \(D(x)\), the distance to the nearest centre chosen so far.
  3. Choose the next centre from the data with probability proportional to \(D(x)^2\) ("D² sampling").
  4. Repeat until \(K\) centres are chosen, then run Lloyd's algorithm.

D² sampling spreads seeds out (far points are likely) without being deterministic (so a single outlier does not always win). Its theoretical payoff is that the expected distortion of the seeding alone satisfies

$$\mathbb{E}[J_{\text{k-means++}}] \;\le\; 8\,(\ln K + 2)\; J_{\text{opt}},$$

an \(O(\log K)\) approximation guarantee, whereas uniform random seeding has no bound at all. Lloyd iterations afterwards can only improve on it. Practical implementations (scikit-learn, R's kmeans with nstart) also run several independent restarts and keep the lowest \(J\); ten restarts is a common default.

Choosing k

k-means needs \(K\) as an input and will cheerfully partition structureless data into \(K\) pieces (try the uniform scenario above). Choosing \(K\) is therefore a model-selection problem, and the honest answer in an exam is that no single criterion is decisive: use two or three, and let domain knowledge (how many phenology regimes or soil horizons are plausible, and interpretable?) have the last word.

Elbow

Plot \(W(k)\) against \(k\). Adding a centre inside a real cluster buys little, so \(W(k)\) drops steeply until \(k\) equals the number of real groups and then flattens: the "elbow". It is quick and often all you need for well-separated data, but the elbow is frequently a gentle curve rather than a corner, and for nested or hierarchical structure there may be several.

Silhouette (Rousseeuw 1987)

For point \(i\) in cluster \(A\), define the mean distance to its own cluster and the mean distance to the nearest other cluster:

$$a(i) = \frac{1}{|A|-1}\sum_{j\in A,\,j\ne i} d(i,j), \qquad b(i) = \min_{B\ne A}\ \frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{j\in B} d(i,j), \qquad s(i) = \frac{b(i)-a(i)}{\max\{a(i),\,b(i)\}} .$$

Because \(a, b \ge 0\), dividing by the larger of the two bounds \(s(i)\) in \([-1, 1]\): \(s \approx 1\) when the point is much closer to its own cluster than to the next (\(a \ll b\)), \(s \approx 0\) when it sits on a boundary (\(a \approx b\)), and \(s < 0\) when it is on average closer to a neighbouring cluster, i.e. probably misassigned. Singletons are given \(s = 0\) by convention. The mean silhouette \(\bar s(k)\) is maximised over \(k\); Kaufman & Rousseeuw's rule of thumb is that \(\bar s > 0.7\) indicates strong structure, 0.5–0.7 reasonable, 0.25–0.5 weak, and below 0.25 essentially none. The per-point silhouette plot (Lab C) is more informative than the mean: it reveals clusters that are coherent and clusters that are leftovers. Note that the silhouette uses any distance, not only Euclidean, so it also validates k-medoids or hierarchical results; and that it is biased toward compact, well-separated, similarly sized clusters, the same assumptions as k-means itself.

Gap statistic (Tibshirani, Walther & Hastie 2001)

Compare \(\log W(k)\) with its expectation under a null of no cluster structure, estimated by clustering \(B\) reference datasets drawn uniformly over the bounding box (or the principal-component box) of the data:

$$\text{Gap}(k) = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\log W^{*}_{b}(k) - \log W(k), \qquad \hat k = \min\{k : \text{Gap}(k) \ge \text{Gap}(k+1) - s_{k+1}\},$$

where \(s_{k+1}\) is the standard deviation of \(\log W^*_b(k+1)\) scaled by \(\sqrt{1 + 1/B}\). The gap is large where the data drop faster than noise would. Unlike the silhouette it is defined for \(k = 1\), so it can say "there are no clusters" (choose \(k = 1\)), which matters for ecological data where the honest answer is often a continuum.

Calinski–Harabasz (1974) and BIC via a mixture

The CH index is a pseudo-F ratio using the decomposition \(T = W + B\):

$$\text{CH}(k) = \frac{B(k)/(k-1)}{W(k)/(N-k)} ,$$

maximised over \(k \ge 2\). It rewards separation relative to compactness and penalises extra clusters through the degrees of freedom, and it is cheap (no pairwise distances). Finally, because k-means is a limiting Gaussian mixture, one can fit GMMs for a range of \(k\) and choose by BIC, \(-2\log L + p\log N\), which adds a formal penalty for the \(p\) parameters; the GMM page does this interactively. In applied ecology the choice is usually settled by asking whether the \(k\)-th cluster is interpretable (does it correspond to a recognisable biome, a distinct soil horizon, a known phenological strategy?) and whether it survives resampling (see validation below).

Assumptions, failure modes, scale and distance

Everything k-means does follows from minimising squared Euclidean distance to a mean, and each word in that phrase is an assumption.

  • Spherical, equal-variance clusters. The Voronoi boundary between two centres is their perpendicular bisector, which is the Bayes boundary only when the two groups have the same isotropic covariance. A large diffuse cluster next to a small tight one loses its edge to the tight one (Lab A, "Unequal variances"). Elongated clusters are sliced across their long axis.
  • Similar cluster sizes. Because \(J\) sums over points, splitting a large cluster saves more SSE than correctly isolating a small one, so k-means tends toward equal-population clusters. A rare ecoregion or a small set of outlier sites will be absorbed.
  • Convexity. Linear boundaries cannot represent crescents, rings or intertwined manifolds (the two-moons scenario). Density-based (DBSCAN) or spectral methods are required.
  • Sensitivity to outliers. The mean is not robust; a few extreme sites drag a centroid or get a cluster of their own. k-medoids, trimmed k-means, or removing outliers first are the remedies.
  • Scale. Squared Euclidean distance adds variances in each feature's units. A temperature in °C (spread of ~20) and an annual precipitation in mm (spread of ~1500) do not compete fairly: precipitation alone decides the clusters (Lab E). Standardise (z-scores) unless the features share units and the raw scale is the one you mean. Standardising also implicitly weights all features equally, which may be wrong in the other direction; weighting features by expert judgement or using Mahalanobis distance are the alternatives.
  • k is fixed, and there are always clusters. k-means returns \(K\) groups whether or not the data have any; the gap statistic or a GMM with BIC are the tools that can answer "none".

Curse of dimensionality

For independent features the squared distance between two random points is a sum of \(d\) terms, so its mean grows like \(d\) while its standard deviation grows like \(\sqrt d\): the ratio of the farthest to the nearest neighbour tends to 1 as \(d\) grows, and "nearest centre" stops meaning much. Hyperspectral reflectance (hundreds of bands) or long NDVI time series (hundreds of dates) are routinely reduced with PCA first, which also decorrelates the inputs so that redundant bands do not get counted ten times. Whitening (dividing PC scores by their standard deviations) makes the implied metric Mahalanobis, which can help or hurt: it equalises directions, including noise directions.

Relatives and alternatives

Gaussian mixtures (soft k-means)

Replace hard assignments by responsibilities \(\gamma_{nk} \in [0,1]\) and give each component its own weight \(\pi_k\) and covariance \(\Sigma_k\). Boundaries become quadratic, clusters can be elliptical and of unequal size, and every point gets a probability of membership rather than a label. The cost is more parameters (a full 2-D covariance has 3, a \(d\)-dimensional one \(d(d+1)/2\)) and the possibility of a component collapsing onto a single point. The GMM page derives EM in full; Lab D shows the difference for one point.

k-medoids / PAM

Partitioning Around Medoids (Kaufman & Rousseeuw 1990) requires each centre to be an actual observation (the medoid) and minimises the sum of dissimilarities (not squared) to it. It works with any dissimilarity matrix (Bray–Curtis on species abundances, Gower on mixed trait types), is robust to outliers, and returns a representative real site or species per cluster, which ecologists like. It costs \(O(K(N-K)^2)\) per iteration in the original swap algorithm; CLARA and CLARANS subsample for large \(N\).

Mini-batch k-means

For \(N\) in the millions (every pixel of a Landsat scene) use a random mini-batch of \(b\) points per iteration and update each centre toward the batch mean with a per-centre learning rate \(\eta_k = 1/n_k\), where \(n_k\) counts points so far assigned to \(k\): \(\mu_k \leftarrow \mu_k + \eta_k (x - \mu_k)\). This is MacQueen's original online update. The result is a slightly worse \(J\) for a large reduction in time and memory.

Hierarchical (agglomerative) clustering

Start with \(N\) singletons and repeatedly merge the two closest clusters, recording the merge height in a dendrogram; cut the tree at any height to obtain a partition, so \(K\) need not be fixed in advance and nested structure is visible. The linkage defines "closest": single (minimum pairwise distance, chains and finds non-convex shapes but is noise-sensitive), complete (maximum, compact spherical groups), average (UPGMA, the ecologist's default), and Ward's method (1963), which merges the pair giving the smallest increase in total within-cluster SSE,

$$\Delta(A,B) = \frac{N_A N_B}{N_A + N_B}\,\lVert \bar x_A - \bar x_B\rVert^2 ,$$

i.e. the same criterion as k-means, optimised greedily and hierarchically rather than iteratively for a fixed \(K\). A common recipe is Ward to choose \(K\) and seed centres, then k-means to refine. Hierarchical methods cost \(O(N^2)\) memory for the distance matrix, which limits them to tens of thousands of objects.

DBSCAN and spectral clustering

DBSCAN (Ester et al. 1996) defines clusters as connected regions of density: a point is a core point if at least minPts neighbours lie within radius \(\varepsilon\); clusters are chains of core points, border points attach to them, and everything else is noise. It discovers arbitrary shapes, finds \(K\) itself and labels outliers explicitly, but struggles when clusters have different densities and \(\varepsilon\) is hard to choose in high dimension (HDBSCAN relaxes this). Spectral clustering builds a similarity graph, takes the eigenvectors of its (normalised) Laplacian, and runs k-means on the first \(K\) eigenvectors; connected components of the graph become separable in that embedding, so moons and rings are recovered. It is k-means after a nonlinear change of coordinates, which is also a fair description of kernel PCA followed by k-means.

Validation and stability

Internal indices (silhouette, CH, gap) judge geometry; external indices compare two partitions. The Rand index is the fraction of point pairs on which two partitions agree (same cluster in both, or different in both). Because it is high by chance for many clusters, Hubert & Arabie (1985) adjusted it:

$$\text{ARI} = \frac{\text{RI} - \mathbb{E}[\text{RI}]}{\max(\text{RI}) - \mathbb{E}[\text{RI}]} = \frac{\sum_{ij}\binom{n_{ij}}{2} - \big[\sum_i \binom{a_i}{2}\sum_j \binom{b_j}{2}\big]/\binom{N}{2}}{\tfrac12\big[\sum_i\binom{a_i}{2} + \sum_j\binom{b_j}{2}\big] - \big[\sum_i\binom{a_i}{2}\sum_j\binom{b_j}{2}\big]/\binom{N}{2}},$$

where \(n_{ij}\) is the contingency table of the two partitions with margins \(a_i, b_j\). ARI is 1 for identical partitions, 0 on average for independent ones, and can be negative. Stability analysis uses it internally: bootstrap or subsample the data, re-cluster, and compute the ARI between the partitions restricted to common points; a \(K\) whose clusters reappear across resamples (ARI ≳ 0.8) is trustworthy, whereas a \(K\) with ARI near 0.5 is slicing a continuum arbitrarily. Ecological applications and remote sensing

Vector quantisation. k-means originated as a signal-compression method (Lloyd worked at Bell Labs on pulse-code modulation): represent every vector by the index of its nearest codebook entry (centroid), so \(N\) vectors in \(d\) dimensions become \(N\) integers plus \(K\) centroids. The distortion \(J\) is literally the quantisation error. This is exactly what unsupervised classification of imagery does: every pixel's spectral vector is replaced by a class index. ISODATA (Iterative Self-Organizing Data Analysis), the classic remote-sensing algorithm, is k-means with heuristics that split clusters whose standard deviation exceeds a threshold and merge clusters whose centres are closer than another threshold, so \(K\) floats within user bounds. Analysts then label clusters as land-cover types by inspection; the clusters are spectral classes, not necessarily informational ones.

Climate classification. The Köppen system is a set of hand-made rules in temperature–precipitation space; clustering offers an objective alternative. Metzger et al. (2013) built the Global Environmental Stratification by PCA of 42 bioclimatic variables followed by ISODATA clustering into 125 strata grouped into 18 zones, now widely used to stratify sampling and to test the representativeness of networks such as FLUXNET. Any such product inherits k-means' properties: strata are convex regions of climate space, their boundaries are bisectors, and their number is a choice.

Plant functional types. Land-surface models need discrete PFTs with fixed parameters (\(V_{cmax}\), SLA, leaf lifespan, rooting depth). Clustering species in trait space (the leaf economics spectrum of Wright et al. 2004: LMA, leaf N, photosynthetic capacity, lifespan) is the empirical route to defining them and to asking how many are justified. Because trait data are log-normal and span orders of magnitude, log-transform and standardise first; because they are strongly correlated, cluster on the first few PCs. The usual finding is a continuum with weak modes, which is an argument for trait-based (continuous) rather than PFT-based model parameterisation.

Phenology regimes from NDVI. Treat each pixel's annual NDVI curve (23 or 46 dates) as a vector, use correlation distance (or standardise each curve) so that timing and shape rather than amplitude drive the grouping, and cluster. Resulting regimes (single summer peak, bimodal, evergreen, cropland double-cropping) map land-management and climate seasonality and are used to stratify phenology-model calibration.

Flux-tower site clustering. Clustering sites by climate, vegetation and flux characteristics (Papale et al. 2015) identifies redundant and missing site types in the network and defines the strata within which machine-learning upscaling (FLUXCOM-type products) can be trusted. Soil horizons and pedons are grouped by clustering depth-wise profiles of colour, texture, carbon and pH; cluster centroids become representative profiles for parameterising soil hydraulic and biogeochemical models.

Connections to ecosystem modelling

  • Discretising continuous drivers. PFTs, soil classes and climate zones in CLM, JULES, ORCHIDEE and DayCent are, in effect, cluster centroids with a fixed parameter vector each. k-means makes the implicit step explicit and lets you quantify within-cluster trait variance that the model discards (compare \(W(k)\) with \(T\)).
  • Site selection and representativeness. Clustering the environmental space of a region and choosing one site per cluster (or checking which clusters contain no sites) is standard practice for designing networks and for judging how far a model calibrated at towers can be extrapolated.
  • Reducing ensembles and parameter sets. After a large Monte Carlo or Latin-hypercube run, clustering parameter vectors that give acceptable fits (the "behavioural" set in GLUE) reveals equifinality: several distinct regions of parameter space that reproduce the data equally well. Cluster centroids are then used as representative parameter sets.
  • Regime detection in model output. Clustering simulated state trajectories (soil moisture, LAI, NEE seasonal cycles) across grid cells groups them into dynamical regimes, which is a compact way to evaluate a model against observed regime maps (phenology regimes from NDVI, hydrological regimes from streamflow).
  • Preprocessing for emulators and data assimilation. Vector quantisation of forcing data or initial states reduces the number of model runs needed; cluster-wise emulators (one per regime) are often more accurate than a single global emulator.
  • Honesty about assumptions. Whenever a model uses discrete classes derived by clustering, the spherical, equal-variance, fixed-\(k\) assumptions have been baked in. A GMM with BIC, or trait-continuum approaches, are the alternatives when the clusters are not convincing.

Exam-style questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer.

1. Write down the k-means objective and prove that Lloyd's algorithm converges. Does it find the global minimum?

\(J = \sum_n \sum_k r_{nk}\lVert x_n - \mu_k\rVert^2\) with \(r_{nk}\in\{0,1\}\), \(\sum_k r_{nk} = 1\). Lloyd alternates two exact minimisations. (i) With \(\mu\) fixed, \(J\) is a sum over points of \(\lVert x_n - \mu_{k(n)}\rVert^2\); choosing the nearest centre minimises each term independently, so \(J\) cannot increase. (ii) With \(r\) fixed, \(J\) separates into per-cluster convex quadratics \(\sum_{n\in C_k}\lVert x_n-\mu\rVert^2\) whose gradient \(-2\sum(x_n-\mu)\) vanishes at the mean, and the Hessian \(2N_kI\) is positive definite, so the mean is the unique minimiser and again \(J\) cannot increase. Hence \(J^{(t)}\) is non-increasing and bounded below by 0. There are finitely many partitions (at most \(K^N\)), and whenever the assignment changes \(J\) strictly decreases (with consistent tie-breaking), so no partition can recur and the algorithm terminates after finitely many steps at a partition-stable point. This is a local minimum of the combinatorial problem; the global problem is NP-hard and different initialisations give different local minima (hence k-means++ and restarts).

2. Derive the silhouette coefficient, explain why it lies in [−1, 1], and state its limitations as a criterion for k.

For point \(i\) in cluster \(A\): \(a(i)\) = mean distance to the other members of \(A\); \(b(i)\) = the smallest, over other clusters \(B\), of the mean distance from \(i\) to members of \(B\) (the "neighbouring cluster"). \(s(i) = (b-a)/\max(a,b)\). Since \(a,b\ge 0\): if \(b > a\), \(s = 1 - a/b \in (0,1]\); if \(a > b\), \(s = b/a - 1 \in [-1,0)\); if equal, \(s = 0\). So \(s \to 1\) for a point deep inside a well-separated cluster, \(s \approx 0\) on a boundary, \(s < 0\) when a point is on average closer to another cluster. Mean \(\bar s\) over all points is maximised over \(k\). Limitations: it needs all pairwise distances (\(O(N^2)\)), is undefined for \(k = 1\) so cannot say "no clusters", favours compact spherical clusters of similar size (the k-means geometry), and can prefer a coarse \(k = 2\) split when clusters are nested. The per-point plot is more diagnostic than the mean.

3. Explain k-means++ seeding. Why does sampling proportional to D² work better than either uniform sampling or always choosing the farthest point?

Choose the first centre uniformly from the data; then repeatedly compute each point's squared distance \(D(x)^2\) to its nearest existing centre and sample the next centre with probability \(D(x)^2/\sum_x D(x)^2\); after \(K\) seeds run Lloyd. Uniform sampling often places two seeds in one dense cluster and none in a distant small one, a mistake Lloyd cannot repair since no local move crosses the gap. The deterministic farthest-first rule always picks the most extreme point, so a handful of outliers become the seeds. D² sampling is the compromise: distant regions are strongly favoured (quadratically), yet a dense cluster with many moderately distant points accumulates enough total probability to be chosen, and a single outlier is not guaranteed to win. Arthur and Vassilvitskii proved the seeding alone is \(O(\log K)\)-competitive, \(\mathbb{E}[J] \le 8(\ln K + 2)J_{opt}\), and in experiments it is both faster (fewer Lloyd iterations) and better than random seeding.

4. Show that k-means is a limiting case of EM for a Gaussian mixture, and list which assumptions of k-means that limit makes explicit.

Take a mixture with \(\pi_k = 1/K\) and \(\Sigma_k = \sigma^2 I\). E-step: \(\gamma_{nk} = \exp(-\lVert x_n-\mu_k\rVert^2/2\sigma^2)/\sum_j \exp(-\lVert x_n-\mu_j\rVert^2/2\sigma^2)\). Dividing numerator and denominator by the largest term, as \(\sigma^2\to 0\) every term with a non-minimal distance decays as \(\exp(-\Delta/2\sigma^2)\to 0\), so \(\gamma_{nk}\to 1\) for the nearest centre and 0 otherwise: hard assignment. The expected complete-data log-likelihood \(\sum_n\sum_k\gamma_{nk}[\log\pi_k + \log\mathcal N(x_n|\mu_k,\sigma^2 I)] = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_n\sum_k r_{nk}\lVert x_n-\mu_k\rVert^2 + \text{const}\), so maximising it over \(\mu\) (M-step) is the centroid update and the quantity being improved is \(-J\). Assumptions made explicit: all clusters share one isotropic covariance (spherical, equal variance, hence linear bisector boundaries), equal mixing weights (so clusters are expected to be equally populous), Gaussian (so sensitivity to outliers), and hard membership (no uncertainty on boundaries). Relaxing each gives the general GMM.

5. You cluster 400 flux-tower sites on mean annual temperature (°C), annual precipitation (mm) and LAI (m² m⁻²). A colleague says standardising is "arbitrary". Respond, and describe what happens if you do not.

Squared Euclidean distance sums squared differences across features in their own units. Precipitation differences are of order 10³ mm, temperature 10 °C, LAI 1; squared, precipitation contributes ~10⁶ versus 10² and 1, so the partition is determined by precipitation alone and the other two variables are decorative. Raw-unit clustering is therefore not "neutral"; it is an implicit weighting by the accident of unit choice (switch mm to m and the result changes completely). Standardising to unit variance makes the implicit weighting equal, which is a defensible default and at least an explicit choice. If equal weights are not what is meant, scale features by weights reflecting their expected importance, or use Mahalanobis distance to also remove correlations (temperature and LAI covary). Skewed variables (precipitation, LAI) should be log-transformed first so that a few very wet sites do not dominate even after scaling. The scaling demo on this page shows ARI against the true groups rising from near 0 to near 1 after standardisation.

6. Compare k-means, k-medoids, Ward's hierarchical clustering and DBSCAN for grouping species by six leaf traits. Which would you use, and why?

k-means: fast, needs \(K\), squared Euclidean on (log-)standardised traits, spherical clusters, sensitive to outliers; good first pass after PCA on the traits. k-medoids/PAM: any dissimilarity (Gower for mixed types), robust, returns a representative real species per cluster, \(O(N^2)\) but fine for a few thousand species. Ward: same SSE criterion as k-means but hierarchical; produces a dendrogram that shows whether trait space is a continuum (merge heights increase smoothly) or has distinct groups (large jumps), and does not require \(K\) upfront; \(O(N^2)\) memory. DBSCAN: finds arbitrary shapes and labels outliers, but trait data are typically a single dense ellipsoid (the leaf economics spectrum) so density-based methods find one cluster or fragment it depending on \(\varepsilon\). Recommended: log-transform and standardise, PCA, Ward to inspect the dendrogram and choose a candidate \(K\) (checking with silhouette/gap and bootstrap stability), then k-means or PAM with that \(K\) to refine; expect a continuum and be ready to conclude that discrete PFTs are a modelling convenience rather than a biological fact.

7. Describe the gap statistic. Why is it the only criterion among elbow, silhouette, CH and gap that can conclude that there is one cluster?

Gap\((k) = \mathbb{E}^*[\log W_k] - \log W_k\), where the expectation is estimated by clustering \(B\) reference datasets drawn uniformly over the data's bounding box (or over the box aligned with the principal components) and averaging \(\log W^*_b(k)\). The gap measures how much faster the real data's within-cluster dispersion falls than that of structureless data. Choose the smallest \(k\) such that Gap\((k) \ge\) Gap\((k+1) - s_{k+1}\), \(s_{k+1} = \text{sd}_b[\log W^*_b(k+1)]\sqrt{1+1/B}\). Because \(W(1)\) and \(W^*(1)\) are both defined (total SSE about the grand mean), Gap(1) exists and is a legitimate candidate: when the data are uniform, Gap(k) hovers near zero for all \(k\) and the rule stops at \(k=1\). The silhouette needs at least two clusters (\(b(i)\) is undefined otherwise), CH divides by \(k-1\), and the elbow compares successive decreases, so none of them can return 1. In ecological data, where continua are common, that capability is valuable.

8. Explain how you would validate a k-means classification of phenology regimes derived from NDVI time series, and what distance you would use.

Distance: each pixel is a vector of 23–46 NDVI values; standardise each curve (or use correlation distance \(1 - r\)) so that the shape and timing of the seasonal cycle, not its amplitude or baseline, drive the clustering; alternatively reduce to a few PCs or to phenometrics (start, peak, end of season) first. Validation: (i) internal, with silhouette plots and the gap statistic over \(k\), looking for a \(k\) that is both supported and interpretable (single-peak temperate, evergreen flat, bimodal double-cropping, arid pulse); (ii) stability, by bootstrapping pixels, re-clustering and computing the adjusted Rand index between partitions, retaining a \(k\) whose ARI across replicates is high (≳ 0.8); (iii) external, comparing the regimes against independent land-cover and crop-calendar maps with ARI or confusion matrices, noting that spectral clusters need not coincide with informational classes; (iv) sensitivity, repeating with different initialisations, years and sensors to check that boundaries are not artefacts. Finally, inspect the centroid curves themselves: they should be physically plausible phenologies, and the Voronoi geometry means intermediate pixels are assigned to the nearest regime even if they are transitional, which a GMM's responsibilities would show.
