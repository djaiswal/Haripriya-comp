Hypothesis Testing and Confidence Intervals

How a handful of leaves, soil cores or flux half-hours becomes a statement about a population: the sampling distribution that makes inference possible, the tests that exploit it, and the intervals that report what the data can and cannot tell you.

The one-paragraph version

Every statistic you compute from a sample (a mean Vcmax, a difference in soil respiration between treatments, a fitted slope) is a random variable: repeat the experiment and you get a different number. Its distribution across hypothetical repeats is the sampling distribution, and its standard deviation is the standard error. Frequentist inference is the art of saying something about the sampling distribution from one realisation of it. A hypothesis test asks whether the observed statistic would be unusual if a specific null hypothesis were true, summarised by a p-value; a confidence interval inverts the test to report every parameter value that the data do not reject. Both rest on assumptions (independence above all) that ecological data routinely violate, and both are misread more often than they are read correctly. The practical message for a modeller: report effect sizes with intervals, choose the test by the data-generating process rather than by habit, and treat p = 0.04 and p = 0.06 as the same evidence.

Examiner's lens

Expect to be asked for exact definitions (p-value, confidence level, power), for a derivation (ANOVA sums of squares, the t-interval from a pivotal quantity), and for judgement: why a t-test on half-hourly flux data is wrong, why five leaves in one growth chamber are not five replicates, and how you would put an uncertainty on a Vcmax fitted from an A–Ci curve. Every section below works an ecosystem example.

Populations, samples and the sampling distribution

A population is the set of units you want to speak about (all soil cores in a field, all possible half-hours of NEE from a stand under given conditions). A parameter θ is a fixed but unknown property of it (its mean μ, variance σ², a regression slope). A sample X1, …, Xn is a set of observations modelled as random draws, and an estimator is a rule \(\hat\theta = T(X_1,\dots,X_n)\) that converts a sample into a guess. Good estimators are unbiased (\(E[\hat\theta] = \theta\)), consistent (\(\hat\theta \to \theta\) as \(n\to\infty\)) and efficient (smallest variance among competitors). The sample mean \(\bar X\) is unbiased for μ with

$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar X) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}, \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\bar X) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} \;\approx\; \frac{s}{\sqrt n},$$

where \(s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum (X_i - \bar X)^2\) is the unbiased sample variance (the n − 1 corrects for having estimated μ; the bias of the n version is −σ²/n, derived on the bias–variance page). The standard error shrinks as \(1/\sqrt n\): halving it costs four times the sampling effort, a fact that governs every field campaign budget.

The central limit theorem (CLT) is what makes the normal distribution ubiquitous. If Xi are independent with finite mean μ and variance σ², then

$$\frac{\bar X - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n} \;\xrightarrow{d}\; N(0,1) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty,$$

whatever the shape of the population. The convergence is fast for symmetric populations and slow for skewed ones: the Berry–Esseen bound puts the error at order \(\rho/(\sigma^3\sqrt n)\), where ρ is the third absolute central moment, so skewness is exactly what delays normality. Soil respiration, root biomass and hydraulic conductivity are typically lognormal with CV above 50%, and the lab below shows that the sampling distribution of their mean is still visibly skewed at n = 5 and only comfortably normal by n ≈ 30. The CLT applies to sums and means; it says nothing about the sampling distribution of a variance, a maximum, or a ratio at small n, which is one reason the bootstrap exists.

The logic of a test

A test begins with a null hypothesis H0, a precise statement about parameters (μelevated − μambient = 0), and an alternative H1 (≠ 0, or > 0 for a one-sided test). You choose a test statistic T whose sampling distribution is known if H0 and the model assumptions hold, compute its observed value tobs, and report

$$p = P\big(T \text{ at least as extreme as } t_{obs} \;\big|\; H_0 \text{ and assumptions true}\big).$$

That is the whole definition. Small p means the data would be surprising under H0; it is a measure of incompatibility between data and a model, nothing more. Greenland et al. [13] list 25 common misreadings; the four that matter most are:

  • p is not P(H0 | data). It conditions on H0, not on the data; reversing the conditioning needs a prior (Bayes).
  • p is not the probability that the result "arose by chance". Under H0 every result arises by chance; p is the probability of a result this extreme, given that.
  • 1 − p is not the probability that H1 is true, and p > 0.05 is not evidence of no effect. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence; a non-significant result with a wide CI is uninformative.
  • p says nothing about the size or importance of an effect. With n = 10⁴ flux half-hours a 0.1 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ difference is "highly significant" and biologically trivial.

In the Neyman–Pearson framing you fix a significance level α in advance and reject H0 when p ≤ α. Two errors are then possible. A Type I error rejects a true H0; its probability is α by construction. A Type II error fails to reject a false H0; its probability β depends on the true effect, the noise and n, and power = 1 − β is the probability of detecting an effect that is really there. Power rises with the effect size, which should be stated in meaningful units (a 15% reduction in Vcmax) and may be standardised as Cohen's d = (μ1 − μ2)/σ (0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large by convention [12]). A one-sided test puts all of α in one tail and is legitimate only when the direction was fixed before the data were seen and the opposite direction would be treated as "no effect"; defaulting to two-sided is the safe habit.

The standard tests and where they come from

z and t: one mean, two means, paired differences

If σ is known, \(z = (\bar X - \mu_0)/(\sigma/\sqrt n) \sim N(0,1)\) under H0. In practice σ is estimated, and replacing it by s adds variability that the normal ignores. Student (1908) showed that for normal data

$$t = \frac{\bar X - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt n} \sim t_{n-1}, \qquad f(t) = \frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu+1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\nu\pi}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)}\left(1 + \frac{t^2}{\nu}\right)^{-\frac{\nu+1}{2}},$$

a bell with heavier tails indexed by its degrees of freedom ν = n − 1 (the number of independent deviations from the mean once the mean itself has been used). The result follows from \(\bar X\) and \(s^2\) being independent for normal data, with \((n-1)s^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-1}\); t is a standard normal divided by \(\sqrt{\chi^2_\nu/\nu}\). As ν → ∞ the t distribution becomes normal; t0.975 is 2.57 at ν = 5, 2.23 at 10 and 2.04 at 30, against z = 1.96.

For paired designs (the same leaf before and after a drought cycle; the same plot in two years) take differences di and apply the one-sample test to them, \(t = \bar d/(s_d/\sqrt n)\), ν = n − 1. Pairing removes between-unit variance from the denominator and is the simplest form of blocking. For two independent samples the classical pooled test assumes equal variances,

$$t = \frac{\bar X_1 - \bar X_2}{s_p\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}}, \qquad s_p^2 = \frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2 + (n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}, \qquad \nu = n_1+n_2-2,$$

while Welch's test [10] drops that assumption:

$$t = \frac{\bar X_1 - \bar X_2}{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2}}, \qquad \nu \approx \frac{\left(s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2\right)^2}{\dfrac{(s_1^2/n_1)^2}{n_1-1} + \dfrac{(s_2^2/n_2)^2}{n_2-1}}.$$

The Welch–Satterthwaite ν comes from matching the variance of the estimated denominator to that of a scaled χ²; it lies between min(n1, n2) − 1 and n1 + n2 − 2. Welch loses almost nothing when variances are equal and protects you when they are not, especially with unequal n, so it should be the default (R's t.test already makes it so). Do not "test for equal variances first": the two-stage procedure has worse properties than simply using Welch.

F and one-way ANOVA

To compare k group means at once (Vcmax across four nitrogen levels), decompose the total variation about the grand mean \(\bar X\) into between-group and within-group parts. With nj observations in group j, N = Σnj, and group means \(\bar X_j\):

$$\underbrace{\sum_j\sum_i (X_{ij}-\bar X)^2}_{SS_T} \;=\; \underbrace{\sum_j n_j(\bar X_j - \bar X)^2}_{SS_B} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_j\sum_i (X_{ij}-\bar X_j)^2}_{SS_W},$$

because the cross term \(\sum_j (\bar X_j-\bar X)\sum_i(X_{ij}-\bar X_j)\) vanishes (the inner sum is zero by definition of the group mean). Divide by degrees of freedom to get mean squares and form

$$F = \frac{MS_B}{MS_W} = \frac{SS_B/(k-1)}{SS_W/(N-k)} \;\sim\; F_{k-1,\,N-k} \text{ under } H_0: \mu_1 = \dots = \mu_k.$$

The logic is that \(E[MS_W] = \sigma^2\) always, while \(E[MS_B] = \sigma^2 + \sum_j n_j\tau_j^2/(k-1)\) with τj = μj − μ: group differences inflate the numerator only. For k = 2 the one-way F equals the pooled t² exactly. The F distribution is the ratio of two independent χ² variables each divided by its df; the same F test, with s1²/s2² ~ Fn₁−1, n₂−1, compares two variances, but it is so sensitive to non-normality that Levene's or the Brown–Forsythe test is preferred for that job.

A significant F says only that not all means are equal. Post-hoc comparisons then ask which pairs differ: Tukey's HSD (studentised range, all pairs), Dunnett's (each treatment vs control), or pairwise t-tests with a multiplicity correction (next section). Planned contrasts specified in advance (linear trend across N levels) are more powerful than unplanned pairwise fishing.

χ² tests for counts

For categorical data (presence/absence of mycorrhizal colonisation across soil types; counts of seedlings per quadrat class) compare observed counts Oi with expected Ei:

$$X^2 = \sum_i \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i} \;\stackrel{\text{approx}}{\sim}\; \chi^2_{\text{df}}.$$

In a goodness-of-fit test against a fully specified distribution df = k − 1, minus one more for each parameter estimated from the data (fitting a Poisson to quadrat counts costs one df for λ̂). In an r × c test of independence, Eij = (row total × column total)/N and df = (r − 1)(c − 1). The approximation needs expected counts of about 5 or more; otherwise use Fisher's exact test or a permutation test. χ² is also the asymptotic distribution of the likelihood-ratio statistic, which is why it reappears in nonlinear-model inference below.

Nonparametric and permutation alternatives

Rank tests replace the data by their ranks and need no distributional form. The Mann–Whitney U (Wilcoxon rank-sum) test compares two independent groups; its null is that the two distributions are identical, and it is best read as a test of P(X > Y) = ½, not strictly of medians. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test is the paired analogue, and Kruskal–Wallis is a rank ANOVA whose statistic H is approximately χ²k−1. They sacrifice a little power for normal data (asymptotic efficiency 3/π ≈ 0.95) and gain a lot for heavy-tailed data, but they do not fix non-independence and they can be sensitive to unequal variances. A permutation test is more general: compute your statistic (a difference in means, a correlation, an F) for the data, then for many random relabellings of the group memberships, and report the fraction of relabellings at least as extreme. It is exact under exchangeability of units under H0, works for any statistic, and is the natural route for odd quantities such as a difference in fitted Γ* between two species. For time series, exchangeability fails and one must permute blocks or whole seasons.

Assumptions and diagnostics

Three assumptions underlie almost everything above. Independence is the most important and the least checkable from the data: it must come from the design. Normality matters for the sampling distribution of the statistic, not the raw data; the CLT rescues tests on means at moderate n but not tests on variances or small-sample tests with skewed data. Homoscedasticity (equal variance across groups or along a regression) matters for pooled tests and for the SE of regression slopes. Diagnose with residual plots (residuals vs fitted; QQ plot of residuals), not with formal tests of the assumptions, which are underpowered at small n and over-sensitive at large n. Ives [16] shows that, for the usual goal of testing a mean or slope, log-transforming skewed count or biomass data and running a linear model often performs as well as or better than a GLM on the raw scale; what you must not do is let the choice follow the p-value.

Multiple testing

Run m independent tests of true nulls at α = 0.05 and the probability of at least one false rejection, the family-wise error rate (FWER), is 1 − (1 − α)m: 0.40 for m = 10, 0.64 for m = 20, 0.99 for m = 100. A screen of 50 soil variables against a treatment is expected to produce 2.5 "significant" results from noise alone. Three corrections are standard.

  • Bonferroni: reject when pi ≤ α/m. Controls FWER ≤ α for any dependence structure (Boole's inequality), but is conservative, with power collapsing as m grows.
  • Holm (step-down): sort p(1) ≤ … ≤ p(m); reject p(i) while p(i) ≤ α/(m − i + 1); stop at the first failure. Uniformly more powerful than Bonferroni, still controls FWER, no extra assumptions. There is no reason to prefer Bonferroni over Holm.
  • Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) [7]: find the largest i with p(i) ≤ iα/m and reject all hypotheses up to it. This controls the false discovery rate, FDR = E[false rejections / total rejections], at π0α ≤ α, where π0 is the fraction of true nulls. It accepts a few false discoveries in exchange for far more true ones, the right trade-off in exploratory screens (which genes, which soil properties, which pixels) where follow-up will happen.

The choice between FWER and FDR is scientific, not statistical: controlling FWER is for confirmatory claims where a single false positive is costly; FDR is for generating a shortlist. Either way, the family must be defined honestly, which means counting the tests you ran and did not report.

Confidence intervals

A confidence procedure at level 1 − α is a pair of statistics L(X), U(X) such that

$$P_\theta\big(L(X) \le \theta \le U(X)\big) \;\ge\; 1-\alpha \quad \text{for every } \theta.$$

The probability is over repeated samples; θ is fixed. A realised interval [52.1, 61.3] either contains θ or does not, and the 95% belongs to the procedure: in the long run 95% of the intervals it produces cover the truth (Neyman 1937 [9]). "There is a 95% probability that Vcmax lies in [52.1, 61.3]" is a Bayesian statement about a credible interval and needs a prior; for flat priors and large n the two intervals coincide numerically, which is why the misreading is so common and so rarely harmful. The lab below makes the frequentist definition concrete by drawing 100 intervals and counting.

Construction via pivotal quantities

The standard recipe is to find a pivot: a function of the data and θ whose distribution does not depend on θ. For a normal mean with unknown σ,

$$\frac{\bar X - \mu}{s/\sqrt n} \sim t_{n-1} \;\Longrightarrow\; P\!\left(-t_{n-1,1-\alpha/2} \le \frac{\bar X - \mu}{s/\sqrt n} \le t_{n-1,1-\alpha/2}\right) = 1-\alpha,$$

and solving the inequality for μ gives the familiar interval \(\bar X \pm t_{n-1,1-\alpha/2}\, s/\sqrt n\). The same recipe gives the other standard intervals:

  • Difference in means (Welch): \((\bar X_1 - \bar X_2) \pm t_{\nu,1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2}\).
  • Variance: from \((n-1)s^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-1}\), \(\sigma^2 \in \left[\dfrac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{n-1,1-\alpha/2}},\; \dfrac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{n-1,\alpha/2}}\right]\), which is asymmetric about s² and very wide at small n (n = 10 gives roughly [0.47 s², 3.3 s²]).
  • Proportion: the Wald interval \(\hat p \pm z\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\) uses the estimated SE and has poor coverage, often far below nominal, when p is near 0 or 1 or n is small (and it collapses to zero width at p̂ = 0). The Wilson interval inverts the score test using the null SE, \(\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}\), and solving \(|\hat p - p_0| = z\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}\) for p0 gives
    $$\frac{\hat p + \frac{z^2}{2n} \;\pm\; z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + z^2/n},$$
    which has close to nominal coverage for all p and n ≥ 5. Use Wilson (or Agresti–Coull) for survival fractions and germination rates; never Wald.

Duality with tests

The 1 − α confidence set is exactly the set of parameter values θ0 that a level-α test would not reject: \(\theta_0 \in CI \iff p(\theta_0) > \alpha\). So a 95% CI for a difference that excludes zero is equivalent to p < 0.05, but the interval says much more: its location gives the effect size and its width gives the precision. Two studies with p = 0.03 may have intervals [0.2, 14] and [6.1, 7.9]; only the second is informative. This is why the ASA statement [6] and most journals now ask for estimates with intervals rather than bare p-values. Interval width scales with SE, so with 1/√n: a 95% interval roughly ±2 SE, a 99% interval ±2.6 SE.

Bootstrap intervals

Many quantities you actually care about have no textbook pivot: a ratio such as Jmax/Vcmax, a median, an optimum temperature read off a fitted curve, the output of a soil-carbon model run at the fitted parameters. Efron's bootstrap [8] replaces the unknown sampling distribution by the distribution of the statistic across resamples drawn with replacement from the data. Draw B resamples of size n, compute \(\hat\theta^*_b\) for each, and:

  • Bootstrap SE = SD of the \(\hat\theta^*_b\); normal interval \(\hat\theta \pm z\,\mathrm{SE}^*\).
  • Percentile interval: the α/2 and 1 − α/2 quantiles of the \(\hat\theta^*_b\). Simple, transformation-respecting, but biased when the bootstrap distribution is shifted or skewed relative to the truth.
  • BCa (bias-corrected and accelerated): adjust the percentiles using a bias term \(z_0 = \Phi^{-1}\big(\#\{\hat\theta^*_b < \hat\theta\}/B\big)\) and an acceleration \(a\) estimated by the jackknife, \(a = \sum_i(\bar\theta_{(\cdot)} - \hat\theta_{(i)})^3 \big/ 6\big[\sum_i(\bar\theta_{(\cdot)} - \hat\theta_{(i)})^2\big]^{3/2}\), where \(\hat\theta_{(i)}\) omits observation i. The endpoints are the bootstrap quantiles at
    $$\alpha_1 = \Phi\!\left(z_0 + \frac{z_0 + z_{\alpha/2}}{1 - a(z_0 + z_{\alpha/2})}\right), \qquad \alpha_2 = \Phi\!\left(z_0 + \frac{z_0 + z_{1-\alpha/2}}{1 - a(z_0 + z_{1-\alpha/2})}\right).$$
    BCa is second-order accurate (coverage error O(1/n) rather than O(1/√n)) and is the default recommendation when B ≥ 2000.

The bootstrap assumes the sample is a fair picture of the population: it fails for very small n (n = 4 has only 35 distinct resamples), for extremes (the maximum), and for dependent data, where you must resample blocks (block bootstrap for flux time series) or whole clusters (resample plots, not leaves within plots). For regression and nonlinear models you can resample cases or resample residuals; the latter keeps the design fixed and is the usual choice for A–Ci curves where the Ci levels are set by the experimenter.

Intervals for nonlinear model parameters: Vcmax ± what?

When you fit the FvCB model to an A–Ci curve by nonlinear least squares and report Vcmax = 62 ± 4 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, the ± is almost always a Wald standard error. With residual variance \(\hat\sigma^2 = RSS/(n-p)\) and the n × p Jacobian \(J_{ik} = \partial f(x_i;\hat\theta)/\partial\theta_k\) of the model at the fitted parameters,

$$\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\theta) \approx \hat\sigma^2\,(J^\top J)^{-1}, \qquad \mathrm{SE}_k = \sqrt{[\hat\sigma^2 (J^\top J)^{-1}]_{kk}}, \qquad \hat\theta_k \pm t_{n-p,\,1-\alpha/2}\,\mathrm{SE}_k.$$

\(J^\top J/\hat\sigma^2\) is the Gauss–Newton approximation to the Hessian of the negative log-likelihood, i.e. the observed Fisher information (see the maximum-likelihood page). The interval is symmetric and assumes the model is locally linear in θ near the optimum. That fails in exactly the cases that matter: when Ci levels barely sample the Rubisco-limited region, when Vcmax and mesophyll conductance gm trade off along a curved valley, or when Tp is weakly identified. Then the Wald SE can be absurdly small or large, and off-diagonal elements of the covariance matrix show correlations near ±1.

The profile-likelihood interval is the robust alternative. For each fixed value of the parameter of interest, re-optimise all other parameters and record the profile log-likelihood \(\ell_p(\theta_k)\). The interval is the set

$$\left\{\theta_k : 2\big[\ell(\hat\theta) - \ell_p(\theta_k)\big] \le \chi^2_{1,\,1-\alpha}\right\}, \qquad \chi^2_{1,0.95} = 3.84 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{drop of } 1.92 \text{ log-likelihood units}.$$

It is asymmetric when the likelihood is, invariant to reparametrisation (the interval for log Vcmax is the log of the interval for Vcmax), and reduces to the Wald interval when the likelihood is quadratic. The underlying result is the likelihood-ratio test (Wilks): for nested models, \(\Lambda = 2[\ell_{full} - \ell_{reduced}] \sim \chi^2_{q}\) with q the number of constrained parameters, so "does adding a TPU limitation improve this A–Ci fit?" is answered by whether 2Δℓ exceeds 3.84. The same statistic compares a one-pool with a two-pool soil-carbon decay model, and model comparison without nesting uses AIC instead.

A Bayesian credible interval answers the question people want answered, P(θ ∈ I | data) = 0.95, by combining the likelihood with a prior and reading off the posterior quantiles (equal-tailed) or the highest-density region. For well-identified parameters and weak priors it is numerically close to the profile interval; it differs, and helps, when the prior carries real information (Vcmax cannot be negative; the Rubisco kinetic constants are known from other data), which is exactly the situation in ecosystem-model calibration. The frequentist and Bayesian intervals are answers to different questions; neither is "the" uncertainty.

Regression, ANOVA, mixed models and the independence problem

In a linear model y = Xβ + ε with ε ~ N(0, σ²I), each coefficient has \(\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\beta) = \hat\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\) and the t statistic \(\hat\beta_k/\mathrm{SE}_k \sim t_{n-p}\) tests βk = 0; the regression t-test and the two-sample t-test are the same test when X is a dummy variable for group. One-way ANOVA is regression on k − 1 dummies, and its F is the nested-model F test

$$F = \frac{(RSS_0 - RSS_1)/(p_1 - p_0)}{RSS_1/(n - p_1)} \sim F_{p_1-p_0,\; n-p_1},$$

comparing the model with and without the group factor. Seeing ANOVA, regression and t-tests as one linear model with one F test removes most of the apparent zoo of procedures.

Pseudoreplication. Hurlbert [5] coined the term for the most common design error in ecology: treating subsamples of one experimental unit as independent replicates. Five leaves from a single elevated-CO₂ chamber and five from a single ambient chamber give n = 1 per treatment, not 5, because chamber effects (position, airflow, a faulty sensor) are confounded with treatment and the leaves within a chamber share them. The t-test on 10 leaves has a hugely inflated Type I error because its SE is the within-chamber SE, which is not the SE relevant to the treatment contrast. The remedies are design (replicate chambers, randomise, interleave) and analysis that respects the hierarchy: mixed models with random effects for chamber, plot or site, so that the treatment effect is tested against the between-unit variance with the correct degrees of freedom. GLMMs extend this to counts and proportions and are now standard in ecology despite real difficulties with inference (Bolker et al. [15]). Partial pooling across sites is also the right way to estimate site-specific parameters (see the bias–variance page).

Autocorrelation. Flux-tower NEE at half-hourly resolution is strongly autocorrelated: tonight's respiration resembles last night's. Treating N = 17,520 half-hours per year as independent makes the SE of an annual difference tens of times too small. For an AR(1) process with lag-1 correlation ρ the effective sample size for a mean is approximately

$$n_{\text{eff}} \approx n\,\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho},$$

so ρ = 0.8 cuts n by a factor of nine and ρ = 0.95 by a factor of 39. Spatial data (soil cores 1 m apart, remote-sensing pixels) have the same problem in two dimensions. Remedies: aggregate to a scale where dependence is weak (daily or monthly means, then test on those), model the dependence explicitly (GLS with ARMA errors, spatial random fields), use heteroscedasticity-and-autocorrelation-consistent (Newey–West) standard errors, or block-bootstrap. The uncertainty of annual NEE sums is a textbook case: Richardson and co-workers showed that random flux error is better described by a Laplace than a normal distribution and scales with flux magnitude, and that gap-filling and u*-filter choices dominate the total uncertainty; none of this is captured by a naive SE.

Power, sample size, and the replication crisis

Power analysis is done before the experiment. For a two-sample comparison with common SD σ, detecting a difference δ with two-sided α and power 1 − β requires approximately

$$n \text{ per group} \;\approx\; \frac{2\,(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2\,\sigma^2}{\delta^2} \;=\; \frac{2\,(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2}{d^2}.$$

With α = 0.05 and power 0.80, \((1.96 + 0.84)^2 = 7.85\), so n ≈ 15.7/d²: 16 per group for d = 1, 63 for d = 0.5, 393 for d = 0.2. The exact t-based calculation adds one or two. The formula comes from requiring the alternative's sampling distribution, centred at δ/SE, to put 1 − β of its mass beyond the critical value: δ/SE = z1−α/2 + z1−β with SE = σ√(2/n). For a FACE study expecting a 15% drop in Vcmax with a between-plot CV of 20%, d = 0.75 and you need about 29 plots per treatment; most FACE experiments have 3–4, which is why they test leaf-level responses within a design that accepts low power at the ring level. Post-hoc power computed from the observed effect is a monotone function of the p-value and adds no information; what helps after the fact is the confidence interval.

Low power has a second cost: when an underpowered study does reach significance, the estimated effect is biased upward (the winner's curse), because only large noise excursions clear the bar. Combine this with flexibility in analysis (p-hacking: trying transformations, covariates, subsets, outlier rules until p < 0.05; the "garden of forking paths" even without intent), publication bias toward positive results, and low prior probability of the hypotheses tested, and Ioannidis's argument [14] follows: a large fraction of published significant findings are false positives, and their effect sizes are inflated. The responses are now standard practice in good labs: pre-registration of hypotheses and analysis plans, separation of exploratory from confirmatory analyses, reporting effect sizes with confidence intervals for every comparison (not just the significant ones), sharing data and code, and replication. The ASA statement [6] summarises the principles: p-values do not measure the probability that a hypothesis is true, scientific conclusions should not rest on whether p crosses a threshold, and a p-value by itself is not a good measure of evidence. For ecosystem modelling in particular, the analogue of p-hacking is tuning a model to a benchmark and then reporting the agreement as validation.

How to report a comparison

"Elevated CO₂ reduced Vcmax,25 from 61.8 (SD 9.4, n = 8 plots) to 53.1 (SD 10.2, n = 8) µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, a difference of 8.7 (95% CI 2.3 to 15.1; Welch t = 1.78, ν = 13.9, p = 0.097; d = 0.89)." Everything an examiner or reader needs is there: direction, size, precision, the test, and the fact that the sample is small. Notice that the interval and p-value here are consistent (p ≈ 0.10 and a CI that excludes zero would be a contradiction—check your output when they disagree). Compare with "Vcmax was not significantly affected (p > 0.05)", which hides an effect of plausible size.

Connections to ecosystem modelling

  • Treatment comparisons that feed parameters. The down-regulation of Vcmax under elevated CO₂ (Ainsworth & Long's FACE meta-analysis), the temperature acclimation of Jmax/Vcmax (Kattge & Knorr), and the CO₂ response of stomatal slope g1 all enter land-surface models as parameters whose evidence is a collection of t-tests, ANOVAs and meta-analytic CIs. Knowing how wide those intervals were tells you how much to trust the acclimation module in CLM or JULES.
  • Parameter uncertainty from curve fitting. Wald SEs on Vcmax, Jmax, Tp and Rd from A–Ci fits (Sharkey et al. 2007), and on turnover times from soil-incubation decay curves, are the inputs to parameter-uncertainty propagation in ecosystem models. Profile-likelihood or bootstrap intervals reveal when a parameter is not identified by the data, which is the first warning of equifinality.
  • Model benchmarking. "Does the model reproduce observed GPP?" is a hypothesis-testing question with a twist: both the observation (flux partitioning, u* filtering, gap filling) and the model have error, and the observations are autocorrelated. Metrics such as RMSE and bias need their own CIs, obtained by block bootstrap over days or years; a t-test on half-hourly residuals is wrong.
  • Trend detection. Testing whether NEE, soil respiration or the growing-season length has changed over 20 years of tower data is a regression t-test on the slope with autocorrelated residuals; the Mann–Kendall trend test is the rank-based alternative, and both need pre-whitening or block methods.
  • Remote sensing and spatial data. Comparing NDVI or SIF between land-cover classes over thousands of pixels gives trivially small p-values; the effective sample size is set by the spatial range of the autocorrelation, and blocked cross-validation (Roberts et al. 2017) is the predictive analogue.
  • Likelihood as the bridge. Every statistic here is a special case of likelihood-based inference; the cost function minimised in model–data fusion (e.g. Richardson et al. 2010, Keenan et al. 2012) is a negative log-likelihood with an observation-error covariance, and the LRT and profile intervals above are how the "significance" of a parameter or process is judged within it. The Bayesian calibration of DayCent, MIMICS or BioCro replaces the test with a posterior but inherits all the independence assumptions in the likelihood.
  • Experimental design for model testing. Hurlbert's lesson applies to model experiments too: 10 model runs with different random seeds compared to 10 runs of another configuration are not 10 independent samples of "the world"; structural differences are confounded with the shared forcing. Multi-site, multi-year designs with site as a random effect are the appropriate frame.

Exam-style questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer.

1. Define the p-value precisely. Give three common misinterpretations and explain why p = 0.3 does not show that a treatment has no effect.

The p-value is the probability, computed under the null hypothesis and the model's assumptions, of obtaining a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed. It conditions on H0; it is a statement about data given a hypothesis. Misinterpretations: (i) p is the probability that H0 is true (this needs a prior and Bayes' theorem; p is P(data | H0), not P(H0 | data)); (ii) 1 − p is the probability that the alternative is true or that the result will replicate; (iii) p measures the size or importance of the effect (it confounds effect size with n; large n makes trivial effects significant). p = 0.3 means the data are compatible with no effect, but they are also compatible with every effect inside the 95% confidence interval. If the interval for a Vcmax difference is [−4, 20] µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, a 15 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ effect is entirely consistent with the data; the study was simply too small. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence; to claim no effect one needs an equivalence test showing the interval lies inside a pre-specified band of practical irrelevance.

2. Derive the one-way ANOVA decomposition, state the expectations of the mean squares, and show that for two groups F = t².

Write Xij − X̄ = (Xij − X̄j) + (X̄j − X̄). Square and sum over i and j. The cross term is 2Σj(X̄j − X̄)Σi(Xij − X̄j) and the inner sum is zero because deviations from a group mean sum to zero, so SST = SSW + SSB with SSB = Σjnj(X̄j − X̄)². Degrees of freedom: N − 1 = (N − k) + (k − 1). Within each group E[Σi(Xij − X̄j)²] = (nj − 1)σ², so E[MSW] = σ². For the between term, X̄j has mean μ + τj and variance σ²/nj, and working through E[Σnj(X̄j − X̄)²] gives (k − 1)σ² + Σnjτj², so E[MSB] = σ² + Σnjτj²/(k − 1), which equals σ² only under H0. Under normality and H0 the two sums of squares are independent scaled χ² variables (Cochran's theorem), so F = MSB/MSW ~ Fk−1,N−k. For k = 2 with group sizes n1, n2: SSB = n1(X̄1 − X̄)² + n2(X̄2 − X̄)². Substituting X̄ = (n1X̄1 + n2X̄2)/N gives SSB = (X̄1 − X̄2)²·n1n2/N = (X̄1 − X̄2)²/(1/n1 + 1/n2), and MSW = sp², so F = (X̄1 − X̄2)²/[sp²(1/n1 + 1/n2)] = t², and F1,ν is the distribution of tν².

3. You have Vcmax from 6 leaves in each of two species with SDs of 6 and 15. Which two-sample test do you use and why? Explain where the Welch degrees of freedom come from.

Welch's t-test. The pooled test assumes equal variances; here the variance ratio is 6.25, and with unequal variances the pooled t has the wrong null distribution (Type I error inflates when the smaller group has the larger variance and deflates otherwise; with equal n the distortion is mild but the df are still wrong). Welch's statistic divides the difference in means by √(s1²/n1 + s2²/n2), the correct SE whatever the variances. Its denominator squared is a linear combination of two independent scaled χ² variables, which is not itself χ²; Welch and Satterthwaite approximate it by a single χ²ν/ν scaled to have the same mean and variance. Matching variances gives ν = (s1²/n1 + s2²/n2)²/[(s1²/n1)²/(n1 − 1) + (s2²/n2)²/(n2 − 1)]. Here (6 + 37.5)²/[36/5 + 1406.25/5] = 1892/288.5 ≈ 6.6, far below the pooled 10, which correctly reflects that most of the uncertainty comes from the noisy species with only 6 leaves. One should also ask whether the heavier variance is a transformation issue (lognormal Vcmax?) and whether "leaves" are independent replicates or come from fewer plants.

4. Define a 95% confidence interval, derive the t-interval for a mean from a pivotal quantity, and explain the duality with hypothesis tests. What is wrong with saying "there is a 95% probability that μ lies in this interval"?

A 95% confidence procedure produces random intervals [L(X), U(X)] with Pθ(L ≤ θ ≤ U) ≥ 0.95 for every θ; the probability refers to repeated sampling, the parameter being fixed. Derivation: for Xi iid N(μ, σ²), X̄ and s² are independent, (X̄ − μ)/(σ/√n) ~ N(0,1) and (n − 1)s²/σ² ~ χ²n−1, so T = (X̄ − μ)/(s/√n) ~ tn−1: its distribution does not involve μ or σ, so it is a pivot. P(−tc ≤ T ≤ tc) = 0.95 with tc = tn−1,0.975; rearranging the event gives X̄ − tcs/√n ≤ μ ≤ X̄ + tcs/√n, an event of probability 0.95 whatever μ is. Duality: the 95% CI is the set of μ0 for which the level-0.05 test of H0: μ = μ0 does not reject, because |X̄ − μ0|/(s/√n) ≤ tc is the same inequality. A CI therefore carries all the information of the test plus the effect size and precision. The sentence "95% probability that μ lies in [a, b]" treats μ as random given the data; after the data are seen the realised interval either contains μ or not, and the frequentist framework assigns no probability to that. The sentence is correct for a Bayesian credible interval with a stated prior, and for flat priors and large n the two intervals coincide numerically, which is why the misreading usually does no harm but is still a different claim.

5. You fit the FvCB model to an A–Ci curve and your software reports Vcmax = 62 ± 4. Explain how that SE was computed, when it is unreliable, two better ways to obtain an interval, and how you would test whether Vcmax differs between ambient and elevated CO₂ plots.

The SE is a Wald SE from nonlinear least squares: with residual variance σ̂² = RSS/(n − p) and the Jacobian J of model predictions with respect to the parameters at the optimum, Var(θ̂) ≈ σ̂²(JᵀJ)⁻¹, and the SE is the square root of the diagonal element. JᵀJ/σ̂² approximates the Hessian of the negative log-likelihood (observed Fisher information), so the interval assumes the log-likelihood is quadratic near the optimum and the errors are independent, homoscedastic and roughly normal. It is unreliable when the Ci range samples the Rubisco-limited region with only two or three points, when Vcmax trades off with gm or with the choice of transition point (a curved likelihood valley, correlations near ±1 in the covariance matrix), and when n − p is small. Better: (i) profile likelihood, fixing Vcmax at a grid of values, re-optimising the other parameters, and taking the set where 2[ℓ(θ̂) − ℓp] ≤ 3.84, which gives an asymmetric, reparametrisation-invariant interval; (ii) bootstrap, resampling residuals (since the Ci levels are fixed by design) and refitting B ≥ 1000 times to obtain percentile or BCa intervals. To compare treatments: the unit of replication is the plot (or ring), not the leaf or the curve. Fit one Vcmax per leaf, average to the plot, and compare plots with Welch's t-test (or a mixed model with plot as a random effect if several leaves per plot are retained), reporting the difference with a CI. Using the per-curve SEs or the leaves as replicates would be pseudoreplication. Alternatively fit a joint model with a treatment effect on Vcmax and test it by a likelihood-ratio test against the model without it, still with plot-level random effects.

6. A soil survey tests 50 variables for a tillage effect; four have p < 0.05 (p = 0.0004, 0.004, 0.02, 0.04). What would you conclude under Bonferroni, Holm and Benjamini–Hochberg, and which is appropriate?

Under 50 true nulls one expects 2.5 results below 0.05 by chance, so four is barely more than noise, and the raw list cannot be reported as discoveries. Bonferroni: threshold 0.05/50 = 0.001; only p = 0.0004 survives. Holm: sorted thresholds 0.05/50 = 0.001, 0.05/49 = 0.00102, 0.05/48, …; 0.0004 ≤ 0.001 (reject), 0.004 > 0.00102 (stop); again one rejection, but Holm would have been more generous had the second p been smaller. BH: compare p(i) with iα/m = 0.001i: 0.0004 ≤ 0.001 yes; 0.004 ≤ 0.002 no; 0.02 ≤ 0.003 no; 0.04 ≤ 0.004 no. The largest i satisfying the condition is 1, so BH also rejects only the first. All three agree here because the remaining p-values are not small relative to their rank. If this is an exploratory screen to choose variables for a follow-up experiment, FDR control (BH) is the right criterion and one might even relax to q = 0.1; if the paper claims a tillage effect on each variable, FWER control (Holm) is required. In either case the paper should list all 50 tests with effect sizes and intervals, not only the four.

7. A student compares annual NEE between a drought year and a normal year by applying a two-sample t-test to the 17,520 half-hourly values of each year and reports p < 10⁻¹⁵. Critique this and describe a defensible analysis.

The t-test assumes independent observations. Half-hourly NEE has strong diurnal and seasonal structure and residual autocorrelation (lag-1 ρ typically 0.8–0.95 after removing the diurnal cycle), so the effective sample size is of order n(1 − ρ)/(1 + ρ), tens to hundreds rather than 17,520; the SE is underestimated by a factor of √(n/neff) and the p-value is meaningless. There are further problems: about 30–50% of the half-hours are gap-filled model output, not measurements; the random flux error is heteroscedastic and heavy-tailed (Laplace-like, scaling with flux magnitude); and systematic errors (u* threshold, energy-balance closure, partitioning) dominate the annual uncertainty. Defensible options: (i) aggregate to daily or weekly sums, check the residual autocorrelation at that scale, and compare with a test or model that accounts for what remains (GLS with AR(1) errors, or a paired comparison of the same days of year); (ii) block-bootstrap the annual sums using blocks of weeks to months to obtain a CI for the difference; (iii) propagate measurement uncertainty via the Richardson et al. random-error model and the u*-threshold ensemble, and report the annual NEE difference with a total uncertainty rather than a p-value. With only one year per condition the comparison is also n = 1 per treatment: climate variables other than drought differed between the years, so the design cannot attribute the difference to drought without a model or additional years.

8. Design the sample size for an experiment to detect a 15% reduction in Vcmax under elevated CO₂, given a between-plot coefficient of variation of 20%, α = 0.05 and power 0.8. What happens to the required n if you can only afford 4 plots per treatment?

Express the effect in SD units: δ = 0.15 μ, σ = 0.20 μ, so d = 0.75. For a two-sided two-sample test with equal n, n ≈ 2(z0.975 + z0.80)²/d² = 2(1.96 + 0.842)²/0.5625 = 2 × 7.85/0.5625 ≈ 27.9, so about 28–30 plots per treatment (the exact t-based value is slightly larger because tν is wider than z at these df). With only 4 plots per treatment, SE of the difference = σ√(2/4) = 0.707 σ and δ/SE = 0.75/0.707 = 1.06; the critical value for ν = 6 is 2.45, so power ≈ P(T > 2.45 − 1.06) ≈ 0.12 (two-sided; slightly more including the other tail), i.e. an 88% chance of missing a real 15% effect, and any significant result would be a strong overestimate. Remedies: pair the design (measure the same plots before and after, or block by soil type) to remove between-plot variance from σ; accept a larger α for a pilot study; measure more leaves per plot to reduce within-plot noise (helps only the part of the 20% CV that is measurement error, not true between-plot variation); or use a meta-analytic approach across sites. Report the power calculation and the resulting CI, not a bare "not significant".
