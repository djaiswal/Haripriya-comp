Topic 6 of the SPAC section

Stomatal Conductance Models

How a leaf sets the valve between carbon gain and water loss, how five generations of models have described that valve, and why the choice of model decides what a land-surface model says about transpiration and CO₂ fertilisation.

The one-paragraph version

Stomata are the only variable resistance in the leaf's CO₂ supply path and the dominant control on transpiration, so every coupled carbon–water model needs a rule for stomatal conductance, gs. The oldest rule (Jarvis 1976) multiplies independent empirical responses to light, temperature, humidity, CO₂ and water status. The Ball–Woodrow–Berry (BWB, 1987) family instead ties gs to the assimilation rate itself, gs = g0 + g1A hs/Cs, which Leuning (1995) refined by using vapour-pressure deficit D instead of humidity. Medlyn et al. (2011) showed that nearly the same form, gs = g0 + 1.6(1 + g1/√D)A/Ca, follows from the Cowan–Farquhar optimality hypothesis that leaves maximise carbon gain minus a water cost λ, so that the single slope g1 ∝ √(Γ*λ) measures a species' water-use strategy. All of these predict a nearly constant Ci/Ca at a given D, which is why the coupled A–gs–Ci system has a stable solution. Where they fail, under soil drying, the newer hydraulic models (Tuzet 2003, Sperry 2017, Eller 2020) make gs a function of leaf water potential and xylem vulnerability, and land-surface models such as CLM5 and JULES are moving that way.

Examiner's lens

Three things get asked over and over: (1) write down and compare the BWB, Leuning and Medlyn equations and state what Ci/Ca each predicts; (2) derive Medlyn's model from the Cowan–Farquhar condition ∂A/∂E = 1/λ and interpret g1; (3) explain how gs is solved together with FvCB and the energy balance in a land model, and where the soil-moisture stress factor β belongs. Be ready to say which model each major land-surface model uses and what difference it makes to ET and CO₂ responses.

Stomatal physiology in one page

A stoma is a pore bounded by two guard cells. Opening is driven by solute accumulation: blue light and red light (via photosynthesis) activate the plasma-membrane H⁺-ATPase, the membrane hyperpolarises, K⁺ enters through inward-rectifying channels together with Cl⁻ and malate (synthesised from starch), water follows osmotically, and the turgid guard cells bow apart. Closure reverses this: abscisic acid (ABA) raises cytosolic Ca²⁺, activates anion channels (SLAC1), depolarises the membrane and drives K⁺ efflux. ABA is produced in roots under soil drying and, as is now considered dominant in angiosperms, in the leaf itself in response to falling leaf turgor. Guard cells therefore integrate hydraulic signals (epidermal and guard-cell turgor, hence leaf water potential and VPD) and chemical signals (ABA, also Ci: high intercellular CO₂ closes stomata, low Ci opens them). Response times are minutes to tens of minutes: closing in response to a step in VPD or darkness typically takes 5–15 min, opening in response to light 10–30 min, and these lags matter for sunfleck and flux-tower half-hourly data but are ignored by all the steady-state models below.

Three conversions recur in every stomatal model. First, conductance is measured for water vapour (gsw, mol H₂O m⁻² s⁻¹) but photosynthesis needs it for CO₂; the ratio of binary diffusivities in air gives

$$g_{sc} = \frac{g_{sw}}{1.6}, \qquad \frac{D_{H_2O}}{D_{CO_2}} = \frac{2.49\times10^{-5}}{1.55\times10^{-5}} \approx 1.6.$$

Second, the leaf boundary layer is a second conductance in series, gbw ≈ 1–3 mol m⁻² s⁻¹ for a leaf of a few centimetres in moderate wind (it scales with √(u/d)). Because transport there is partly turbulent the CO₂ ratio is 1.62/3 ≈ 1.37 rather than 1.6. The total CO₂ conductance from bulk air to the intercellular spaces is

$$\frac{1}{g_{tc}} = \frac{1.6}{g_{sw}} + \frac{1.37}{g_{bw}}, \qquad A = g_{tc}\,(C_a - C_i),$$

and Cs, the CO₂ mole fraction at the leaf surface that BWB-type models use, is Cs = Ca − 1.37A/gbw. Third, conductance in mol m⁻² s⁻¹ (the physiologist's unit, since the diffusion driving force is a mole-fraction difference) converts to the meteorologist's m s⁻¹ by dividing by the molar density of air, P/(RT) ≈ 41 mol m⁻³ at 25 °C and sea level: 0.4 mol m⁻² s⁻¹ ≈ 9.8 mm s⁻¹, i.e. a resistance of ≈ 100 s m⁻¹.

Empirical and semi-empirical models

Jarvis 1976: a product of independent responses

Jarvis [1] fitted conductance of Sitka spruce and Douglas fir to measurements and proposed that gs is a maximum conductance multiplied by independent, bounded response functions of each environmental driver:

$$g_s = g_{s,max}\; f_1(Q)\; f_2(T)\; f_3(D)\; f_4(\Psi_{leaf}\ \text{or}\ \theta_{soil})\; f_5(C_a), \qquad 0 \le f_k \le 1.$$

The light function is a saturating hyperbola, the temperature function a parabola around an optimum, the humidity function a linear decline with D, and the water-status function a threshold. The model is transparent and still underlies many hydrological and some biosphere models (SiB1, BIOME-BGC, Stewart's 1988 canopy version), but it has 8–12 parameters per species that must be re-fitted for each site, assumes the responses are independent (they are not: the D response depends on light and on soil water), and contains no mechanism linking conductance to photosynthesis, so it cannot predict the stomatal response to rising CO₂ except by an ad hoc f5.

Ball, Woodrow and Berry 1987

Ball et al. [2] plotted measured gs against A hs/Cs, where hs is the fractional relative humidity and Cs the CO₂ mole fraction, both at the leaf surface, and found a single straight line across light, temperature, humidity and CO₂ treatments:

$$g_{sw} = g_0 + g_1\,\frac{A\,h_s}{C_s}.$$

g1 is dimensionless (≈ 9–10 for C3 species, ≈ 4 for C4) and g0 is the residual conductance when A → 0 (≈ 0.01 mol m⁻² s⁻¹ for C3, 0.04 for C4 in Collatz's parameterisations [4, 5]). Its strengths are its simplicity and the built-in coupling to A, which gives sensible responses to light, temperature and CO₂ for free. Its weaknesses: the linear hs response has no mechanistic basis and predicts gs → g0 in very dry air regardless of A; gs does not approach g0 correctly at the CO₂ compensation point (A → 0 there, but the real leaf keeps a finite conductance); and it predicts, with g0 ≈ 0 and no boundary layer, from 1.6/(Ca − Ci) = g1hs/Ca,

$$\frac{C_i}{C_a} = 1 - \frac{1.6}{g_1 h_s}, \qquad \text{e.g. } 1 - \frac{1.6}{9\times0.7} = 0.75 .$$

Collatz et al. [4] made this the standard closure for coupled leaf models; it was used in SiB2, in CLM4 and CLM4.5, and in most of the first-generation coupled land-surface models.

Leuning 1995

Leuning [3] made two changes. Replacing Cs by Cs − Γ (Γ the CO₂ compensation point) fixes the low-CO₂ behaviour, since A and Cs − Γ now go to zero together and gs stays finite. Replacing hs by a hyperbolic function of VPD matches the observed response of stomata to the evaporative driving force rather than to humidity as such:

$$g_{sw} = g_0 + \frac{g_1\,A}{(C_s - \Gamma)\left(1 + D/D_0\right)},$$

with D0 an empirical sensitivity (≈ 1.5 kPa, range 0.35–3 kPa) and g1 (Leuning's a1) ≈ 5–15. The implied ratio is

$$\frac{C_i}{C_a} = 1 - \frac{1.6\,(1 - \Gamma/C_a)(1 + D/D_0)}{g_1},$$

which falls linearly with D rather than with 1 − hs. This is the closure used in CABLE and in several Australian and European models. Both BWB and Leuning are "semi-empirical": A does the mechanistic work, and the humidity or D factor is a fitted correlation.

Optimal stomatal behaviour and the Medlyn model

Cowan and Farquhar [6] asked what stomatal behaviour would maximise total carbon gain over a day for a fixed total water loss. By the Lagrangian condition, the marginal gain per unit water must be the same at every moment:

$$\frac{\partial A}{\partial E} = \frac{1}{\lambda} = \text{const}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{\partial E}{\partial A}\ \ (\text{mol H}_2\text{O mol}^{-1}\text{ CO}_2),$$

where λ is the marginal water cost of carbon gain, typically of order 10³ mol mol⁻¹. Equivalently, at each instant the leaf maximises A − E/λ. The condition says stomata should close more in dry air, where each unit of opening buys the same carbon but more water loss, and open more in the morning when D is low.

Medlyn et al. [7] turned this into a usable model by assuming photosynthesis is RuBP-regeneration limited, A = J(Ci − Γ*)/(4(Ci + 2Γ*)), and E = 1.6 gsc D (D as a mole fraction). Differentiating A with respect to gsc through Ci, and assuming Ci ≫ Γ* and that the slope term is small relative to A itself, the optimality condition becomes

$$\frac{3\,\Gamma^*\,(C_a - C_i)^2}{1.6\,D\,C_i^2} = \frac{1}{\lambda} \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{C_a - C_i}{C_i} = \sqrt{\frac{1.6\,D}{3\,\Gamma^*\lambda}} \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{C_i}{C_a} = \frac{g_1}{g_1 + \sqrt{D}},\quad g_1 = \sqrt{\frac{3\,\Gamma^*\lambda}{1.6}} .$$

Substituting into gsw = 1.6A/(Ca − Ci) and adding an intercept gives the unified stomatal optimisation (USO) model:

Medlyn et al. 2011

$$g_{sw} = g_0 + 1.6\left(1 + \frac{g_1}{\sqrt{D}}\right)\frac{A}{C_a}, \qquad \frac{C_i}{C_a} \approx \frac{g_1}{g_1 + \sqrt{D}}, \qquad \frac{A}{g_{sw}} \approx \frac{C_a\sqrt{D}}{1.6\,(g_1 + \sqrt{D})} .$$

g1 has units of √kPa when D is in kPa. It is the only free slope and has a clear meaning: a leaf with a large g1 has a high marginal water cost of carbon (λ large), keeps Ci/Ca high, and is "water-spending"; a small g1 leaf is conservative. Because Γ* rises with temperature, g1 is predicted to increase with growth temperature, which Lin et al. [8] confirmed. The 1.6 is kept explicit so that g1 is comparable across the water and CO₂ bases.

Lin et al. [8] compiled leaf gas-exchange data from 314 species and fitted g1 by plant functional type (approximate values, √kPa): C4 grasses 1.6, evergreen gymnosperms 2.3, tundra and arctic shrubs 2.2, evergreen angiosperm trees 4.1, deciduous angiosperm trees 4.5, shrubs 4.7, C3 grasses 5.3, C3 crops 5.8. The ordering follows wood density and leaf economics: dense-wooded, slow conifers are conservative, fast crops are profligate. Those are the values now used in CABLE, CLM5 and JULES.

The intrinsic water-use efficiency A/gsw = Ca(1 − Ci/Ca)/1.6 is a direct consequence of the Ci/Ca prediction. For Medlyn, at constant D and g1 it is proportional to Ca: as CO₂ rises, gs falls so that Ci/Ca stays constant, which is the behaviour seen in tree-ring δ¹³C records and FACE experiments. The three models differ in which humidity variable holds Ci/Ca constant (hs, 1 + D/D0, √D) and therefore in how transpiration responds to a drying atmosphere, which the first lab makes visible.

Water-potential feedback and hydraulic models

None of the models above contains soil water. In practice a β factor (0–1, a function of soil moisture or soil water potential) is applied somewhere: to Vcmax (CLM, ORCHIDEE, JSBACH), to g1 or the whole gs (CABLE in some versions, LPJ), or to both. De Kauwe et al. [16] tested the alternatives in CABLE against FLUXNET drought sites: applying β only to Vcmax leaves Ci/Ca unchanged (because gs follows A) and underestimates the rise in water-use efficiency during drought; applying it to g1 captures the WUE rise but gives the wrong A. Observations (e.g. Zhou et al. 2013) show both the slope g1 and Vcmax declining as predawn water potential falls, with the stomatal decline leading. This is the main failure mode of all three semi-empirical models under soil drying and the motivation for making gs depend on plant water status.

Tuzet, Perrier and Leuning 2003

Tuzet et al. [9] replaced the humidity term by a sigmoid function of leaf water potential Ψl and coupled it to a soil–root–xylem hydraulic model:

$$g_{sw} = g_0 + \frac{a\,A}{C_i - \Gamma}\,f_\Psi, \qquad f_\Psi = \frac{1 + e^{s_f\Psi_f}}{1 + e^{s_f(\Psi_f - \Psi_l)}}, \qquad E = \frac{\Psi_{soil} - \Psi_l}{R_{soil} + R_{plant}},$$

with Ψf ≈ −1.9 MPa the potential at which fΨ has fallen to half and sf ≈ 3.2 MPa⁻¹ its steepness. Because E = 1.6 gsD depends on gs, Ψl depends on E and gs depends on Ψl, the system is a closed feedback loop solved iteratively. The VPD response emerges rather than being imposed: higher D raises E, lowers Ψl, closes stomata. Soil drying does the same through Ψsoil. This is the conceptual ancestor of the plant-hydraulics schemes now in land models.

Profit maximisation and SOX

Sperry et al. [10] proposed that stomata maximise the difference between a normalised carbon gain A/Amax and a normalised hydraulic risk (Kmax − K(Ψ))/Kmax, where K(Ψ) is the xylem hydraulic conductance from the vulnerability curve. Opening increases A, but it also lowers Ψ along the pathway and moves the plant down its vulnerability curve, so the profit has a maximum at intermediate gs. The model needs no g1 and no empirical D function: both the VPD and soil-moisture responses come from the vulnerability curve (P50, slope) and the maximum conductance. Eller et al. [11] simplified this to the stomatal optimisation based on xylem hydraulics (SOX) model, in which stomata maximise A × K(Ψleaf)/Kmax, i.e. carbon gain weighted by the remaining fraction of hydraulic conductance; it has an analytical approximation cheap enough for a global model and has been implemented in JULES. Both predict that species with vulnerable xylem (less negative P50) should close earlier and behave more isohydrically.

At the model-infrastructure level, CLM5 replaced the soil-moisture β with plant hydraulic stress (PHS): a resistor network from soil to root to stem to leaf, with Ψleaf solved each time step and used to scale Vcmax and g0; JULES has an optional SOX scheme; ED2 and other demographic models carry plant hydraulics for mortality. The stomatal model itself (Medlyn in CLM5 and JULES) is unchanged, but its drought behaviour is now set by hydraulics rather than by soil moisture in the root zone. Buckley [12] reviews the mechanistic basis for all of these schemes and the remaining gaps, particularly the lack of an agreed mechanism for the VPD response.

Coupling gs with FvCB and the energy balance

In a coupled leaf model three equations must hold simultaneously: the biochemical demand for CO₂ (FvCB), the diffusive supply through stomata and boundary layer, and the stomatal rule itself:

$$A = \min(A_c, A_j, A_p)(C_i,\,T_l), \qquad A = \frac{C_a - C_i}{1.6/g_{sw} + 1.37/g_{bw}}, \qquad g_{sw} = f(A, D, C_a, \dots).$$

A fourth, the leaf energy balance, sets Tl from radiation, sensible heat and λE = λ·E(gsw, D), and D itself depends on Tl. Collatz et al. [4] showed how to solve this. For a given gsc = gsw/1.6 and leaf temperature, substituting Ci = Ca − A/gsc into each FvCB rate gives a quadratic in A. For a rate of the generic form A + Rd = Vm(Ci − Γ*)/(Ci + Km) (Vm = Vcmax, Km = Kc(1 + O/Ko) for Rubisco; Vm = J/4, Km = 2Γ* for RuBP regeneration), writing A′ = A + Rd and C′ = Ca + Rd/gsc,

$$A'^2 - \left[g_{sc}(C' + K_m) + V_m\right]A' + g_{sc}V_m\,(C' - \Gamma^*) = 0,$$

and the smaller root is the physical one. The outer loop then updates gsw from the stomatal model and iterates, usually 3–5 times, until A, Ci and gsw stop changing; the leaf temperature is updated in a further outer loop or solved jointly by Newton's method. With BWB closure the whole system can be reduced to a cubic in A (Baldocchi 1994), which is what CLM4.5 solved; CLM5 uses a bracketed root-finder on Ci. The solution is stable because all the semi-empirical models imply a near-constant Ci/Ca at given D: the supply and demand curves cross once.

Behaviour under elevated CO₂, drought and at night

Elevated CO₂. All A-based models predict gs falling as Ca rises because Ci/Ca is held constant: a doubling of Ca with a ~30% increase in A gives a ~35% reduction in gs, consistent with the FACE meta-analysis average of about −20% for +200 µmol mol⁻¹. Jarvis-type models need an explicit CO₂ function. Franks et al. [13] compared the responses of the model families to CO₂ across geological to decadal time scales and recommended the optimality form because it gets the Ci/Ca conservation right; they also note that the long-term response includes stomatal density and size changes that no instantaneous model captures.

Drought. The semi-empirical models respond to atmospheric drought (high D) but not to soil drought unless β is added. Where β is applied matters, as discussed above; the observed pattern is that g1 declines first and Vcmax later, which the hydraulic models reproduce because stomatal closure precedes the loss of biochemical capacity. A further failure of BWB under heat waves is its dependence on hs: at a given D, hot air has higher hs, so BWB predicts stomata opening with temperature when they are observed to close.

Night-time conductance g0. The intercept is not a fitting convenience: stomata do not shut completely at night, and measured night-time gsw is 0.01–0.05 mol m⁻² s⁻¹ in most C3 species, higher in some crops and fast-growing trees. Night-time transpiration can be 5–15% of the daily total in dry climates, and g0 also sets the daytime conductance of deeply shaded leaves, so it has a disproportionate effect on simulated canopy water loss. Many models set g0 to zero or to Collatz's 0.01 mol m⁻² s⁻¹; a recent tendency is to treat it as a separate minimum conductance rather than as the intercept of the daytime fit, because the two are not the same quantity.

Parameter estimation

From leaf gas exchange. Measure A, gsw, D and Ca on well-watered leaves across a range of light, temperature and D; regress gsw on 1.6A/(Ca√D) with intercept g0, or better, fit the non-linear form by least squares with g0 fixed at a measured night-time value, because g0 and g1 are strongly correlated in the fit. Lin et al. [8] used Bayesian non-linear fitting. Points with A < 0 or very low light are excluded. The same data give g1 for BWB (regress on A hs/Cs) and Leuning (fit g1 and D0 jointly), so model comparison on a common dataset is straightforward.

From eddy-covariance towers. Medlyn et al. [17] inverted canopy conductance (from the Penman–Monteith equation and measured λE) and GPP from FLUXNET sites to estimate an ecosystem-scale g1. The tower g1 values ranked PFTs much as the leaf data did but were systematically larger (roughly 1.3–2× for several PFTs), partly because canopy conductance includes soil and wet-canopy evaporation, partly because of the non-linear averaging over sunlit and shaded leaves, and partly because g0 is poorly constrained at the canopy scale. They also showed g1 declining with soil water deficit. This is the empirical basis for a soil-moisture modifier on g1, and a caution that leaf-fitted and canopy-fitted g1 are not interchangeable in a model.

Connections to ecosystem models

Three intercomparisons are worth knowing. Knauer et al. [15] ran BWB, Leuning and Medlyn closures in JSBACH at FLUXNET sites and found that the choice changed simulated ET and GPP by tens of percent, with the humidity formulation mattering most in dry climates, and that parameters tuned to one site transferred poorly. De Kauwe et al. [16] implemented Medlyn with Lin's g1 in CABLE and found a global reduction in simulated transpiration compared with Leuning, largest for evergreen needleleaf forests and C4 grasslands where the PFT-specific g1 is small; this is also the paper that located the β factor problem. Franks et al. [13] compared model families on their CO₂ response and argued for the optimality closure plus an explicit treatment of long-term stomatal anatomical adjustment. The consensus direction is: Medlyn-type closure with PFT g1, hydraulics rather than root-zone β for stress, and separate treatment of g0.

Exam-style questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer.

1. Write down the Ball–Woodrow–Berry, Leuning and Medlyn stomatal models. For each, derive the Ci/Ca it implies when g0 and the boundary layer are neglected, and explain what the result means for water-use efficiency.

BWB: gsw = g0 + g1A hs/Cs. Leuning: gsw = g0 + g1A/((Cs − Γ)(1 + D/D0)). Medlyn: gsw = g0 + 1.6(1 + g1/√D)A/Ca. In each case use the supply equation A = (gsw/1.6)(Ca − Ci), i.e. gsw = 1.6A/(Ca − Ci), and equate. BWB: 1.6/(Ca − Ci) = g1hs/Ca ⇒ Ci/Ca = 1 − 1.6/(g1hs). Leuning: Ci/Ca = 1 − 1.6(1 − Γ/Ca)(1 + D/D0)/g1. Medlyn: Ca/(Ca − Ci) = 1 + g1/√D ⇒ Ci/Ca = g1/(g1 + √D). In all three the ratio is independent of A and of light and temperature, and depends only on humidity and the slope parameter. Since intrinsic WUE is A/gsw = Ca(1 − Ci/Ca)/1.6, a constant Ci/Ca means iWUE ∝ Ca: stomata close in proportion as CO₂ rises, which is what tree-ring δ¹³C and FACE experiments show. The models differ in how iWUE depends on humidity: linearly in 1/hs, linearly in D, or as √D.

2. Starting from the Cowan–Farquhar optimality condition, derive the Medlyn et al. (2011) model and state the assumptions made. Interpret g1.

Cowan and Farquhar: over a day with fixed total water loss, carbon gain is maximised when ∂A/∂E = 1/λ is constant, where λ = ∂E/∂A is the marginal water cost of carbon. Instantaneously the leaf maximises A − E/λ. Assume (i) RuBP-regeneration-limited photosynthesis, A = J(Ci − Γ*)/(4(Ci + 2Γ*)); (ii) E = 1.6gscD with D as a mole fraction and no boundary layer; (iii) A = gsc(Ca − Ci). Then ∂A/∂E = (∂A/∂gsc)/(1.6D). Writing gsc = A(Ci)/(Ca − Ci) and differentiating, ∂A/∂gsc = A′(Ca − Ci)²/(A′(Ca − Ci) + A), with A′ = dA/dCi = 3JΓ*/(4(Ci + 2Γ*)²). Assuming A′(Ca − Ci) ≪ A and Ci ≫ Γ*, this reduces to ∂A/∂gsc ≈ 3Γ*(Ca − Ci)²/Ci². Setting it equal to 1.6D/λ gives (Ca − Ci)/Ci = √(1.6D/(3Γ*λ)), so Ci/Ca = g1/(g1 + √D) with g1 = √(3Γ*λ/1.6). Substituting into gsw = 1.6A/(Ca − Ci) gives gsw = 1.6(1 + g1/√D)A/Ca, and g0 is added empirically. g1 (√kPa) is proportional to √λ: a high g1 species spends water freely and keeps Ci/Ca high; a low g1 species is conservative. It should rise with Γ*, hence with temperature, and across PFTs it ranges from ≈ 1.6 (C4 grasses) to ≈ 5.8 (C3 crops). Note the model predicts the shape of the D response (gs ∝ D−1/2), not merely fitting it.

3. A land-surface model couples FvCB photosynthesis with a stomatal model and a leaf energy balance. Describe the system of equations and an algorithm for solving it. Why does a unique, stable solution exist?

Unknowns: A, Ci, gsw, Tl. Equations: (1) demand, A = min(Ac, Aj, Ap) evaluated at Ci and Tl; (2) supply, A = (Ca − Ci)/(1.6/gsw + 1.37/gbw); (3) stomatal rule, e.g. gsw = g0 + 1.6(1 + g1/√D)A/Ca with D = es(Tl) − ea; (4) energy balance, Rn = cpgH(Tl − Ta) + λE with E = gtwD/P. Algorithm (Collatz 1991): guess Tl = Ta and Ci = 0.7Ca; compute A from FvCB; compute gsw from the stomatal model; update Ci from supply (or, for fixed gs, solve the quadratic in A obtained by substituting Ci = Ca − A/gsc into each FvCB rate); iterate until Ci converges, typically with damping or a bracketed root-finder on the residual Ci − Ci′(Ci); then update Tl from the energy balance (Newton step on the residual) and repeat the inner loop. Uniqueness and stability follow because the demand curve A(Ci) is increasing and concave while the effective supply implied by the stomatal rule corresponds to a nearly fixed Ci/Ca at given D (a near-vertical line in the A–Ci plane), so the two cross exactly once. Difficulties arise at low light (A ≈ 0, gs ≈ g0, Ci → Ca) and when the min() switches limitation, which is why CLM5 uses a bracketing method rather than fixed-point iteration.

4. Explain how the three semi-empirical models respond to a doubling of atmospheric CO₂, and why the Jarvis model cannot make the same prediction. How well do the predictions match FACE observations?

In BWB, Leuning and Medlyn, gs ∝ A/Ca (or A/Cs), so gs changes by the ratio (Ae/Aa)/(Ca,e/Ca,a). With a doubling of CO₂ and a ~30–40% stimulation of A under RuBP-limited conditions, gs falls by 30–35%; the mechanism is that Ci/Ca is conserved, so the leaf needs proportionally less conductance to maintain the same drawdown fraction. In optimality terms, at higher Ca each unit of conductance buys more carbon, so the optimum shifts to lower gs at the same λ. The Jarvis model has no link to A, so its CO₂ response is whatever f5(Ca) the modeller supplies, often omitted entirely, which is why early hydrological models missed the "physiological forcing" of runoff by rising CO₂. FACE experiments across trees, crops and grasses show gs reductions averaging about 20% for +200 µmol mol⁻¹ (roughly +50% CO₂), with large variation; the coupled models reproduce the average well, but over-predict the response where stomata acclimate, where canopy conductance is dominated by boundary-layer or aerodynamic limits (tall forests, low Ω), or where longer-term anatomical adjustments (fewer, smaller stomata) are the dominant response (Franks et al. 2018).

5. Soil drying is often represented by a factor β. Compare applying β to Vcmax versus to g1, and explain how plant-hydraulic schemes change the picture.

With β on Vcmax only, A falls and gs follows through the stomatal rule, so Ci/Ca and intrinsic WUE are unchanged by drought: this contradicts observations of rising iWUE (and more positive δ¹³C) during drought, and gives too much transpiration per unit carbon. With β on g1 only, stomata close and Ci/Ca falls, iWUE rises, but photosynthetic capacity is retained; A falls only through the lower Ci, which is often too little to match observed GPP declines. Leaf-level data (e.g. Zhou et al. 2013) show the stomatal slope declining first and Vcmax later as predawn Ψ falls, so both are needed with different thresholds; De Kauwe et al. (2015) showed that CABLE's performance at drought sites depended strongly on this choice. Hydraulic schemes (Tuzet 2003; Sperry 2017; Eller 2020 SOX; CLM5 PHS) replace the root-zone θ with the leaf water potential solved from a soil–root–xylem resistance network. Stomata then respond to the actual Ψleaf, which integrates soil Ψ, root distribution, xylem conductance and the current transpiration rate; the VPD and soil-moisture responses become one mechanism, species differences are encoded in P50 and Kmax rather than a tuned β curve, and the models can represent isohydric versus anisohydric behaviour and hydraulic failure. The price is extra parameters (vulnerability curves, root hydraulic conductances) that are poorly constrained globally.

6. What is intrinsic water-use efficiency and how does it differ from instantaneous and ecosystem WUE? Show how the Medlyn model predicts each should respond to VPD and CO₂.

Intrinsic WUE is A/gsw (µmol CO₂ mol⁻¹ H₂O), a property of the stomata–biochemistry balance independent of the evaporative demand; instantaneous WUE is A/E = A/(gswD/P), which falls with D; ecosystem WUE is GPP/ET (g C kg⁻¹ H₂O), which includes soil and canopy-interception evaporation and respiration-free GPP. From Medlyn, A/gsw = Ca(1 − Ci/Ca)/1.6 = Ca√D/(1.6(g1 + √D)): iWUE rises with √D (stomata close in dry air more than A falls) and linearly with Ca. Instantaneous WUE = A/E = CaP/(1.6D) × √D/(g1 + √D) ∝ Ca/(√D(g1 + √D)), which declines with D roughly as D−1/2 to D−1. Hence the "underlying WUE" GPP√D/ET used in flux-tower analyses: Medlyn's model predicts it should be nearly constant with D at a site and proportional to Ca, which is approximately what multi-decade FLUXNET records show. Deviations identify non-stomatal evaporation or changes in g1 with soil moisture.

7. Describe how you would estimate g1 (a) from leaf gas-exchange measurements and (b) from eddy-covariance data, and why the two estimates differ.

(a) Measure A, gsw, leaf-to-air D and Ca on well-watered leaves over a range of light, temperature and humidity; exclude A ≤ 0; fit gsw = g0 + 1.6(1 + g1/√D)A/Ca by non-linear least squares, ideally fixing g0 at a measured dark value because g0 and g1 trade off. Report units (√kPa) and the D range used. (b) Invert the Penman–Monteith equation with measured λE, Rn, G, D, and an aerodynamic conductance from u* to obtain canopy conductance Gc; take GPP from NEE partitioning; use D and Ca at the reference height; fit the same equation with A → GPP and gsw → Gc, restricting to dry-canopy, daytime, growing-season periods. Medlyn et al. (2017) found canopy g1 ranked PFTs as the leaf data did but was higher, because Gc contains soil and wet-surface evaporation, the canopy integrates sunlit and shaded leaves non-linearly (shaded leaves have low A and relatively high gs), the reference-height D is lower than leaf-to-air D at the top of the canopy, and g0 is confounded with non-stomatal fluxes. The practical lesson for a modeller is to use the g1 appropriate to the scale at which the model applies it, and to treat g0 separately.

8. Compare the Jarvis, Ball–Berry-type, optimality and hydraulic classes of stomatal model in terms of assumptions, parameters, strengths and the situations in which each fails.

Jarvis (1976): product of independent empirical responses; 8–12 parameters per species; transparent and easy to constrain where data exist; fails to extrapolate (new CO₂, new climates), treats interacting responses as independent, no link to carbon. Ball–Berry/Leuning: gs ∝ A × humidity function / CO₂; 2–3 parameters; strong coupling to photosynthesis, sensible CO₂ and light responses; the humidity function is empirical (hs in BWB gives wrong temperature behaviour at fixed D), no soil-water response without β, g0 poorly defined. Optimality (Medlyn 2011): same form derived from maximising A − E/λ; one meaningful slope g1 ∝ √(Γ*λ) with PFT values from a global database; predicts the √D response and constant Ci/Ca; assumes RuBP limitation and a constant λ, so it still lacks soil-water and hydraulic behaviour and gives no mechanism for how λ is set. Hydraulic/profit-maximisation (Tuzet 2003; Sperry 2017; SOX; CLM5 PHS): gs responds to leaf water potential solved from a resistance network, or maximises gain minus hydraulic risk; needs P50, vulnerability slope, Kmax, root parameters; unifies VPD and soil-drought responses and predicts isohydry and mortality; parameters are scarce globally, the schemes are computationally heavier, and they still require an assumption about what is being optimised. In practice land-surface models are converging on a Medlyn-type closure for the humidity and CO₂ response with hydraulics supplying the water-stress term.
