Topic 2 of the SPAC section

C4 Photosynthesis

A biochemical CO₂ pump bolted onto the front of the C3 cycle: how two cell types share the work, what the pump costs in ATP, why it leaks, and why that makes C4 leaves saturate at low Ci, win in hot bright places, and need a different model in CLM, JULES and BioCro.

The one-paragraph version

C4 plants fix CO₂ twice. In the mesophyll, phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC) adds bicarbonate to PEP to form the four-carbon acid oxaloacetate, which is reduced to malate or transaminated to aspartate. The C4 acid diffuses into the bundle-sheath cells wrapped around the vein, where it is decarboxylated. The released CO₂ accumulates to roughly 10–20 times the ambient level because the bundle-sheath wall resists its escape, and Rubisco, confined to those cells, fixes it through the ordinary Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle at near-saturation and with negligible oxygenation. The three-carbon remnant (pyruvate or alanine) returns to the mesophyll, where pyruvate,orthophosphate dikinase (PPDK) remakes PEP at a cost of two ATP equivalents per cycle. The pump therefore trades about 2 extra ATP per CO₂ for the elimination of photorespiration, a steeper initial slope and saturation of the A–Ci curve by about 150–200 µbar, a CO₂ compensation point near zero, Ci/Ca ≈ 0.4 and hence about twice the water-use efficiency of C3, and higher nitrogen-use efficiency because far less Rubisco is needed. Because some CO₂ leaks back out of the bundle sheath (leakiness φ ≈ 0.2), the pump over-cycles, and that leak leaves a fingerprint in the leaf's carbon isotope ratio.

Examiner's lens

Three threads run through every C4 question: the anatomy–biochemistry match (which reaction happens in which cell and why), the energy and leak budget (2 extra ATP, leakiness, what happens at low light or cool temperatures), and the model consequences (C4 is CO₂-saturated, so land models give it a flat CO₂ response and a different temperature optimum). Be ready to derive the bundle-sheath CO₂ balance and the isotope equation, and to say what Collatz et al. 1992 simplified away.

Kranz anatomy and the division of labour

Most C4 leaves show Kranz anatomy (German for "wreath"): each vascular bundle is enclosed by a ring of large, thick-walled bundle-sheath (BS) cells, which in turn is surrounded by a layer of mesophyll (M) cells, so that no mesophyll cell is more than one or two cells from a bundle sheath. Veins are closely spaced (interveinal distance ~100 µm rather than ~200–300 µm in C3 grasses) and M and BS cells are connected by dense fields of plasmodesmata. The BS wall is often suberised, which lowers its permeability to CO₂. Rubisco, the Calvin cycle and starch synthesis are confined to the BS chloroplasts; PEPC, carbonic anhydrase (CA) and PPDK are confined to the mesophyll cytosol and chloroplasts respectively. Hatch and Slack worked this out in sugarcane in 1966 using ¹⁴CO₂ pulse-chase labelling, which showed malate and aspartate, not 3-PGA, as the first labelled products [1, 2].

The C4 cycle across two cells (NADP-ME type)

Carbonic anhydrase converts CO₂ to HCO₃⁻, the true PEPC substrate. Malate carries both carbon and reductant into the bundle sheath, so NADP-ME bundle-sheath chloroplasts need little PSII, which keeps O₂ low where Rubisco works. The dashed leak is bundle-sheath leakiness; the return of pyruvate and its re-phosphorylation by PPDK is where the extra ATP goes.

PEP carboxylase: why the first carboxylation does not photorespire

PEPC uses HCO₃⁻, not CO₂, as its substrate. Carbonic anhydrase in the mesophyll cytosol keeps CO₂ and bicarbonate at equilibrium, and at cytosolic pH ≈ 7.3 most of the dissolved inorganic carbon is bicarbonate, so the carboxylase sees a substrate pool several-fold larger than dissolved CO₂. O₂ has no structural resemblance to HCO₃⁻, so PEPC has no oxygenase activity and no O₂ sensitivity. Its Km for bicarbonate is low (the equivalent CO₂ half-saturation Kp ≈ 80 µbar in the von Caemmerer model), and the maximum activity Vpmax is typically 1.5–2 times Rubisco's Vcmax, so the enzyme pulls mesophyll CO₂ down hard. This is why C4 leaves can draw Ci to ~0.4 Ca and still saturate. PEPC is regulated by light through phosphorylation by PEPC kinase, which lowers its sensitivity to feedback inhibition by malate; the same regulation reappears, inverted in time, in CAM.

Two refinements examiners like. First, the groups are not pure: maize runs a substantial PEPCK flux alongside NADP-ME, and PEPCK is now seen as a supplementary pathway layered on either malic-enzyme type rather than a third independent design. Second, the subtypes differ ecologically: NADP-ME grasses dominate wet, high-rainfall tropical savannas, while NAD-ME grasses are over-represented in arid regions; NADP-ME species also have the highest quantum yields (≈ 0.065 mol CO₂ per absorbed photon versus ≈ 0.053–0.060 for NAD-ME and PEPCK). The return of PEP rather than pyruvate in PEPCK types saves part of the PPDK cost, which may be why it is added when energy is limiting.

The CO₂ pump: energetics and leakiness

The C4 acid cycle is a pump that moves inorganic carbon from a low concentration in the mesophyll (Cm) to a high concentration in the bundle sheath (Cs). The energy is spent regenerating PEP:

pyruvate + ATP + Pi → PEP + AMP + PPi  (PPDK) ;  AMP + ATP → 2 ADP ;  PPi → 2 Pi

Because AMP must be re-phosphorylated by adenylate kinase and the pyrophosphate is hydrolysed, each PEP costs two ATP equivalents. Added to the 3 ATP + 2 NADPH of the C3 cycle, the minimum C4 bill is 5 ATP and 2 NADPH per CO₂. In NADP-ME types the NADPH generated in the bundle sheath by malic enzyme is exactly the NADPH consumed in the mesophyll by NADP-malate dehydrogenase, so the reductant books balance and the only net extra is the ATP.

The bundle sheath is not gas-tight. CO₂ diffuses back to the mesophyll at a rate L = gbs(Cs − Cm), where gbs ≈ 1–5 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ bar⁻¹ is the bundle-sheath conductance. Leakiness is the fraction of pumped carbon that escapes:

$$\phi = \frac{L}{V_p}, \qquad \text{ATP per net CO}_2 = 3 + \frac{2}{1-\phi}$$

Every leaked molecule has been paid for by PPDK and must be pumped again, so at φ = 0.2 the cost rises to 5.5 ATP per CO₂ and at φ = 0.5 to 7. Measured leakiness in maize, sorghum and Miscanthus is typically 0.15–0.25 at high light and rises at low light, where the balance between PEPC and Rubisco activities is harder to hold [11, 12]. Leakiness is why C4 plants have a slightly lower maximum quantum yield than C3 plants when photorespiration is suppressed, and why C4 does not pay off in shade.

Why the pump needs a leaky-but-not-too-leaky sheath

If gbs were zero, no CO₂ would escape but neither could O₂ escape, and Cs would rise until Rubisco was saturated and the pump was wasting ATP. If gbs were as large as mesophyll conductance, Cs would collapse to Cm and there would be no concentrating mechanism. The observed gbs, two to three orders of magnitude below mesophyll conductance, gives Cs of 1000–3000 µbar with φ ≈ 0.2: enough to saturate Rubisco (Kc ≈ 650 µbar for C4 Rubiscos) and suppress Vo/Vc to a few percent, at a modest over-cycling cost [4].

The von Caemmerer C4 model

von Caemmerer (2000) [3] couples a PEPC rate in the mesophyll to a Rubisco (FvCB) rate in the bundle sheath through the CO₂ balance of the sheath. The PEP carboxylation rate is the lesser of an enzyme-limited and a PEP-regeneration-limited rate:

$$V_p = \min\!\left(\frac{V_{pmax}\,C_m}{C_m + K_p},\; V_{pr}\right)$$

with Cm the mesophyll CO₂ (taken equal to Ci if mesophyll conductance is ignored), Kp ≈ 80 µbar and Vpr ≈ 80 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ the ceiling set by PPDK. At steady state the carbon entering the sheath equals the carbon fixed there plus the leak, which gives two expressions for net assimilation:

$$A = V_p - L - R_m = \underbrace{V_c - 0.5\,V_o}_{\text{BS Rubisco}} - R_s, \qquad L = g_{bs}\,(C_s - C_m)$$
$$V_c - 0.5V_o = \frac{V_{cmax}\,(C_s - \gamma^* O_s)}{C_s + K_c\,(1 + O_s/K_o)}, \qquad \gamma^* = \frac{0.5}{S_{c/o}}$$

Here Rm is mesophyll mitochondrial respiration (usually set to 0.5 Rd) and Rs the bundle-sheath share. Eliminating Cs = Cm + (Vp − Rm − A)/gbs gives a quadratic in A; the smaller root is the enzyme-limited rate Ac. Replacing Vpmax by the ATP available to PPDK (xJt/2, with x ≈ 0.4 the share of electron transport serving the C4 cycle) and Vcmax by the bundle-sheath electron transport ((1 − x)Jt/3) gives the electron-transport-limited rate Aj; the leaf rate is A = min(Ac, Aj). If the bundle sheath has PSII (α > 0) the O₂ there rises above ambient and a further term appears; the explorer below sets α = 0, as is reasonable for NADP-ME grasses.

Derivation of the enzyme-limited quadratic (an examiner may ask for this)

Write X = Vp − Rm + gbsCm so that Cs = (X − A)/gbs, and K′ = Kc(1 + O/Ko). The bundle-sheath equation A + Rd = Vcmax(Cs − γ*O)/(Cs + K′) becomes, after multiplying through by gbs, (A + Rd)(X − A + gbsK′) = Vcmax(X − A − gbsγ*O). Collecting terms: A² − [X + (Vcmax − Rd) + gbsK′] A + [(Vcmax − Rd)X − Vcmaxgbsγ*O − RdgbsK′] = 0. The physically meaningful root is the smaller one. In the limit gbs → 0 it reduces to A = min(Vp − Rm, Vcmax − Rd): the leaf is either PEPC-limited (steep initial slope, set by Vpmax/Kp) or Rubisco-limited (flat plateau, set by Vcmax). That two-segment shape is exactly what Collatz et al. [5] adopted.

What the shape tells you

  • Initial slope is set by PEPC (Vpmax/Kp), not Rubisco. It is 5–10 times steeper than in C3 and is what Collatz et al. parameterise with a single constant k.
  • Saturation by 150–200 µbar Ci, well below the operating Ci of ~170 µbar at today's Ca. Elevated CO₂ therefore adds little directly; any gain comes through stomatal closure and water savings.
  • Γ ≈ 0–5 µbar. Because bundle-sheath CO₂ is high even when Ci is tiny, Vo is negligible; Γ is set by the small leak plus respiration, not by Rubisco's specificity. A C4 leaf in a closed chamber draws the air down to near zero CO₂, the classic field test.
  • Plateau equals Vcmax − Rd (or the electron-transport ceiling), so a C4 leaf achieves a given A with roughly half the Rubisco of a C3 leaf.

Temperature, light, water and nitrogen

Temperature. Photorespiration is the main reason C3 photosynthesis declines above 25–30 °C; C4 leaves escape it, so their temperature optimum lies at 30–40 °C and the gap widens with temperature. At the cold end C4 is disadvantaged: PPDK and Rubisco (of which the leaf has little) are both cold-labile, and the pump still costs 2 ATP while C3 photorespiration is small in cool air. The cleanest demonstration is the quantum yield of CO₂ uptake. Ehleringer and Björkman (1977) [7] showed that the C3 yield falls with temperature in normal air but is constant (~0.073 per absorbed photon in their plants) at 2% O₂, whereas the C4 yield is constant (~0.054) regardless of O₂ or temperature. The two curves cross near 22–25 °C at the CO₂ of the 1970s, and the crossover moves to higher temperature as CO₂ rises and to lower temperature as it falls, which is the basis of the Ehleringer, Cerling and Helliker (1997) [8] model of C4 distribution and the late-Miocene expansion of C4 grasslands under low CO₂.

In FvCB terms, the C3 quantum yield at low light is set by the electron requirement of carboxylation plus oxygenation:

$$\Phi_{C3} = \Phi_0\,\frac{C_c - \Gamma^*}{C_c + 2\Gamma^*}, \qquad \Phi_{C4} \approx \text{const} \approx 0.060\text{–}0.065$$

where Φ₀ (≈ 0.093 mol CO₂ per absorbed photon when photorespiration is suppressed) is the yield of the C3 cycle alone and Γ*(T) carries the temperature dependence. Each oxygenation costs the same electrons as a carboxylation and loses half a CO₂, hence the (Cc − Γ*)/(Cc + 2Γ*) form.

Light. Because the C4 pump costs ATP regardless of light, C4 leaves have a slightly lower quantum yield than C3 leaves without photorespiration, and leakiness rises at low light. But C4 leaves rarely light-saturate: with Rubisco saturated by CO₂, A keeps rising to full sunlight (Amax 40–60 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ in maize and Miscanthus) while C3 leaves typically saturate at 20–30 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ in half of full sun. C4 is therefore an open-habitat, high-light syndrome; there are almost no C4 trees and few C4 understorey plants.

Water. Operating at Ci/Ca ≈ 0.4 instead of 0.7 doubles the CO₂ gradient across the stomata. Writing A = gsc(Ca − Ci) and E = 1.6 gsc D/P, the intrinsic water-use efficiency A/E = Ca(1 − Ci/Ca) P/(1.6 D) is about twice the C3 value at the same vapour pressure deficit; transpiration ratios are roughly 250–350 g H₂O per g dry matter for C4 crops versus 450–950 for C3. This does not make C4 plants drought-tolerant in the physiological sense. Ghannoum (2009) [13] showed that C4 assimilation often falls under mild water stress through metabolic, not stomatal, limitation, before Ci has dropped, and that C4 grasses succeed in dry regions mostly through water saving, deep roots and high growth rates between rains rather than through tolerance of low water potential.

Nitrogen. Rubisco is 25–50% of soluble protein in a C3 leaf and only 10–25% in a C4 leaf, because a saturated enzyme needs fewer copies to deliver the same flux, and C4 Rubiscos have a higher kcat (and a higher Kc, a trade-off that is affordable at high Cs). Photosynthetic nitrogen-use efficiency is therefore 1.5–2 times higher, and C4 crops reach high yields on relatively low leaf N (1–2 g m⁻² versus 2–3 g m⁻² in C3 crops). Sugarcane and Miscanthus owe much of their bioenergy appeal to this combination of high radiation-use, water-use and nitrogen-use efficiency.

Carbon isotopes: measuring leakiness

Plants discriminate against ¹³CO₂. For a C3 leaf, Farquhar, O'Leary and Berry's simple model combines diffusive fractionation a = 4.4‰ with Rubisco fractionation b ≈ 27–29‰, weighted by how much of the supply is used:

$$\Delta_{C3} = a + (b - a)\frac{C_i}{C_a}$$

For a C4 leaf Farquhar (1983) [6] showed that the carbon reaching Rubisco has first passed through PEPC, whose effective fractionation b₄ ≈ −5.7‰ (PEPC itself ~2‰, combined with the −9‰ equilibrium enrichment of HCO₃⁻ relative to CO₂) is negative, and that Rubisco's fractionation b₃ ≈ 29–30‰ can only be expressed to the extent that CO₂ leaks back out (with a small leak fractionation s ≈ 1.8‰):

$$\Delta_{C4} = a + \left[\,b_4 + \phi\,(b_3 - s) - a\,\right]\frac{C_i}{C_a}$$

The bracket changes sign at a critical leakiness φ = (a − b₄)/(b₃ − s) ≈ (4.4 + 5.7)/(29 − 1.8) ≈ 0.37. Below it Δ decreases as Ci/Ca rises, the opposite of C3; above it Δ increases. Because most C4 leaves have φ ≈ 0.2, their Δ is small (3–5‰), their tissue δ¹³C is −11 to −14‰, and the slope of Δ against Ci/Ca is slightly negative. Measuring Δ online (with a gas-exchange system coupled to an isotope-ratio mass spectrometer or laser) together with Ci/Ca and inverting the equation is the standard way to estimate φ; refinements add ternary effects, respiration and mesophyll conductance [11, 12].

Evolution of C4

C4 photosynthesis is a textbook case of convergent evolution. Sage (2004) [9] counted at least 45 independent origins; current counts exceed 60 lineages in 19 angiosperm families, with grasses (about 18 origins, ~4,500 species), sedges and the Chenopodiaceae/Amaranthaceae the richest. The first C4 lineages appear in the Oligocene, 30–35 million years ago, shortly after atmospheric CO₂ fell below ~500 µbar, and C4 grasslands expanded dramatically in the late Miocene (8–3 Ma). The selective drivers are all conditions that increase photorespiration or the cost of water: low CO₂, high temperature, aridity, salinity and high light in open habitats, often with fire and grazing maintaining the openness. Sage, Sage and Kocacinar (2012) [10] describe the evolutionary path through intermediate states: closer veins and enlarged bundle sheaths (proto-Kranz), then relocation of glycine decarboxylase to the bundle sheath so that photorespiratory CO₂ is released there (C2 photosynthesis, a glycine shuttle that concentrates CO₂ by a factor of 2–3), and finally the up-regulation of PEPC and PPDK and the restriction of Rubisco to the sheath. The genus Flaveria displays the whole series and is the standard model system; engineering rice toward C4 attempts to retrace it.

Globally, C4 species are only ~3% of vascular plants but contribute roughly a quarter of terrestrial gross primary production, through tropical savannas and grasslands and through maize, sorghum, millet and sugarcane. Any land model that treats grassland must therefore decide, grid cell by grid cell, how much of the grass is C4; the usual rule (Collatz et al. 1998, Still et al. 2003) is a climatology of the crossover temperature, assigning C4 where the warmest-month mean exceeds ~22 °C and there is enough growing-season rain.

Connections to ecosystem models

Collatz, Ribas-Carbo and Berry (1992) [5] distilled the von Caemmerer model into three potential rates, chosen so that a maize leaf could be fitted with a handful of parameters and coupled to the Ball–Berry stomatal model:

$$A_c = V_{max}\,f(T), \qquad A_j = \alpha\,Q_{abs}, \qquad A_p = k\,\frac{C_i}{P}, \qquad A = \text{smooth}\min(A_c, A_j, A_p) - R_d$$

Ac is the Rubisco-saturated plateau (no CO₂ dependence, because the bundle sheath is saturated); Aj is the light-limited rate with α ≈ 0.05–0.067 mol CO₂ per mol photons; Ap is the PEPC-limited initial slope with k ≈ 0.7 mol m⁻² s⁻¹ at 25 °C (Q₁₀ = 2). The minimum is smoothed with two quadratic co-limitation steps (θ = 0.83, β = 0.93 in the original). Vmax has a Q₁₀ of 2 with high- and low-temperature inhibition functions, 1/[1 + exp(0.3(T − 36))] and 1/[1 + exp(0.3(13 − T))], which give the C4 optimum of ~35 °C and the cold-sensitivity. This model, essentially unchanged, is the C4 option in SiB2, CLM (CLM4.5/CLM5 write Ap = 20000 Vcmax Ci/Patm and use θ = 0.80, 0.95), JULES, Noah-MP and BioCro, whose C4 leaf routine is Collatz coupled to Ball–Berry and was parameterised for Miscanthus (Vmax ≈ 39, α ≈ 0.04) and later for sugarcane and maize [14]. ORCHIDEE instead implements the Yin and Struik (2009) analytical C4 model, and Agro-IBIS and DayCent-style crop models use Collatz or an empirical radiation-use-efficiency equivalent.

Three modelling consequences follow. (i) CO₂ response: a C4 PFT shows almost no direct fertilisation; projected C4 productivity gains under elevated CO₂ come through reduced gs and soil water conservation, so the stomatal coupling matters more than the biochemistry. (ii) Temperature: the C4 optimum is 8–10 °C warmer, so warming shifts the C3/C4 balance, and models that use a fixed crossover climatology cannot capture the CO₂-dependence of that crossover. (iii) Nitrogen: C4 Vcmax per unit N is higher, so N-based Vcmax schemes need a pathway-specific slope. The simplifications to remember when criticising Collatz are the loss of leakiness (no over-cycling cost, so low-light efficiency is overestimated), the loss of any Kp (initial slope is linear to the origin, Γ = 0), and the absence of mesophyll conductance.

Exam-style questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer.

1. Explain why PEP carboxylase is insensitive to O₂ and what that buys the C4 leaf.

PEPC carboxylates PEP with bicarbonate, HCO₃⁻, formed from CO₂ by carbonic anhydrase in the mesophyll cytosol. Its active site binds an ionic substrate with no steric or electronic resemblance to O₂, so there is no competing oxygenase reaction, unlike Rubisco, whose enediol intermediate of RuBP reacts with either CO₂ or O₂. The leaf therefore gains a first carboxylation that is unaffected by atmospheric O₂, that draws on the larger bicarbonate pool (most dissolved inorganic carbon at pH 7.3) and has a low effective Km (Kp ≈ 80 µbar CO₂ equivalent). This lets PEPC deplete mesophyll CO₂ to ~0.4 Ca while still running near saturation, and it supplies the C4 acid flux that concentrates CO₂ where Rubisco sits. Rubisco itself is unchanged: it still oxygenates, but at Cs ≈ 2000 µbar the ratio Vo/Vc = 2Γ*/Cs is only a few percent.

2. Derive the ATP cost of C4 photosynthesis per net CO₂ fixed as a function of leakiness, and comment on what happens at low light.

The C3 cycle costs 3 ATP and 2 NADPH per CO₂. Each turn of the C4 cycle regenerates PEP via PPDK (pyruvate + ATP + Pi → PEP + AMP + PPi); restoring AMP to ADP with adenylate kinase consumes a second ATP and the PPi is hydrolysed, so each PEP costs 2 ATP equivalents. If a fraction φ of the CO₂ delivered to the bundle sheath leaks back, only (1 − φ)Vp is fixed, so Vp = A/(1 − φ) turns of the pump are needed per A molecules fixed. Cost per net CO₂ = 3 + 2/(1 − φ): 5 ATP at φ = 0, 5.5 at φ = 0.2, 7 at φ = 0.5. NADPH is unaffected because the NADP-MDH/NADP-ME pair is reductant-neutral. At low light ATP supply limits both PEPC (through PPDK) and Rubisco; coordination weakens and φ rises toward 0.3–0.5, so the energy cost per CO₂ climbs just when energy is scarce. This is why the C4 quantum yield is below the C3 yield in the absence of photorespiration, and why C4 plants are rare in shade.

3. Write the bundle-sheath CO₂ balance of the von Caemmerer model and show how it produces an A–Ci curve that saturates at low Ci with a near-zero compensation point.

Carbon enters the sheath at the PEP carboxylation rate Vp = min(VpmaxCm/(Cm + Kp), Vpr) and leaves either by Rubisco net fixation Vc − 0.5Vo = Vcmax(Cs − γ*O)/(Cs + Kc(1 + O/Ko)) or by leakage L = gbs(Cs − Cm). At steady state Vp − Rm = (Vc − 0.5Vo) + L, and A = Vp − L − Rm = Vc − 0.5Vo − Rs. Solving for Cs and substituting yields a quadratic in A whose smaller root is Ac. Behaviour: at low Cm, Vp rises steeply (slope Vpmax/Kp) and almost all of it is fixed because Rubisco is far from saturation, so A ≈ Vp − Rm, a steep initial slope. As Cm approaches 150–200 µbar, Vp exceeds what Rubisco can fix; Cs rises until the leak absorbs the excess and A plateaus at ≈ Vcmax − Rd. The compensation point is where Vp − Rm balances L plus respiration; since Cs remains far above γ*O even as Cm → 0, photorespiratory loss is negligible and Γ is set only by respiration against a steep PEPC slope, giving 0–5 µbar. With gbs large the two cells merge, Cs → Cm and the curve degenerates to the C3 form.

4. The quantum yields of C3 and C4 leaves cross at about 22–25 °C in late-20th-century air. Explain the mechanism and how the crossover depends on CO₂, O₂ and C4 subtype.

At low light A is limited by electron transport, so the quantum yield is the inverse of the electron (photon) requirement per net CO₂. In C3 leaves each oxygenation consumes as many electrons as a carboxylation and loses half a CO₂, giving ΦC3 = Φ₀(Cc − Γ*)/(Cc + 2Γ*). Γ* rises roughly exponentially with temperature (Sc/o falls and CO₂ solubility declines faster than O₂), so ΦC3 declines with warming; at 2% O₂ Γ* is tiny and ΦC3 is flat at ≈ 0.09. C4 leaves have no photorespiration but pay a fixed 2 ATP per CO₂ (more with leakiness), so ΦC4 ≈ 0.06 independent of temperature and O₂. The crossover is where Φ₀(Cc − Γ*)/(Cc + 2Γ*) = ΦC4. Raising Ca (and Cc) reduces Γ*/Cc and pushes the crossover to warmer temperatures (above 35 °C at 700 µbar); lowering CO₂ to glacial 180–200 µbar brings it below 15 °C, which is the argument for C4 expansion under low Miocene and glacial CO₂. Lower O₂ favours C3 everywhere. Among C4 subtypes, NADP-ME has the highest yield (~0.065) and the lowest crossover; NAD-ME (~0.053) crosses at a warmer temperature, consistent with its distribution in hotter, drier sites.

5. Starting from the Farquhar (1983) equation, explain how bundle-sheath leakiness is estimated from carbon isotope discrimination and why the slope of Δ against Ci/Ca changes sign at φ ≈ 0.37.

Δ = a + [b₄ + φ(b₃ − s) − a] Ci/Ca, with a = 4.4‰ (diffusion in air), b₄ ≈ −5.7‰ (PEPC fractionation of ~2‰ combined with the ~−9‰ equilibrium enrichment of HCO₃⁻ over CO₂ at 25 °C), b₃ ≈ 29‰ (Rubisco) and s ≈ 1.8‰ (leak of dissolved CO₂). Only the fraction φ of carbon that re-escapes allows Rubisco's preference for ¹²C to be expressed in the leaf; when φ = 0 every molecule delivered is fixed and Δ is set by diffusion and PEPC alone. The slope coefficient b₄ + φ(b₃ − s) − a is zero when φ = (a − b₄)/(b₃ − s) ≈ 10.1/27.2 ≈ 0.37: below this, more open stomata (higher Ci/Ca) expose carbon mostly to the negatively fractionating PEPC step, so Δ falls; above it the Rubisco term dominates and Δ rises, as in C3. To estimate φ, Δ is measured online during gas exchange (CO₂ entering and leaving the cuvette analysed by IRMS or laser spectroscopy) alongside Ci/Ca, and the equation is inverted; full treatments add ternary corrections, respiratory and photorespiratory fractionation and mesophyll conductance (Ubierna et al., Kromdijk et al.). Typical results: φ ≈ 0.2 at high light, higher at low light and low temperature.

6. Compare the direct and indirect responses of C3 and C4 crops to elevated CO₂ and to drought, and state how a land-surface model would represent each.

Direct CO₂ response: C3 leaves operate on the steep, Rubisco-limited part of the A–Ci curve with photorespiration suppressing A, so doubling CO₂ raises A by 30–50% at the leaf scale; C4 leaves are saturated by ~200 µbar Ci, so direct stimulation is near zero. Indirect: both close stomata (gs falls ~20–30%), conserving soil water, so in dry years C4 crops do gain under elevated CO₂ through delayed drought stress (FACE results for sorghum and maize). Drought: C4 has intrinsically higher A/E because of low Ci/Ca, but its A is sensitive to mild water stress through metabolic limitation (Ghannoum 2009), and the steep initial slope means that any further Ci reduction costs little A until stomata nearly close. In a model such as CLM: the C3 PFT uses FvCB, where Ci enters the Rubisco- and RuBP-limited rates, so CO₂ fertilisation emerges; the C4 PFT uses Collatz 1992, where only Ap = kCi depends on CO₂ and is rarely limiting, so direct fertilisation is absent and the CO₂ effect is carried entirely by the stomatal model (Ball–Berry or Medlyn) and soil-moisture stress factor β. Models that omit the β-dependence of Vmax or treat C4 drought response only through gs will miss the metabolic limitation.

7. What anatomical and biochemical steps are thought to have led from C3 to C4, and which environmental conditions selected for each step?

Sage et al. (2012) describe a sequence. (1) Preconditioning: increased vein density and larger bundle-sheath cells (proto-Kranz), favoured in hot, high-light, often saline or arid open habitats, perhaps initially for hydraulic reasons. (2) Relocation of glycine decarboxylase to bundle-sheath mitochondria, so that glycine from photorespiration in the mesophyll is decarboxylated in the sheath: this "C2" or glycine-shuttle photosynthesis raises sheath CO₂ two- to three-fold and lowers Γ to 10–30 µbar. It is selected only when photorespiration is high, i.e. under low atmospheric CO₂ (below ~500 µbar, reached in the Oligocene), heat and drought-induced stomatal closure. (3) Up-regulation of PEPC, PPDK and carbonic anhydrase in the mesophyll and decarboxylases in the sheath, initially to recapture the CO₂ leaking from the C2 shuttle, giving C3–C4 intermediates with partial C4 cycles. (4) Loss of Rubisco from the mesophyll, kinetic optimisation of Rubisco (higher kcat, higher Kc) and PEPC (lower malate sensitivity), and tightening of the sheath wall. Each step has small positive fitness in the right environment, which explains the extraordinary number (> 60) of independent origins concentrated in warm, open, low-CO₂ settings, and why C4 is absent from cool, shaded and aquatic habitats where photorespiration is low or light is scarce.

8. You are asked to add a C4 bioenergy grass to an ecosystem model that currently has only C3 FvCB. List the minimum set of changes and the parameter values you would start with.

(i) Replace the leaf biochemistry with a C4 scheme: either Collatz 1992 (Vmax,25 ≈ 30–40 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for Miscanthus, 40–60 for maize; α ≈ 0.04–0.06 mol CO₂ mol⁻¹ photons; k ≈ 0.7 mol m⁻² s⁻¹; θ = 0.83, β = 0.93; Q₁₀ = 2 with low- and high-temperature inhibition around 13 and 36 °C; Rd ≈ 0.01–0.02 Vmax) or the fuller von Caemmerer model (Vpmax ≈ 120, Vcmax ≈ 60, gbs ≈ 3 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ bar⁻¹, Kp ≈ 80 µbar, Jmax ≈ 400) if leakiness or isotopes matter. (ii) Change the stomatal model's slope: in Ball–Berry the C4 slope m ≈ 4 versus 9 for C3, or in Medlyn g₁ ≈ 1.6 versus 4, reflecting Ci/Ca ≈ 0.4. (iii) Adjust the N–Vcmax relationship (higher Vmax per unit N) and the specific leaf area and leaf N targets. (iv) Change the thermal time and phenology (base temperature ~10 °C, high optimum) and the canopy radiation-use efficiency (~ 4 g DM MJ⁻¹ intercepted PAR for Miscanthus, about 1.5 × a C3 crop). (v) Check the CO₂ response: direct leaf stimulation should be near zero, with gains only via water savings. BioCro and Agro-IBIS are examples that followed exactly this route for Miscanthus, maize and sugarcane.
