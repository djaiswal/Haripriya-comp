Topic 1 of the SPAC section

C3 Photosynthesis

How a C3 leaf turns photons and CO₂ into triose phosphate, why Rubisco makes that process leaky, and how those two facts produce the A–Ci curve that every leaf-to-ecosystem model is built on.

The one-paragraph version

Light reactions in the thylakoid split water, pump protons, and deliver electrons to NADP⁺, producing ATP and NADPH. The Calvin–Benson–Bassham (CBB) cycle in the stroma spends them to fix CO₂: Rubisco adds CO₂ to the five-carbon sugar RuBP and the product immediately splits into two molecules of the three-carbon acid 3-phosphoglycerate. That first stable product is why the pathway is called "C3". Three turns of the cycle (3 CO₂, 9 ATP, 6 NADPH) export one triose phosphate and regenerate the RuBP. Rubisco also reacts with O₂, and the salvage pathway for that mistake (photorespiration) releases roughly one CO₂ for every two oxygenations. Because C3 leaves expose Rubisco directly to atmospheric CO₂ and O₂, their assimilation rate is sensitive to CO₂, O₂ and temperature in exactly the way the Farquhar–von Caemmerer–Berry (FvCB) model describes.

Examiner's lens

Expect to be asked to move between scales: from a reaction (Rubisco carboxylation) to a leaf-level flux (A), to a model parameter (Vcmax, Jmax, Γ*), to an ecosystem prediction (CO₂ fertilisation, temperature response). Every section below ends with that link.

Where carbon enters: a diffusion plus biochemistry system

CO₂ reaches Rubisco by diffusing from the bulk air (Ca) through the boundary layer and stomata to the intercellular air spaces (Ci), then dissolving and crossing the cell wall, plasma membrane, cytosol and chloroplast envelope to the stroma (Cc). At steady state the same flux passes every stage:

$$A = g_{sc}\,(C_a - C_i) = g_m\,(C_i - C_c), \qquad g_{sc} = g_{sw}/1.6$$

The factor 1.6 is the ratio of the diffusivities of water vapour and CO₂ in air. Because stomatal conductance is normally measured for water vapour, this conversion appears everywhere in coupled photosynthesis–conductance models. In a C3 leaf under typical conditions Ci/Ca ≈ 0.7 and Cc is a further 50–100 µbar lower, so Rubisco sees roughly 200–280 µbar CO₂ when the air is at 420 µbar. That gap is the root of almost every C3 property discussed on this page: Rubisco operates well below its Kc, so carboxylation is substrate-limited and O₂ competes effectively.

The biochemistry sets the demand for CO₂ (how fast the chloroplast can use it at a given Cc); diffusion sets the supply. The operating point is where the two agree. Keep this picture in mind for the stomatal-conductance and canopy pages: they are all about the supply side, while this page is about the demand side.

Light reactions: making ATP and NADPH

Two photosystems work in series. Photosystem II (P680) uses photon energy to pull electrons out of water, releasing O₂ and protons into the lumen. The electrons pass through plastoquinone (PQ), the cytochrome b₆f complex (which pumps more protons across the membrane), and plastocyanin (PC) to photosystem I (P700). A second photon lifts them to a very negative redox potential, and ferredoxin (Fd) and ferredoxin–NADP⁺ reductase (FNR) deliver them to NADP⁺. The proton gradient drives ATP synthase. Plotted against redox potential the path draws a "Z", hence the Z-scheme.

Z-scheme: linear electron transport

Each dot is one electron. Two photons (one per photosystem) move one electron from H₂O to NADP⁺; four electrons per O₂ released; two electrons per NADPH. The dashed return from Fd to PQ is cyclic electron flow, which makes ATP without NADPH.

What the Calvin cycle demands from the light reactions

Per CO₂ fixed the cycle consumes 3 ATP and 2 NADPH, an ATP:NADPH ratio of 1.5. Linear electron flow alone yields about 1.28 ATP per NADPH (with 4 H⁺ per ATP and 14 c-subunits in the ATP synthase ring, 12 H⁺ per 2 NADPH gives ~2.57 ATP), so some additional ATP must come from cyclic electron flow around PSI, the Q-cycle, or the water–water cycle. Photorespiration raises the ATP demand further. This balance is why the ATP-limited form of the RuBP-regeneration equation, J(Cc − Γ*)/(4.5 Cc + 10.5 Γ*), exists alongside the NADPH-limited form with 4 and 8.

In FvCB the variable J (electron transport rate, µmol e⁻ m⁻² s⁻¹) summarises the whole thylakoid. It saturates at Jmax and depends on absorbed irradiance through a non-rectangular hyperbola:

$$J = \frac{I_2 + J_{max} - \sqrt{(I_2 + J_{max})^2 - 4\theta I_2 J_{max}}}{2\theta}, \qquad I_2 = Q \cdot \alpha_{abs}\,\frac{1-f}{2}$$

with absorptance αabs ≈ 0.85, a spectral-quality correction f ≈ 0.15, the factor ½ because two photosystems share the photons, and curvature θ ≈ 0.7–0.9. The quantum requirement is therefore at least 8 photons per CO₂ (4 e⁻ × 2 photosystems), and in practice 9–10 at low O₂ and 12–14 in air once photorespiration is paid for.

The Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle

The cycle has three phases. Carboxylation: Rubisco (ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase) adds CO₂ to RuBP; the six-carbon intermediate hydrolyses to two 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA). Reduction: 3-PGA is phosphorylated by ATP (PGA kinase) and reduced by NADPH (G3P dehydrogenase) to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P, a triose phosphate). Regeneration: five of every six G3P are rearranged by aldolase, transketolase, and phosphatases into three ribulose-5-phosphate, which phosphoribulokinase (PRK) phosphorylates with ATP back to RuBP. Per CO₂: 3 ATP and 2 NADPH, or 4 electrons through the chain (2 per NADPH). This 4 e⁻ per carboxylation is where the "4" in Aj = J(Cc − Γ*)/(4Cc + 8Γ*) comes from. The "8" arrives in the next section.

Two regulatory facts worth knowing for the exam. Rubisco must be activated by carbamylation of a lysine and Mg²⁺ binding, maintained by Rubisco activase, an ATP-dependent enzyme that is itself heat-sensitive and is one explanation for the decline of photosynthesis above ~35 °C. Several cycle enzymes (G3P dehydrogenase, FBPase, SBPase, PRK) are switched on in the light by the ferredoxin–thioredoxin system, so the cycle runs only when electrons are flowing.

Rubisco and photorespiration

Rubisco is slow (kcat ≈ 3 s⁻¹ per active site), which is why it can be 25–50% of soluble leaf protein and a large share of leaf nitrogen. More importantly it cannot fully discriminate between CO₂ and O₂. The oxygenation of RuBP produces one 3-PGA and one 2-phosphoglycolate, a two-carbon compound the cycle cannot use. The C2 (photorespiratory) pathway salvages it across three organelles: chloroplast (2-PG → glycolate), peroxisome (glycolate → glyoxylate → glycine), mitochondrion (2 glycine → serine + CO₂ + NH₃ via glycine decarboxylase), back through the peroxisome (serine → hydroxypyruvate → glycerate) to the chloroplast (glycerate → 3-PGA). Net: two oxygenations lose one carbon as CO₂, release one NH₃ that must be re-assimilated at a cost of ATP and reductant, and recover one 3-PGA.

Both reactions follow Michaelis–Menten kinetics with competitive inhibition by the other gas:

$$V_c = \frac{V_{cmax}\,C_c}{C_c + K_c\left(1 + O/K_o\right)}, \qquad \frac{V_o}{V_c} = \frac{1}{S_{c/o}}\frac{O}{C_c} = \frac{2\,\Gamma^*}{C_c}$$

Sc/o is the specificity factor, a property of the enzyme (and of temperature). Γ* is the Cc at which the CO₂ released by photorespiration (0.5 Vo) exactly equals the CO₂ fixed (Vc), so net chloroplast CO₂ exchange is zero in the absence of mitochondrial respiration: Γ* = 0.5 O / Sc/o. Because 0.5 Vo is released per carboxylation-unit of enzyme activity, the net carboxylation per unit Vc is 1 − 0.5 Vo/Vc = 1 − Γ*/Cc, giving the universal FvCB form

$$A = V_c\left(1 - \frac{\Gamma^*}{C_c}\right) - R_d = V_c\,\frac{C_c - \Gamma^*}{C_c} - R_d.$$

Three consequences follow directly from the kinetics and the explorer. Temperature raises Γ* (Sc/o declines, and CO₂ solubility falls faster than O₂ solubility), so photorespiration takes a growing share as leaves warm. Low Cc, as under drought when stomata close, has the same effect. High CO₂ suppresses oxygenation and simultaneously relieves substrate limitation, which is why C3 plants, unlike C4 plants, respond strongly to CO₂ enrichment [10]. Walker et al. [6] estimate photorespiration costs US wheat and soybean 20–36% of potential yield, and the fraction is largest in warm, dry years.

From enzyme kinetics to an A–Ci curve

FvCB [1] states that at any moment the carboxylation rate is set by whichever of three processes is slowest. Substituting each into A = Vc(1 − Γ*/Cc) − Rd:

$$A_c = \frac{V_{cmax}\,(C_c - \Gamma^*)}{C_c + K_c(1 + O/K_o)} - R_d \quad\text{(Rubisco-limited)}$$
$$A_j = \frac{J\,(C_c - \Gamma^*)}{4C_c + 8\Gamma^*} - R_d \quad\text{(RuBP-regeneration, i.e. electron-transport, limited)}$$
$$A_p = 3\,T_p - R_d \quad\text{(triose-phosphate-utilisation limited)}$$
$$A = \min(A_c,\; A_j,\; A_p)$$
Where do the 4 and the 8 come from? (derivation examiners like)

Each carboxylation produces two 3-PGA which need 2 NADPH = 4 e⁻ to reduce. Each oxygenation produces one 3-PGA plus one 2-PG; reducing the 3-PGA and processing the glycolate back to 3-PGA also costs the equivalent of 4 e⁻ in the original FvCB accounting. So J = 4Vc + 4Vo. Substitute Vo = 2Γ*Vc/Cc: J = 4Vc(1 + 2Γ*/Cc), so Vc = J Cc/(4Cc + 8Γ*). Multiply by (Cc − Γ*)/Cc and subtract Rd. If instead ATP is limiting, 3 ATP per carboxylation and 3.5 per oxygenation give 4.5 and 10.5 (von Caemmerer 2000 [2]).

Reading a measured curve

  • Initial slope (Ci below ~250 µbar) is Rubisco-limited and gives Vcmax; its curvature comes from Kc(1 + O/Ko).
  • The bend and plateau are RuBP-regeneration-limited and give J (hence Jmax under saturating light). A flat or slightly declining tail at very high Ci signals TPU limitation.
  • x-intercept is the CO₂ compensation point Γ, which exceeds Γ* by roughly RdKc(1 + O/Ko)/Vcmax. Γ* itself is found from the common intersection of A–Ci curves measured at several irradiances (the Laisk method).
  • Leaves usually operate near the Ac/Aj crossover at ambient CO₂, which is the co-limitation that optimal-allocation arguments predict and that gives Jmax/Vcmax ≈ 1.6–2.0 at 25 °C [4, 11].
  • Fitting C3 curves properly requires deciding which points belong to which limitation and accounting for gm; see Sharkey et al. [5].

Why C3 behaves the way it does, and where C4 and CAM step in

Every environmental response of C3 photosynthesis traces to Rubisco being exposed to the ambient CO₂/O₂ mixture at a Cc well below Kc. CO₂: A rises steeply with Ci and has not saturated at today's 420 µbar. Temperature: Vcmax and Jmax rise with T, but so do Kc and Γ*, so the optimum of A is cooler than the optimum of Vcmax and shifts upward under elevated CO₂. Drought and high VPD: stomatal closure lowers Ci, increasing the photorespiratory share and lowering A per unit Rubisco. Nitrogen: Vcmax scales with leaf N because Rubisco is a large N sink, which is the basis of the N-based Vcmax parameterisations in land-surface models [12].

C4 and CAM plants add a CO₂-concentrating mechanism in front of Rubisco (spatially in C4, temporally in CAM) so that Cc is several thousand µbar and oxygenation nearly vanishes, at an extra cost of about 2 ATP per CO₂. Exam-style questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer.

1. Why is the pathway called "C3", and in what sense do C4 plants also use it?

The first stable product of CO₂ fixation is the three-carbon acid 3-phosphoglycerate, formed when the six-carbon product of Rubisco carboxylation of RuBP splits in two. C4 plants first fix CO₂ into a four-carbon acid (oxaloacetate, via PEP carboxylase in the mesophyll), but that acid is only a shuttle: it is decarboxylated in the bundle sheath and the released CO₂ is refixed by Rubisco through the same Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle. Every photosynthetic organism reduces carbon through the C3 cycle; C4 and CAM are CO₂-concentrating front ends.

2. Derive the ATP and NADPH requirement per CO₂ fixed and explain why the ratio matters for the thylakoid.

Per 3 CO₂: carboxylation makes 6 × 3-PGA; reduction costs 6 ATP (PGA kinase) and 6 NADPH (G3P dehydrogenase); regeneration of 3 RuBP from 5 G3P costs 3 ATP (PRK). Total 9 ATP + 6 NADPH per 3 CO₂, i.e. 3 ATP and 2 NADPH per CO₂, ratio 1.5. Linear electron flow produces roughly 1.28 ATP per NADPH (12 H⁺ per 2 NADPH with Q-cycle; 4.67 H⁺ per ATP with a 14-subunit c-ring), so the deficit is met by cyclic electron flow around PSI or other alternative sinks. Photorespiration and nitrate assimilation increase the ATP demand further, which is why ATP-limited and NADPH-limited versions of Aj exist.

3. Define Γ* and explain how it differs from the CO₂ compensation point Γ. How would you measure each?

Γ* is the chloroplast CO₂ partial pressure at which photorespiratory CO₂ release (0.5 Vo) equals carboxylation (Vc), so net chloroplast CO₂ exchange is zero in the absence of mitochondrial respiration: Γ* = 0.5 O/Sc/o. Γ is the Ci at which net leaf CO₂ exchange is zero including Rd; since Rd must also be covered, Γ > Γ*, approximately Γ ≈ Γ* + RdKc(1 + O/Ko)/Vcmax. Γ is simply the x-intercept of a measured A–Ci curve. Γ* is the Ci where A–Ci curves measured at different irradiances intersect (Laisk method), because at that point A is independent of Vc; the common A at the intersection estimates −Rd. Strictly the method gives Ci*, which must be corrected for gm to obtain Γ*.

4. Why does rising atmospheric CO₂ stimulate C3 photosynthesis but barely affect C4?

In C3 leaves Cc (≈ 250 µbar) is far below the effective Km of Rubisco for CO₂ in air (≈ 700 µbar), so more CO₂ increases Vc almost linearly (substrate effect) and at the same time lowers Vo/Vc = 2Γ*/Cc (suppression of photorespiration). Both terms in A = Vc(1 − Γ*/Cc) − Rd improve. Under Rubisco limitation the stimulation is largest; under RuBP-regeneration limitation it is smaller but still positive through the Γ* term. In C4 leaves Rubisco already operates at several thousand µbar in the bundle sheath, so it is CO₂-saturated and photorespiration is negligible; any C4 response to elevated CO₂ comes indirectly, through reduced stomatal conductance and improved water status. Long-term responses are moderated by acclimation (down-regulation of Vcmax) and nutrient limitation [10].

5. Explain mechanistically why photorespiration increases with temperature, and what this implies for the temperature optimum of C3 photosynthesis.

Three effects act in the same direction. (i) Rubisco specificity Sc/o declines with temperature, raising Γ*. (ii) The solubility of CO₂ in water falls faster than that of O₂ as temperature rises, so the dissolved O₂/CO₂ ratio at the enzyme increases. (iii) Kc rises faster with temperature than Ko (activation energies ≈ 79 vs 36 kJ mol⁻¹), so the effective Km for CO₂ increases and Vc becomes more substrate-limited. Consequently the fraction of Rubisco reactions that are oxygenations and the CO₂ loss per carboxylation both climb. Although Vcmax keeps increasing to ~40 °C, net A peaks earlier (typically 20–30 °C) and the optimum shifts to higher temperatures under elevated CO₂, which suppresses the oxygenation term. Above ~35 °C Rubisco activase and membrane integrity add further limitations.

6. Sketch an A–Ci curve and state what each region reveals.

Starting at A = −Rd near Ci = 0, A rises steeply and nearly linearly through Γ (x-intercept, ~45 µbar) because carboxylation is Rubisco-limited; the slope and curvature give Vcmax given Kc, Ko, Γ*. Around 250–400 µbar the curve bends as regeneration of RuBP (electron transport, J) becomes limiting, and A approaches a plateau whose height is set by J (so Jmax at saturating light). If A flattens completely or declines at very high Ci, triose-phosphate utilisation limits. The ambient operating point (Ci ≈ 290 µbar) typically sits near the Rubisco/RuBP transition. Switching the leaf to 2% O₂ steepens the initial slope and raises the plateau because Γ* and the competitive inhibition term shrink, which confirms the role of photorespiration.

7. Trace the photorespiratory (C2) pathway and account for its carbon and nitrogen costs.

Oxygenation of RuBP yields 3-PGA plus 2-phosphoglycolate. In the chloroplast 2-PG is dephosphorylated to glycolate, which moves to the peroxisome and is oxidised to glyoxylate (producing H₂O₂, removed by catalase) and transaminated to glycine. In the mitochondrion glycine decarboxylase and serine hydroxymethyltransferase convert two glycines to one serine, releasing one CO₂ and one NH₃ and reducing NAD⁺. Serine returns to the peroxisome, is converted to hydroxypyruvate then glycerate, which enters the chloroplast and is phosphorylated (1 ATP) to 3-PGA. Balance per two oxygenations: 4 carbons in two 2-PG become 3 carbons in one 3-PGA plus 1 CO₂, so 25% of the carbon diverted is lost and net fixation falls by 0.5 CO₂ per oxygenation. The NH₃ is re-assimilated by glutamine synthetase/GOGAT at 1 ATP and 1 reductant equivalent, and the glycerate kinase step costs 1 ATP, so the pathway consumes about 3.5 ATP and 2 NADPH-equivalents per oxygenation.

8. A land-surface model represents C3 photosynthesis with FvCB. What are the three or four most uncertain inputs, and how do they connect to the biochemistry on this page?

(i) Vcmax,25: the Rubisco capacity, usually predicted from leaf N or plant functional type, with large within-type variance [12]. (ii) Its temperature response and acclimation (activation and deactivation energies, entropy term), which set how Kc, Γ* and Vcmax trade off as climate warms. (iii) Jmax/Vcmax and the light-response curvature, which decide where the Rubisco/RuBP transition sits relative to the operating Ci. (iv) Mesophyll conductance, which most models omit (Cc = Ci) and which biases fitted Vcmax and the apparent temperature response. Coupled to a stomatal model, these determine simulated CO₂ fertilisation and transpiration, so their uncertainty propagates straight into projections of the land carbon sink [13].
