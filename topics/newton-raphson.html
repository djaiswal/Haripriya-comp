The Newton–Raphson Method

Follow the tangent line to where it crosses zero, and repeat. That one idea solves nonlinear equations, minimises likelihoods, fits logistic regressions, finds the leaf's intercellular CO₂ and temperature, and sits inside every implicit soil-water solver.

The one-paragraph version

To solve \(f(x) = 0\), Newton's method replaces \(f\) near the current guess \(x_k\) by its tangent line and takes the tangent's zero as the next guess: \(x_{k+1} = x_k - f(x_k)/f'(x_k)\). When the root is simple (\(f'(x^*)\ne0\)), \(f''\) is continuous, and the start is close enough, the error squares at each step (quadratic convergence: the number of correct digits roughly doubles), which is why four to six iterations usually suffice. Far from the root the method can overshoot, cycle or diverge, at a multiple root it slows to linear convergence, and which root it finds depends on the start in an intricate way (basins of attraction), so practical codes safeguard it with bracketing (bisection, Brent) or damping. Applied to \(\nabla f = 0\) it becomes Newton's method for optimisation, \(x_{k+1} = x_k - H^{-1}\nabla f\), the minimiser of the local quadratic model, with Levenberg–Marquardt and trust regions as the damped forms, BFGS as the derivative-light form, Gauss–Newton as the least-squares form, and iteratively reweighted least squares (Fisher scoring) as the form that fits every generalised linear model. For systems of equations the derivative becomes a Jacobian, and that is how a leaf model solves for \(C_i\) and \(T_{leaf}\) simultaneously and how Richards' equation is advanced implicitly in time.

Examiner's lens

You will be asked to derive the iteration from a Taylor expansion, to prove (or sketch) quadratic convergence and state its conditions, to describe at least two failure modes, and to connect the method to something you use: IRLS for logistic regression, Levenberg–Marquardt for FvCB fitting, or the Newton loop inside a coupled leaf or soil-water model. Knowing why the secant method has order 1.618 and why bisection is the safe fallback earns the extra marks.

Root finding: derivation, geometry and quadratic convergence

Suppose \(f\) is twice continuously differentiable and \(x^*\) is a root, \(f(x^*) = 0\). Expand \(f\) about the current iterate \(x_k\), with \(e_k = x_k - x^*\) the current error:

$$0 = f(x^*) = f(x_k) + f'(x_k)(x^* - x_k) + \tfrac12 f''(\xi_k)(x^* - x_k)^2, \qquad \xi_k \text{ between } x_k \text{ and } x^*.$$

Dropping the second-order term and solving the linear remainder for \(x^*\) gives the Newton–Raphson iteration

$$x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{f(x_k)}{f'(x_k)}.$$

Geometrically, \(y = f(x_k) + f'(x_k)(x - x_k)\) is the tangent line at \(x_k\), and \(x_{k+1}\) is where it crosses the \(x\)-axis. Newton (1669) applied the idea to polynomials by successive substitution; Raphson (1690) wrote it as the iteration we use; Simpson (1740) extended it to general functions and to systems; Ypma (1995) [2] traces the history.

Quadratic convergence: proof sketch

Keep the second-order term this time. From the expansion, \(f(x_k) = f'(x_k)e_k - \tfrac12 f''(\xi_k)e_k^2\). Substitute into the iteration:

$$e_{k+1} = x_{k+1} - x^* = e_k - \frac{f'(x_k)e_k - \tfrac12 f''(\xi_k)e_k^2}{f'(x_k)} = \frac{f''(\xi_k)}{2f'(x_k)}\,e_k^2 .$$

Near the root \(f''(\xi_k)/2f'(x_k)\to f''(x^*)/2f'(x^*) =: C\), so \(|e_{k+1}|\approx C\,e_k^2\): quadratic convergence with asymptotic constant \(C = |f''(x^*)/2f'(x^*)|\). The conditions are exactly those used: (i) \(f'(x^*)\ne0\), a simple root, so the division is safe and \(C\) is finite; (ii) \(f''\) continuous (bounded) near \(x^*\); (iii) the start close enough that \(C|e_0| \lt 1\), since then \(C|e_1| \le (C|e_0|)^2 \lt C|e_0|\) and the errors \(C|e_k|\le(C|e_0|)^{2^k}\) collapse. If \(C|e_0| = 0.1\) the errors go \(10^{-1}, 10^{-2}, 10^{-4}, 10^{-8}, 10^{-16}\): four steps to machine precision. Lab 1 prints the ratio \(e_{k+1}/e_k^2\) so you can watch it settle to \(C\).

Two classical special cases. The Babylonian square root: applying the iteration to \(f(x) = x^2 - a\) gives \(x_{k+1} = x_k - (x_k^2 - a)/2x_k = \tfrac12(x_k + a/x_k)\), the average of the guess and \(a\)/guess, which converges from any positive start (for \(\sqrt2\) from 1: 1.5, 1.41667, 1.4142157, 1.41421356237). And division without dividing: \(f(x) = 1/x - a\) gives \(x_{k+1} = x_k(2 - ax_k)\), used in hardware reciprocal units.

Newton's method is itself a fixed-point iteration \(x_{k+1} = g(x_k)\) with \(g(x) = x - f(x)/f'(x)\). A general fixed-point iteration converges to \(x^*\) when \(|g'(x^*)| \lt 1\) and then only linearly, with error ratio \(g'(x^*)\) (the familiar Picard or successive-substitution iteration of many leaf and soil codes, where one guesses \(C_i\), computes \(A\), updates \(g_s\), recomputes \(C_i\), and so on). For Newton's \(g\), \(g'(x) = f(x)f''(x)/f'(x)^2\), which is zero at a simple root: that is why the convergence is better than linear.

Failure modes, basins and safeguards

  • Zero or small derivative. If \(f'(x_k)\approx0\) the tangent is nearly flat and \(x_{k+1}\) is thrown far away. Starting a cubic near one of its turning points is the standard demonstration (Lab 1).
  • Divergence. For \(f(x) = \arctan x\) the tangent at \(x_k\) crosses zero at \(x_{k+1} = x_k - (1+x_k^2)\arctan x_k\); for \(|x_0| \gt 1.3917\) the new iterate is farther from zero than the old one and the sequence runs away, even though the function has a single, perfectly simple root. Saturating functions (logistic, Michaelis–Menten, light-response curves) do this routinely.
  • Cycles. \(f(x) = x^3 - 2x + 2\) from \(x_0 = 0\) gives \(x_1 = 1\), \(x_2 = 0\), \(x_3 = 1,\dots\), a period-2 orbit that never reaches the real root near \(-1.77\).
  • Multiple roots. If \(f(x) = (x - x^*)^m h(x)\) with \(m\ge2\), then \(f'(x^*) = 0\), the ratio \(f/f'\approx e_k/m\), and \(e_{k+1}\approx(1 - 1/m)e_k\): linear convergence with factor \(\tfrac12\) for a double root, no better than bisection. The modified iteration \(x_{k+1} = x_k - m f/f'\) restores quadratic convergence if \(m\) is known.
  • Wrong root. With several roots, which one is found depends on the start in a way that is simple near each root and chaotic in between (Lab 2).

Safeguards. Damped Newton takes \(x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha_k f/f'\) with \(\alpha_k\in(0,1]\) chosen by backtracking so that \(|f(x_{k+1})| \lt |f(x_k)|\). Bracketing hybrids maintain an interval \([a,b]\) with \(f(a)f(b) \lt 0\), take the Newton step if it lands inside the bracket and a bisection step otherwise; this keeps the guaranteed (if slow, factor \(\tfrac12\) per step) convergence of bisection with Newton's speed near the root (Press et al. [4], rtsafe). Brent's method (1973) [7] combines bisection, the secant step and inverse quadratic interpolation without any derivative, with provable convergence and superlinear speed; it is what uniroot() in R and scipy.optimize.brentq use and should be the default for any scalar root (such as inverting a soil water retention curve for \(\psi\) given \(\theta\), or finding \(C_i\) with a prescribed \(g_s\)).

The secant method replaces \(f'(x_k)\) by the slope through the last two iterates, \([f(x_k) - f(x_{k-1})]/(x_k - x_{k-1})\). Its error satisfies \(e_{k+1}\approx C e_k e_{k-1}\), which gives convergence of order \(\varphi = (1+\sqrt5)/2\approx1.618\) (the golden ratio, because the order \(p\) must satisfy \(p^2 = p + 1\)). Slower than Newton per step, but each step costs one function evaluation instead of a function and a derivative, so if \(f'\) is as expensive as \(f\) the secant method's efficiency index \(1.618\) beats Newton's \(\sqrt2\approx1.414\) per evaluation.

Newton's method for optimisation

A minimiser of a smooth \(f:\mathbb{R}^p\to\mathbb{R}\) satisfies \(\nabla f(x^*) = 0\), a system of \(p\) equations. Applying Newton's method to this system, with the Hessian \(H = \nabla^2 f\) as the derivative of the gradient, gives

$$x_{k+1} = x_k - H(x_k)^{-1}\nabla f(x_k).$$

The same step comes from a second viewpoint that matters more in practice: build the local quadratic model \(m_k(\delta) = f(x_k) + \nabla f(x_k)^\top\delta + \tfrac12\delta^\top H_k\delta\) and minimise it; the stationarity condition \(\nabla f + H_k\delta = 0\) gives \(\delta = -H_k^{-1}\nabla f\). This model has a minimum only if \(H_k\) is positive definite; otherwise the "Newton step" heads for a saddle or a maximum of the model, and it may not even be a descent direction. Near a strict local minimum \(H\) is positive definite, the step is well defined, and the convergence is quadratic (Nocedal & Wright [3], Theorem 3.5: with \(H\) Lipschitz near \(x^*\), \(\lVert x_{k+1} - x^*\rVert\le C\lVert x_k - x^*\rVert^2\)). The method is also invariant to affine changes of variable, which is why, unlike gradient descent, it does not care about the scaling or correlation of parameters.

Each iteration costs the gradient (\(p\) derivatives), the Hessian (\(p(p+1)/2\) second derivatives), and a linear solve, \(O(p^3)\) by Cholesky. For \(p\le\) a few hundred that is negligible; for \(p\sim10^5\) it is prohibitive, which is the regime of the gradient methods on the previous page.

Making it robust: Levenberg–Marquardt damping and trust regions

Far from the solution two things go wrong: \(H\) may be indefinite, and the quadratic model may be a poor guide so the full step overshoots. Levenberg–Marquardt replaces \(H\) by \(H + \lambda I\) (Levenberg 1944 [8]) or \(H + \lambda\,\mathrm{diag}(H)\) (Marquardt 1963 [9]), with \(\lambda\ge0\) adjusted each iteration: if the step reduces \(f\), accept it and shrink \(\lambda\) (say by 3), otherwise reject it and grow \(\lambda\) (by 2 or 10) and retry. As \(\lambda\to0\) the step is Newton's; as \(\lambda\to\infty\) it is a short step along \(-\nabla f\). The trust-region view makes this principled: minimise the model subject to \(\lVert\delta\rVert\le\Delta_k\), expanding \(\Delta\) when the model predicts the actual decrease well and shrinking it when it does not; the solution of the constrained subproblem is exactly a Levenberg–Marquardt step for some \(\lambda\). Any modern "Newton" code (and nls, optim's trust-region variants, scipy.optimize.least_squares) is one of these.

Quasi-Newton and Gauss–Newton

When second derivatives are unavailable or too expensive, BFGS builds an approximate inverse Hessian from successive gradient differences by imposing the secant condition \(B_{k+1}y_k = s_k\) (with \(s_k = x_{k+1} - x_k\), \(y_k = \nabla f_{k+1} - \nabla f_k\)): the one-dimensional secant idea transplanted to \(p\) dimensions, where it fixes only one direction per step and the update rule chooses the rest. Convergence is superlinear, each step is \(O(p^2)\), and L-BFGS reduces it to \(O(mp)\) by keeping only the last \(m\) pairs. For nonlinear least squares, \(f = \tfrac12\sum_i r_i(\theta)^2\), the Hessian is \(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J} + \sum_i r_i\nabla^2r_i\) with \(\mathbf{J}\) the Jacobian of the residuals, and Gauss–Newton keeps only the first term, which needs first derivatives only and is always positive semi-definite. The step \((\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J})\delta = -\mathbf{J}^\top r\) is a linear least-squares problem, and Levenberg–Marquardt damping of it, \((\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J} + \lambda\,\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J}))\delta = -\mathbf{J}^\top r\), is the algorithm that fits Michaelis–Menten, FvCB, van Genuchten retention curves and light-response curves in every standard package. It converges quadratically when the residuals at the solution are small (the dropped term is then negligible) and linearly otherwise.

Fisher scoring and IRLS: Newton's method fits every GLM

Maximum-likelihood estimates solve the score equation \(\nabla\ell(\beta) = 0\). Newton's method on the log-likelihood is \(\beta_{k+1} = \beta_k - [\nabla^2\ell(\beta_k)]^{-1}\nabla\ell(\beta_k)\). Fisher scoring replaces the observed Hessian by its expectation, minus the Fisher information \(-\mathcal{I}(\beta) = \mathbb{E}[\nabla^2\ell]\), which is always negative definite and often simpler; for canonical-link GLMs the two coincide. For logistic regression with \(p_i = \sigma(x_i^\top\beta) = 1/(1 + e^{-x_i^\top\beta})\):

$$\begin{aligned}\ell(\beta) &= \sum_i\big[y_i\,x_i^\top\beta - \log(1 + e^{x_i^\top\beta})\big],\\ \nabla\ell &= \sum_i (y_i - p_i)\,x_i = X^\top(y - p),\\ \nabla^2\ell &= -\sum_i p_i(1-p_i)\,x_ix_i^\top = -X^\top W X,\\ W &= \mathrm{diag}\big(p_i(1-p_i)\big),\end{aligned}$$

using \(\partial p_i/\partial\eta_i = p_i(1-p_i)\) for \(\eta_i = x_i^\top\beta\). The Hessian is negative definite whenever \(X\) has full rank, so the log-likelihood is strictly concave and Newton's iteration converges to the unique maximiser from any start (barring perfect separation, where no finite maximiser exists and \(\lVert\beta\rVert\to\infty\)). The Newton step is

$$\begin{aligned}\beta_{k+1} &= \beta_k + (X^\top W X)^{-1}X^\top(y - p) = (X^\top W X)^{-1}X^\top W z,\\ z &= X\beta_k + W^{-1}(y - p),\end{aligned}$$

which is a weighted least-squares fit of the "working response" \(z\) on \(X\) with weights \(W\): hence iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS). The weights \(w_i = p_i(1-p_i)\) are largest where the prediction is uncertain (\(p_i\approx0.5\)) and vanish where it is confident, so observations near the decision boundary drive the fit. For a general GLM with mean \(\mu_i = g^{-1}(\eta_i)\) and variance function \(V(\mu)\), the same derivation gives \(w_i = (d\mu_i/d\eta_i)^2/V(\mu_i)\) and \(z_i = \eta_i + (y_i - \mu_i)\,d\eta_i/d\mu_i\) (McCullagh & Nelder 1989 [10]); this is the algorithm inside glm(), and it typically converges in four to six iterations.

Systems of equations, the coupled leaf, and implicit time stepping

Multivariate Newton

For \(F:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n\), \(F(x) = 0\), the derivative is the Jacobian \(J_{ij} = \partial F_i/\partial x_j\), and the Taylor argument gives the iteration

$$J(x_k)\,\delta_k = -F(x_k),\qquad x_{k+1} = x_k + \delta_k,$$

one linear solve per iteration, never an explicit inverse. Quadratic convergence holds under the same conditions (\(J(x^*)\) non-singular, \(J\) Lipschitz, close start), and the same safeguards (damping by backtracking on \(\lVert F\rVert\), trust regions) apply. Scaling matters even though Newton is affine invariant in exact arithmetic: the stopping test and any damping based on \(\lVert F\rVert\) are not, so each residual and unknown should be scaled to \(O(1)\) (divide \(C_i\) by 300, \(T_{leaf}\) by 300 K, energy residuals by 100 W m⁻²).

The coupled leaf model as a 2 × 2 Newton problem

Unknowns \(x = (C_i, T_{leaf})\). Given air temperature \(T_a\), humidity \(e_a\), \(C_a\), absorbed radiation \(R_{abs}\) and boundary-layer conductances, define

$$F_1(C_i, T_l) = A(C_i, T_l) - g_{sc}\big(A, T_l, C_i\big)\,(C_a - C_i),$$
$$F_2(C_i, T_l) = R_{abs} - \varepsilon\sigma T_l^4 - c_p\,g_{bH}(T_l - T_a) - \lambda\,g_w(T_l, C_i)\,\frac{e_s(T_l) - e_a}{P},$$

where \(A\) is the FvCB rate with Arrhenius temperature functions in \(T_l\), \(g_{sc}\) is a Ball–Berry or Medlyn conductance that depends on \(A\) and on the leaf-surface humidity (itself a function of \(T_l\)), \(g_w\) is the total conductance to water vapour (stomatal and boundary-layer in series), and \(e_s(T_l)\) is the saturation vapour pressure (Clausius–Clapeyron, exponential in \(T_l\)). \(F_1\) is the CO₂ supply–demand balance; \(F_2\) is the energy balance. Neither can be solved alone: \(A\) needs \(T_l\), the energy balance needs \(g_w\) which needs \(A\). Newton forms the \(2\times2\) Jacobian (analytically or by finite differences), solves \(J\delta = -F\), and converges in three to five iterations from \(C_i = 0.7C_a\), \(T_l = T_a\). Collatz et al. (1991) [14] iterated the coupled system in the model that became the SiB/CLM photosynthesis scheme; Baldocchi (1994) [15] showed that for fixed \(T_l\) the Ball–Berry–FvCB coupling reduces to a cubic in \(A\) with an analytical solution, removing the inner iteration; most land models still wrap an outer iteration on \(T_l\) around it. The closed-form page gives the quadratic for prescribed \(g_s\).

Newton inside implicit time integration

An implicit Euler step for \(\dot y = f(y)\) is \(y_{n+1} = y_n + \Delta t\,f(y_{n+1})\): the unknown appears on both sides, so each time step is a nonlinear system \(G(y) = y - y_n - \Delta t f(y) = 0\) with Jacobian \(I - \Delta t\,\partial f/\partial y\), solved by Newton from the guess \(y_n\). The reward for the extra work is unconditional stability, which is what allows hourly steps for a soil column whose fastest processes have time scales of seconds.

Richards' equation is the standard example. In its mixed form \(\partial\theta/\partial t = \partial_z[K(\psi)(\partial_z\psi + 1)]\) with \(\theta = \theta(\psi)\) and \(K = K(\psi)\) both strongly nonlinear (van Genuchten), the implicit discretisation at each time step is a nonlinear system in the nodal \(\psi\). Celia, Bouloutas and Zarba (1990) [12] showed that the modified Picard iteration, which linearises \(\theta(\psi^{m+1})\approx\theta(\psi^m) + C(\psi^m)(\psi^{m+1} - \psi^m)\) with \(C = d\theta/d\psi\) and lags \(K\) at the previous iterate, conserves mass exactly, unlike the naive \(\psi\)-based form, and converges linearly; Newton uses the full Jacobian, including \(\partial K/\partial\psi\) terms, converges quadratically, but needs a better initial guess, the Jacobian is unsymmetric, and damping or step-size control is required near sharp wetting fronts. Hydrus, CLM, JULES and ORCHIDEE all use one of these two iterations, with adaptive time stepping driven by the iteration count.

For large systems (a 3-D subsurface, or a land model's coupled energy–water column) forming and factoring the Jacobian is the bottleneck, and Jacobian-free Newton–Krylov (Knoll & Keyes 2004 [13]) solves \(J\delta = -F\) by an iterative Krylov method (GMRES) that needs only Jacobian–vector products, approximated by a finite difference \(Jv\approx[F(x + \epsilon v) - F(x)]/\epsilon\): Newton's quadratic convergence without ever storing \(J\). PFLOTRAN and ATS, used for permafrost and hillslope hydrology, are built on this.

Computing the derivatives

  • Analytic: best when available; for FvCB, \(\partial A_c/\partial C_i = V_{cmax}(\Gamma^* + K)/(C_i + K)^2\) is a two-line derivation.
  • Finite differences: forward \([f(x+h) - f(x)]/h\) has truncation error \(O(h)\) and round-off error \(O(\epsilon_{mach}/h)\), minimised at \(h\approx\sqrt{\epsilon_{mach}}\,|x|\approx10^{-8}|x|\) with total error \(\sim10^{-8}\); central \([f(x+h) - f(x-h)]/2h\) has truncation \(O(h^2)\), optimal \(h\approx\epsilon^{1/3}|x|\approx6\times10^{-6}|x|\) with error \(\sim10^{-11}\). Inaccurate derivatives degrade Newton's convergence from quadratic to linear but rarely break it, which is why finite-difference Newton (and secant/Broyden updates) are common in land models.
  • Automatic differentiation: exact to round-off, forward mode being natural for Jacobians of small systems. The gradient-descent page covers it.

Stopping, and when to prefer Newton

Stop when \(|f(x_k)|\le\tau_f\) and \(|x_{k+1} - x_k|\le\tau_x(1 + |x_k|)\): the function test alone fails for flat functions, the step test alone fails for steep ones. Because the convergence is quadratic, the last step is a good estimate of the error of the previous iterate, and the final iterate is typically far more accurate than the tolerance. Newton-type methods are the right choice when \(p\) is small to moderate, derivatives are cheap (analytic or AD), the function is smooth, and high accuracy is needed: fitting a dozen parameters, solving a leaf or soil column, computing a GLM. Gradient methods win when \(p\) is huge, the objective is noisy or only stochastic gradients are affordable, or a rough solution suffices. The Newton fractal is a reminder that "start close enough" is not a formality: a good initial guess (the previous time step's solution, a closed-form approximation, a coarse grid search) is part of the algorithm.

Connections to ecosystem modelling

  • Leaf gas exchange. CLM, JULES, ORCHIDEE, CABLE and BioCro solve the coupled \(A\)–\(g_s\)–\(C_i\) system every time step for every canopy layer. CLM uses a bracketed Brent/secant hybrid on \(C_i\) nested inside a fixed-point iteration on leaf temperature; the analytical cubic (Baldocchi 1994) or the quadratic for Medlyn-type conductances avoids the inner loop. Convergence failures here are a known source of spurious fluxes and are why residual tolerances are set in \(C_i\) units of about 0.1 µmol mol⁻¹.
  • Soil water. Every Richards'-equation solver (Hydrus, CLM's soil hydrology, SWAP, the ATS/PFLOTRAN family) is an implicit scheme with Picard or Newton iteration; the Celia (1990) mass-conservative form is the standard, and time steps are cut when the iteration fails to converge.
  • Parameter fitting. Levenberg–Marquardt (Gauss–Newton with damping) fits FvCB parameters to A–Ci curves, Michaelis–Menten constants to enzyme assays, van Genuchten parameters to retention data, and \(Q_{10}\)/Arrhenius functions to respiration data; the \((\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J})^{-1}\) at the solution gives the parameter covariance.
  • GLMs for ecological data. Species presence–absence (logistic), counts of seedlings or nests (Poisson, negative binomial) and proportions are all fitted by IRLS; understanding the weights explains why a few observations near the response threshold dominate the fit and its standard errors.
  • Steady states and spin-up. Finding the equilibrium of a nonlinear carbon–nitrogen model (where turnover depends on pool sizes, as in MIMICS or microbial-explicit models) is a Newton problem on \(F(X) = \dot X(X) = 0\); the linear-model closed form \(X^* = -A^{-1}Bu\) is the special case where the Jacobian is constant.
  • Inverting monotone functions. \(\psi\) from \(\theta\) in a retention curve, LAI from a non-exponential gap fraction, leaf temperature from measured longwave emission, surface temperature from the energy balance: all scalar root problems best handled by Brent or safeguarded Newton.
  • Inside the statistics you already use. glm(), nls(), optim(method="BFGS"), uniroot() and mixed-model fitting (lme4 uses a nested Newton/penalised least squares) are Newton-family algorithms; "did not converge" warnings mean exactly the failure modes of this page.

Exam-style questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer.

1. Derive the Newton–Raphson iteration from a Taylor expansion, prove that its convergence is quadratic, and state precisely the conditions required.

Let \(x^*\) be a root and \(e_k = x_k - x^*\). Taylor's theorem with Lagrange remainder about \(x_k\): \(0 = f(x^*) = f(x_k) + f'(x_k)(x^* - x_k) + \tfrac12f''(\xi_k)(x^* - x_k)^2\) for some \(\xi_k\) between \(x_k\) and \(x^*\). Dropping the quadratic term and solving for \(x^*\) gives the next iterate \(x_{k+1} = x_k - f(x_k)/f'(x_k)\), geometrically the zero of the tangent line. For the rate, rearrange the full expansion as \(f(x_k) = f'(x_k)e_k - \tfrac12f''(\xi_k)e_k^2\) and substitute: \(e_{k+1} = e_k - f(x_k)/f'(x_k) = e_k - e_k + \tfrac12[f''(\xi_k)/f'(x_k)]e_k^2 = [f''(\xi_k)/2f'(x_k)]\,e_k^2\). As \(x_k\to x^*\), the bracket tends to \(C = f''(x^*)/2f'(x^*)\), so \(|e_{k+1}|\le C'|e_k|^2\) for some \(C'\) slightly larger than \(|C|\) in a neighbourhood: quadratic convergence. Conditions: (i) \(f'(x^*)\ne0\) (simple root) so the quotient is finite and \(f'(x_k)\ne0\) near \(x^*\); (ii) \(f''\) exists and is continuous (bounded) in a neighbourhood, for the remainder term; (iii) \(x_0\) in a neighbourhood where \(C'|e_0| \lt 1\), because then \(C'|e_k|\le(C'|e_0|)^{2^k}\to0\). Failing (i) gives linear convergence (multiple root); failing (iii) can give divergence or cycles even for well-behaved \(f\) (arctan).

2. Give three distinct ways Newton's method can fail to find a root, with an example of each, and explain how Brent's method or a bisection hybrid avoids them.

(a) Divergence: for \(f = \arctan x\) the step is \(x - (1+x^2)\arctan x\); for \(|x_0| \gt 1.3917\) the tangent is so flat that \(|x_1| \gt |x_0|\), and the iterates grow without bound even though there is one simple root. Any saturating function does this far from its root. (b) Cycling: \(f = x^3 - 2x + 2\) from \(x_0 = 0\) gives \(x_1 = 0 - 2/(-2) = 1\), \(x_2 = 1 - 1/1 = 0\), and so on forever, never approaching the root near \(-1.77\). (c) Zero derivative / wrong root: starting \(x^3 - 3x + 1\) near its turning point \(x = 1\) sends the iterate far away, and which of the three roots is eventually reached depends sensitively on \(x_0\). A fourth, milder failure is linear convergence at a multiple root. A bracketing hybrid keeps an interval \([a,b]\) with \(f(a)f(b) \lt 0\) (so a root is guaranteed inside, by the intermediate value theorem for continuous \(f\)); at each step it computes the Newton candidate and accepts it only if it falls inside the bracket and reduces the interval adequately, otherwise it bisects; after every step the bracket is updated with the new sign information. Divergence is impossible because iterates stay in \([a,b]\); cycles are broken because the bracket shrinks monotonically; the root found is the one in the bracket. Brent's method follows the same logic without derivatives, choosing among bisection, secant and inverse quadratic interpolation steps, and guarantees convergence in at most about \((\log_2((b-a)/\tau))^2\) evaluations while achieving superlinear convergence near the root. The cost of safety is that one must first find a bracket, which for leaf \(C_i\) is easy (\(0\) and \(C_a\)) and for a general problem may not be.

3. Show that the secant method has convergence order \((1+\sqrt5)/2\) and discuss when it is preferable to Newton's method.

The secant method replaces \(f'(x_k)\) with the divided difference \(f[x_{k-1}, x_k] = (f(x_k) - f(x_{k-1}))/(x_k - x_{k-1})\). Using the Newton error analysis with this approximate slope (or directly from the error formula for linear interpolation), one finds \(e_{k+1}\approx\dfrac{f''(x^*)}{2f'(x^*)}\,e_k e_{k-1} = C\,e_ke_{k-1}\). Suppose \(|e_{k+1}|\approx K|e_k|^p\) for some order \(p\) and constant \(K\). Then \(|e_k|\approx K|e_{k-1}|^p\), so \(|e_{k-1}|\approx(|e_k|/K)^{1/p}\). Substituting into the recursion: \(K|e_k|^p\approx C|e_k|\,(|e_k|/K)^{1/p}\), i.e. \(|e_k|^{p}\propto|e_k|^{1 + 1/p}\). Matching exponents gives \(p = 1 + 1/p\), \(p^2 - p - 1 = 0\), \(p = (1+\sqrt5)/2\approx1.618\): superlinear but sub-quadratic. Each secant step needs one new function evaluation; a Newton step needs \(f\) and \(f'\). If the derivative costs about as much as the function, two secant steps cost the same as one Newton step and reduce the error by order \(1.618^2\approx2.6 \gt 2\), so the secant method is more efficient per evaluation (efficiency indices \(1.618\) vs \(\sqrt2\)). Prefer the secant method (or Brent) when \(f'\) is unavailable, expensive, or unreliable (a model with switches, look-up tables or iterative sub-solvers); prefer Newton when \(f'\) is cheap and analytic, when very high accuracy is required in few steps, or in several dimensions where the secant generalisation (Broyden) is less clean.

4. Derive the IRLS algorithm for logistic regression from Newton's method, interpret the weights, and explain why the iteration is guaranteed to find the global maximum.

With \(\eta_i = x_i^\top\beta\) and \(p_i = 1/(1+e^{-\eta_i})\), the Bernoulli log-likelihood is \(\ell = \sum_i[y_i\log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)] = \sum_i[y_i\eta_i - \log(1+e^{\eta_i})]\). Differentiating with \(d\log(1+e^\eta)/d\eta = p\): \(\partial\ell/\partial\beta = \sum_i(y_i - p_i)x_i = X^\top(y-p)\). Differentiating again with \(dp_i/d\eta_i = p_i(1-p_i)\): \(\partial^2\ell/\partial\beta\partial\beta^\top = -\sum_ip_i(1-p_i)x_ix_i^\top = -X^\top WX\), \(W = \mathrm{diag}(p_i(1-p_i))\). Newton's step for maximising is \(\beta_{new} = \beta - [\nabla^2\ell]^{-1}\nabla\ell = \beta + (X^\top WX)^{-1}X^\top(y-p)\). Write \(\beta = (X^\top WX)^{-1}X^\top WX\beta\) and add: \(\beta_{new} = (X^\top WX)^{-1}X^\top W[X\beta + W^{-1}(y-p)] = (X^\top WX)^{-1}X^\top Wz\) with working response \(z = \eta + (y-p)/[p(1-p)]\): a weighted least-squares regression of \(z\) on \(X\) with weights \(W\), recomputed each iteration. Interpretation: \(z_i\) is the linearised logit of \(y_i\) about the current fit (first-order Taylor expansion of the link function at \(p_i\)), and \(w_i = p_i(1-p_i) = 1/\mathrm{Var}(z_i)\) is the inverse variance of that working observation, so IRLS is precisely weighted least squares on linearised data, which is Gauss–Newton for the GLM. Weights peak at \(0.25\) when \(p_i = 0.5\) and vanish for confidently classified points, so the decision-boundary observations drive both the estimate and its covariance \((X^\top WX)^{-1}\). Global convergence: \(X^\top WX\) is positive definite whenever \(X\) has full column rank and all \(0 \lt p_i \lt 1\), so \(\ell\) is strictly concave and has at most one stationary point, which is the global maximum; Newton's method on a strictly concave function with step-halving if needed converges to it from any start. The exception is complete or quasi-complete separation, when the likelihood increases without bound as \(\lVert\beta\rVert\to\infty\) and the iteration "converges" to huge coefficients with huge standard errors; penalised (Firth) logistic regression is the remedy.

5. Formulate the simultaneous solution of a leaf's intercellular CO₂ and temperature as a two-dimensional Newton problem. What makes it nonlinear, how would you obtain the Jacobian, and what initial guess and stopping criteria would you use?

Unknowns \(x = (C_i, T_l)\). Residuals: \(F_1 = A(C_i,T_l) - g_{sc}(A, T_l, C_i)(C_a - C_i)\), the equality of biochemical demand and diffusive supply of CO₂; \(F_2 = R_{abs} - \varepsilon\sigma T_l^4 - c_pg_{bH}(T_l - T_a) - \lambda g_w(e_s(T_l) - e_a)/P\), the leaf energy balance (absorbed radiation minus emitted longwave, sensible and latent heat). Nonlinearities: \(A\) is a minimum of hyperbolic functions of \(C_i\) with Arrhenius/peaked temperature dependence of \(V_{cmax}\), \(J_{max}\), \(K_c\), \(K_o\), \(\Gamma^*\) in \(T_l\); \(g_{sc}\) (Ball–Berry \(g_0 + g_1Ah_s/C_s\) or Medlyn) depends on \(A\) and on leaf-surface humidity, which depends on \(e_s(T_l)\); \(e_s(T_l)\) is exponential (Clausius–Clapeyron); longwave is \(T_l^4\); and \(g_w\) combines \(g_s\) and the boundary layer, which itself depends weakly on \(T_l - T_a\) through free convection. Newton: \(J = \begin{pmatrix}\partial F_1/\partial C_i & \partial F_1/\partial T_l\\ \partial F_2/\partial C_i & \partial F_2/\partial T_l\end{pmatrix}\), solve \(J\delta = -F\), update. The Jacobian can be analytic (tedious but the pieces are known: \(\partial A_c/\partial C_i = V_{cmax}(\Gamma^*+K)/(C_i+K)^2\), \(de_s/dT\) from the Clausius–Clapeyron form, \(4\varepsilon\sigma T^3\)), by forward finite differences with steps of about 1 µmol mol⁻¹ and 0.01 K (cost: two extra evaluations), or by forward-mode AD. Scale the unknowns and residuals to \(O(1)\) (e.g. \(C_i/C_a\), \((T_l - T_a)/10\), \(F_1/10\) µmol, \(F_2/100\) W m⁻²) so that the stopping test \(\lVert F\rVert\le10^{-4}\) and \(\lVert\delta\rVert\le10^{-4}\) are meaningful. Initial guess: \(C_i = 0.7C_a\) (C3) and \(T_l = T_a\), or better the previous time step's solution; three to five iterations are typical. Safeguards: damp the step so that \(C_i\) stays in \((\Gamma^*, C_a)\) and \(|T_l - T_a| \lt 15\) K, and watch the \(\min()\) in FvCB, which makes \(F_1\) only piecewise smooth and can cause a limit cycle between two limitation states; smoothing the minimum, or Baldocchi's analytical cubic for \(C_i\) at fixed \(T_l\) inside a scalar iteration on \(T_l\), removes that problem.

6. Compare Picard (modified Picard) and Newton iteration for the implicit solution of Richards' equation. Why did Celia et al. (1990) matter, and when would you choose each?

Discretising the mixed-form Richards' equation implicitly in time gives, at each step, a nonlinear system in the nodal pressure heads \(\psi^{n+1}\) because \(\theta(\psi)\) and \(K(\psi)\) are nonlinear (van Genuchten/Mualem). Celia et al. (1990) showed that the common \(\psi\)-based form, \(C(\psi)\partial\psi/\partial t = \nabla\cdot K\nabla(\psi+z)\), produces large mass-balance errors because the chain rule \(C = d\theta/d\psi\) is applied to a discretised, lagged \(C\); their modified Picard scheme expands \(\theta^{n+1,m+1}\approx\theta^{n+1,m} + C^{n+1,m}(\psi^{n+1,m+1} - \psi^{n+1,m})\) so that at convergence the discrete equation is the mixed form itself and mass is conserved exactly, while the linear system each iteration has the symmetric structure of the \(\psi\)-form. Picard: at iteration \(m\), freeze \(K\) and \(C\) at \(\psi^m\) and solve the resulting linear system; this is a fixed-point iteration, converging linearly with a ratio that approaches 1 near sharp fronts, very robust to the initial guess, symmetric positive-definite matrices (cheap solvers), and no derivatives of \(K\). Newton: use the full Jacobian including \(\partial K/\partial\psi\) and \(\partial C/\partial\psi\) terms; quadratic convergence, typically 2–4 iterations instead of 5–20, but an unsymmetric Jacobian, more work per iteration, and a smaller basin of convergence, so it needs damping (line search on the residual norm) or a Picard start, and the Jacobian is expensive to form in 2-D/3-D unless Jacobian-free Newton–Krylov is used. Choose Picard for 1-D columns with modest time steps, smooth soils and robustness priorities (most land-surface models); Newton (damped, or JFNK) for large 3-D domains, sharp infiltration fronts into dry soil, coupled surface–subsurface problems, and whenever the iteration count under Picard forces very small time steps. Either way, control the time step by the iteration count: cut \(\Delta t\) if convergence fails, grow it when convergence takes few iterations.

7. Explain the relationship between Newton's method, Gauss–Newton, Levenberg–Marquardt and gradient descent for minimising a sum of squares. For an FvCB fit, which would you use and why?

For \(f(\theta) = \tfrac12\sum_ir_i(\theta)^2\) with Jacobian \(\mathbf{J}_{ij} = \partial r_i/\partial\theta_j\): \(\nabla f = \mathbf{J}^\top r\) and \(H = \mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J} + \sum_ir_i\nabla^2r_i\). Newton: \(\delta = -H^{-1}\mathbf{J}^\top r\), needs second derivatives of every residual, quadratic convergence, \(H\) may be indefinite far away. Gauss–Newton: drop the second term, \(\delta = -(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J})^{-1}\mathbf{J}^\top r\), which is the Newton step for the linearised residuals \(r + \mathbf{J}\delta\), i.e. a sequence of linear least-squares problems; first derivatives only; \(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J}\) is positive semi-definite so the step is a descent direction; convergence quadratic if the residuals at the solution are zero (the dropped term vanishes), linear with rate governed by \(\sum r_i\nabla^2r_i\) otherwise; fails if \(\mathbf{J}\) is rank-deficient (non-identifiable parameters). Levenberg–Marquardt: \(\delta = -(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J} + \lambda D)^{-1}\mathbf{J}^\top r\) with \(D = I\) or \(\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J})\); for \(\lambda\to0\) this is Gauss–Newton, for \(\lambda\to\infty\) it is a small step along \(-\mathbf{J}^\top r = -\nabla f\), i.e. gradient descent with step \(1/\lambda\); \(\lambda\) is adapted so that the method behaves like gradient descent when the linear model is poor and like Gauss–Newton when it is good, and the damping also fixes rank deficiency of \(\mathbf{J}\). Gradient descent alone is the \(\lambda\to\infty\) limit: cheapest per step, but on the elongated valleys of an FvCB fit (\(V_{cmax}\) trading against \(R_d\), \(J_{max}\) against \(T_p\)) it needs thousands of steps. For an FvCB fit with 4 parameters and 10–15 points: Levenberg–Marquardt (as in nls/minpack.lm/plantecophys), with analytic or finite-difference Jacobians, parameters scaled (fit \(\log V_{cmax}\) etc.), multiple starts to handle the piecewise \(\min(A_c, A_j, A_p)\) structure, and inspection of \(\mathrm{cond}(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J})\) to detect that \(T_p\) or \(R_d\) is unidentifiable from the data at hand. The result's covariance \(\hat\sigma^2(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J})^{-1}\) is a by-product.

8. A colleague's leaf-temperature solver "sometimes doesn't converge" and they propose fixing it by increasing the iteration limit. Critique this and propose a diagnosis and a fix.

Raising the limit helps only if the iteration is converging slowly, which for Newton (quadratic) or even Picard (linear) is rarely the issue; the typical causes are structural. Diagnose by logging the residual norm per iteration: (1) a residual that oscillates between two values indicates a limit cycle, usually from a discontinuity such as the FvCB \(\min()\) switching between \(A_c\) and \(A_j\), a conductance clamped at \(g_0\), or a stability-dependent boundary-layer conductance that flips between free and forced convection; (2) a residual that grows indicates divergence from a poor start (a large \(T_l - T_a\) guess, or a wet-to-dry transition where \(g_s\) collapses), typically because \(e_s(T_l)\) is exponential and the Newton step overshoots; (3) a residual that stalls at a non-zero value indicates an inconsistent system (e.g. \(C_i\) driven below \(\Gamma^*\) or above \(C_a\), where the supply–demand equation has no physical root) or a tolerance set below the noise of a finite-difference Jacobian. Fixes in order of priority: scale unknowns and residuals; start from the previous time step's solution; damp the Newton step (halve until \(\lVert F\rVert\) decreases) and bound \(C_i\in(\Gamma^*, C_a)\), \(|T_l - T_a|\le15\) K; replace hard switches by smooth minima (a hyperbolic minimum with curvature 0.98–0.999) so that the Jacobian is continuous; for the scalar sub-problems use a bracketed Brent iteration, which cannot diverge; and set tolerances consistent with the Jacobian accuracy (about \(10^{-4}\) relative with finite differences). If failures persist only under specific conditions (very low light, high VPD), that is physics, not numerics: the model is being asked for a state that does not exist, and the fallback should be a physically defined limit (e.g. \(g_s = g_0\), \(A = -R_d\)) rather than more iterations.
