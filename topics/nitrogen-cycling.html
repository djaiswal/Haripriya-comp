Topic 5 of the Biogeochemistry section

Nitrogen Cycling

Nitrogen moves through eight oxidation states, each step run by a different set of microbes with its own enzymes and environmental controls. This page follows N from N₂ to organic matter to ammonium, nitrate and back to the atmosphere, and shows how that chain is compressed into the N submodels of ecosystem and Earth system models.

The one-paragraph version

Almost all nitrogen is in the atmosphere as triple-bonded N₂, inaccessible to most life. Nitrogenase in a few prokaryotes breaks that bond (biological N fixation, ~58 Tg N yr⁻¹ on land naturally), and since 1913 the Haber–Bosch process has added roughly twice as much again. In soil, 95–99% of N is organic; the rate-limiting step in supplying plants and microbes is the depolymerisation of proteins and chitin into monomers, after which ammonification releases NH₄⁺. Ammonium is oxidised to nitrite and nitrate by nitrifiers (archaea, bacteria and, since 2015, complete ammonia oxidisers) when oxygen is present, and nitrate is reduced stepwise to N₂ by denitrifiers when it is not, leaking the greenhouse gas N₂O along the way. Plants and microbes compete for NH₄⁺, NO₃⁻ and amino acids; what escapes is lost as NH₃ gas (at high pH), as leached NO₃⁻ (a mobile anion), or as NO, N₂O and N₂. Terrestrial ecosystems are mostly N-limited because fixation is expensive and losses are leaky; adding N first stimulates growth, then saturates the system and exports nitrate and N₂O. Models represent all of this with a handful of pools and first-order rates modified by temperature, moisture and substrate, and the way they do it decides how much CO₂ fertilisation they allow.

Examiner's lens

An examiner will test whether you can name the organisms, enzymes and oxidation-state changes for each transformation; explain which environmental variable controls each one and why; derive a simple consequence (e.g. the NH₃/NH₄⁺ fraction at a given pH, or why N₂O peaks at intermediate moisture); and connect that to a model structure (DayCent's hole-in-the-pipe, CLM5's FUN) and to a global question (does N limitation cap the land carbon sink?).

Pools and fluxes, global and local

The atmosphere holds about 3.9 × 10⁹ Tg N as N₂, soils about 95–140 Pg N in the top metre (overwhelmingly organic), and vegetation only 3–4 Pg N. Before humans, terrestrial biological N fixation (BNF) supplied roughly 58 Tg N yr⁻¹ (range 40–100; Vitousek et al. 2013; Fowler et al. [6]) and lightning ~5 Tg. Today Haber–Bosch fixation is ~120 Tg N yr⁻¹, crop BNF ~60 Tg, and fossil-fuel combustion ~30 Tg of NOx, so reactive N creation has more than doubled (Galloway et al. [4, 5]). About half of fertiliser N is taken up by the crop; the rest leaches, volatilises or denitrifies. Atmospheric deposition returns ~60–70 Tg N yr⁻¹ to land, much of it to ecosystems that never received deposition before. Rockström and Steffen's planetary-boundary analysis put a safe limit of ~62 Tg N yr⁻¹ for industrial and intentional fixation against ~150 Tg now, which is why N is among the most transgressed boundaries.

At the ecosystem scale the numbers are smaller but the structure is the same: a temperate forest cycles 50–100 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ through litterfall and mineralisation, receives 5–30 kg in deposition, fixes 1–10 kg, and loses a few kg as nitrate and gases. Internal recycling exceeds inputs by an order of magnitude, which is why mineralisation, not fixation, sets annual plant N supply.

The transformations: a redox ladder

Nitrogen's chemistry is a story of electrons. The oxidation state runs from −3 (NH₄⁺, amines) to +5 (NO₃⁻). Oxidations (fixation's reverse, nitrification) require an electron acceptor, usually O₂, and release energy that chemolithoautotrophs live on; reductions (fixation, denitrification, DNRA, assimilatory nitrate reduction) need electron donors, usually organic carbon or H₂, and most run only when O₂ is scarce. Biological N fixation

Nitrogenase, a two-component Fe protein / MoFe protein complex (alternative V and Fe-only forms exist), reduces N₂ at great cost: N₂ + 8 H⁺ + 8 e⁻ + 16 ATP → 2 NH₃ + H₂ + 16 ADP + 16 Pi. The enzyme is irreversibly inactivated by O₂, so fixers either live anaerobically, separate fixation in time or in heterocysts (cyanobacteria), or, in legume nodules, bathe the bacteroids in leghaemoglobin that keeps free O₂ in the nanomolar range while supplying respiration. Symbiotic fixation (rhizobia–legumes, Frankia–actinorhizal plants, cyanobacteria–lichens and cycads) can deliver 50–300 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹; associative fixation on root surfaces and in the rhizosphere (Azospirillum, grasses) and free-living fixation (Azotobacter, cyanobacterial crusts, wood-decaying fungi–bacteria consortia) are typically 1–20 kg. Controls: energy (carbon supply, light for phototrophs), O₂, Mo and P availability (nitrogenase and the ATP bill), temperature, and the availability of mineral N, which represses nif gene expression because fixing is pointless when NH₄⁺ is cheap. In models BNF is prescribed as a function of NPP or evapotranspiration (CLM4.5, JULES), computed as a cost-based option within FUN (CLM5), or made responsive to plant N status (ORCHIDEE-CNP, QUINCY).

Depolymerisation, mineralisation, immobilisation

For most of the 20th century the paradigm was that mineralisation of NH₄⁺ was the bottleneck of N supply and that plants used only mineral N. Schimel and Bennett [1] reframed it: the rate-limiting step is the depolymerisation of proteins, peptides, chitin and nucleic acids by extracellular enzymes (proteases, chitinases, peptidases) into monomers; amino acids are then taken up directly by microbes and by plants and mycorrhizae, and ammonification is the release of NH₄⁺ from amino acids by microbes that have carbon to spare and N in excess (the stoichiometric argument on the stoichiometry page). In N-poor systems plants compete for amino acids at the micro-site scale; in N-rich systems microbes are saturated, NH₄⁺ and NO₃⁻ accumulate, and the classic mineral-N view is a fair approximation. The distinction matters for measurements: net mineralisation (change in NH₄⁺ + NO₃⁻ in an incubation without plants) is the balance of gross mineralisation and gross immobilisation and can be near zero while gross rates are large. ¹⁵N pool dilution (Kirkham and Bartholomew 1954) labels the NH₄⁺ pool with ¹⁵NH₄⁺ and follows the decline in enrichment: unlabelled N entering by gross mineralisation dilutes the pool, while consumption (immobilisation, nitrification) removes label and unlabelled N in proportion. Gross mineralisation in forest soils is typically 1–5 mg N kg⁻¹ d⁻¹, often five to ten times the net rate.

Nitrification

Nitrification is a two-organism, two-step chemolithoautotrophic oxidation. Ammonia oxidisers convert NH₃ (the substrate is the un-ionised form) to hydroxylamine with ammonia monooxygenase (AMO, amoA gene), then to nitrite via hydroxylamine oxidoreductase (HAO) and, in bacteria, a nitric oxide intermediate; the energy yield is small (~270 kJ mol⁻¹), so they fix CO₂ slowly and grow slowly. Ammonia-oxidising archaea (AOA; Nitrososphaera, Nitrosopumilus) usually outnumber ammonia-oxidising bacteria (AOB; Nitrosomonas, Nitrosospira) in soils, especially acidic and low-NH₄⁺ soils, because they have a much higher affinity for NH₃ (Prosser and Nicol [11]); AOB dominate after fertilisation. Nitrite oxidisers (NOB; Nitrobacter, Nitrospira) use nitrite oxidoreductase (nxr) to make NO₃⁻. Daims et al. [12] and van Kessel et al. discovered Nitrospira strains that perform comammox, complete oxidation of NH₃ to NO₃⁻ in one cell, overturning the century-old two-organism rule. Controls: O₂ (strictly aerobic), NH₄⁺ supply (and hence mineralisation and fertiliser), pH (NH₃ availability falls at low pH; AOA cope better), temperature (optimum 25–35 °C), and moisture (optimum ~60% water-filled pore space, the compromise between substrate diffusion and O₂ supply). Nitrification produces N₂O as a by-product of hydroxylamine oxidation (~0.1–1% of N nitrified) and, at low O₂, through nitrifier denitrification, in which AOB reduce their own nitrite to NO and N₂O with nirK and norB.

Denitrification

Facultative anaerobes (Pseudomonas, Paracoccus, many others, plus some fungi and archaea) respire NO₃⁻ when O₂ is scarce, reducing it stepwise: NO₃⁻ → NO₂⁻ (nitrate reductase, narG membrane-bound or napA periplasmic) → NO (nitrite reductase, nirK copper or nirS cytochrome cd₁) → N₂O (NO reductase, norB/cnor/qnor) → N₂ (N₂O reductase, nosZ, clades I and II). The pathway is modular: many organisms lack nosZ and stop at N₂O, and the N₂O reductase is the most sensitive enzyme to O₂ and to low pH, so the N₂O:N₂ ratio rises in acidic, freshly anoxic, nitrate-rich soils. Controls (in order of importance in most soils): anoxia (water-filled pore space above ~60–70%, aggregate interiors, high respiration), NO₃⁻ supply, labile carbon (the electron donor), pH, and temperature. Denitrification is the least constrained flux in the global N budget, and the hole-in-the-pipe model below is the standard way to think about its gaseous products. Other anaerobic routes: DNRA (dissimilatory nitrate reduction to ammonium, nrfA), favoured when labile C is abundant relative to NO₃⁻ and conditions are strongly reducing, conserves N in the ecosystem; anammox (anaerobic ammonium oxidation, NH₄⁺ + NO₂⁻ → N₂ + 2 H₂O, via hydrazine, by Planctomycetes such as Candidatus Brocadia and Kuenenia) dominates N₂ production in marine oxygen-minimum zones and sediments and occurs in wetland and paddy soils. Kuypers, Marchant and Kartal [10] give the modern microbial map of all of these.

Losses: gases, volatilisation, leaching

The hole-in-the-pipe model

Firestone and Davidson [7] pictured N flowing through a pipe: the total flow is the rate of nitrification plus denitrification (set by N supply), and the gases NO and N₂O leak through holes whose size is set by soil conditions, chiefly water content. Davidson et al. [8] added the moisture dependence: as water-filled pore space (WFPS) rises, O₂ diffusion falls (D ∝ air-filled porosity), nitrification peaks near 60% WFPS, denitrification takes over above ~70%, and the dominant gas shifts from NO (dry, aerobic) to N₂O (60–80%) to N₂ (>85%, when N₂O reductase is fully active and N₂O cannot escape before being reduced). The model explains the classic pulse of N₂O after rain on dry soil (nitrification burst plus transient anoxia) and the N₂O peak at intermediate moisture seen in incubation studies (Butterbach-Bahl et al. [9]).

N₂O as a greenhouse gas

N₂O has a global warming potential of about 273 (AR6; 298 in AR4) over 100 years, an atmospheric lifetime of ~115 years, and is now the dominant ozone-depleting emission. Agricultural soils contribute roughly 4 Tg N₂O-N yr⁻¹ of a 17 Tg total. The IPCC Tier 1 default emission factor is 1% of applied N emitted directly as N₂O-N (uncertainty 0.3–3%; the 2019 refinement splits it into 1.6% for wet and 0.5% for dry climates), plus indirect emissions from leached and volatilised N. Shcherbak, Millar and Robertson [14] showed from a meta-analysis that the emission factor is not constant: N₂O rises exponentially with N rate, so the marginal emission from fertiliser beyond crop demand is far higher than from the first increments, a strong argument for rate-based rather than flat emission accounting and for matching supply to demand.

Ammonia volatilisation

NH₄⁺ and NH₃ are in acid–base equilibrium with pKa ≈ 9.25 at 25 °C; only the un-ionised NH₃ escapes to the air. The fraction present as NH₃ is

$$f_{NH_3} = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{\,pK_a - pH}}, \qquad pK_a(T) \approx 0.0902 + \frac{2729.9}{T\,[\mathrm{K}]},$$

so at pH 7 about 0.6% is NH₃, at pH 8 about 5%, at pH 9 about 36%. Urea hydrolysis by urease raises the pH around the granule to 8–9 and drives large losses (10–40% of applied N on calcareous or dry warm soils); injection, incorporation, urease inhibitors (NBPT) and acidifying fertilisers (ammonium sulphate) reduce them. Flooded rice paddies, feedlots and surface-applied slurry are the big sources. Volatilised NH₃ is re-deposited within tens to hundreds of kilometres, which is why NHx deposition now dominates in agricultural regions.

Leaching

Nitrate is an anion, and most soils have little anion exchange capacity at normal pH, so NO₃⁻ moves with water: concentrations in drainage track the balance between nitrification and uptake, and losses are concentrated in periods with water surplus and no plant demand (autumn and winter in temperate agriculture, snowmelt in boreal systems). Typical losses are 10–60 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ from fertilised cropland and <5 kg from intact forests. Dissolved organic N (DON) dominates leaching in unpolluted ecosystems and is a "leak" that does not depend on N saturation (Hedin et al. 1995). NH₄⁺ is held on the cation exchange complex and leaches little, except in sandy, low-CEC soils.

Plants, N limitation and N saturation

Uptake. Roots take up NH₄⁺ (AMT transporters; cheaper to assimilate, no reduction needed, but toxic at high concentration and acidifying as H⁺ is extruded), NO₃⁻ (NRT1/NRT2 transporters; must be reduced by nitrate reductase and nitrite reductase at a cost of 8 e⁻ per N, but it is the more mobile and often the more abundant form in aerated soils), and amino acids (AAP transporters; important in cold, acidic, organic soils such as tundra and boreal forest). Ectomycorrhizal and ericoid fungi produce proteases and access organic N directly; arbuscular mycorrhizae mostly move NH₄⁺. Species differ in preference: many conifers and ericaceous plants prefer NH₄⁺ or amino acids, many crops and ruderals prefer NO₃⁻, and rice is adapted to NH₄⁺ in flooded soil.

Why N limits. Vitousek and Howarth [2] asked why, with an unlimited atmospheric reservoir and organisms that can tap it, N should limit productivity on land and in the sea. Their answers: fixation is energetically costly and fixers are limited by P, Mo, Fe, light and grazing, so they do not fix enough to remove N limitation; N is lost by pathways (denitrification, leaching, fire) that have no counterpart for P; and the slow release of N from organic matter during succession means ecosystems can be N-limited for centuries after disturbance or glaciation. Vitousek and Field (1999) added the fixer paradox: symbiotic fixers are abundant in the tropics, where N is relatively available, and scarce in N-poor temperate and boreal forests, suggesting that fixers lose their advantage once N accumulates and are constrained by cold, shade and P.

N saturation and deposition. Aber et al. [13] described stages as chronic N deposition exceeds biological demand: stage 0 (N-limited, high retention), stage 1 (growth stimulation, foliar N rises, nitrification begins), stage 2 (nitrification and NO₃⁻ leaching increase, Ca and Mg are stripped as counter-ions, Al mobilised), and stage 3 (declining productivity, fine-root and mycorrhizal loss, N₂O emission). Experimental additions at Harvard Forest and in Europe (NITREX) broadly confirmed the sequence but showed large soil retention, mostly through immobilisation into soil organic matter. Deposition also shifts species composition (grasses over heath, nitrophilous mosses), acidifies soil, and in the short term raises forest carbon sequestration by ~20–40 kg C per kg N.

Agriculture. Globally about 47% of N inputs to cropland end up in harvested products; the balance goes to volatilisation (10–20% of surface-applied urea), leaching (10–30% of surplus), denitrification and soil storage. The "4R" prescription (right rate, source, time, place) and enhanced-efficiency fertilisers all target the mismatch between N supply and crop demand that drives Shcherbak's exponential N₂O response and nitrate leaching.

Putting the pieces together: a mineral-N simulator

Most soil N models (Century, DayCent, DNDC, the N module of APSIM, CLM) share a skeleton: an organic N pool that mineralises slowly, an NH₄⁺ pool that is nitrified, and a NO₃⁻ pool that is taken up, leached and denitrified, each process first-order in its substrate and modified by temperature and moisture functions. Isotopes: δ¹⁵N as an integrator

¹⁵N is 0.3663% of natural N; δ¹⁵N = (Rsample/Rair − 1) × 1000‰ with atmospheric N₂ as the zero. Enzymes prefer the light isotope, so every fractionating loss leaves the residual pool heavier. Nitrification fractionates strongly (ε ≈ −15 to −35‰; the NO₃⁻ produced is light, the NH₄⁺ left behind heavy), denitrification too (ε ≈ −10 to −30‰), ammonia volatilisation very strongly (ε ≈ −20 to −40‰), while fixation and plant uptake at low concentration fractionate little (0 to −3‰). Fractionation is only expressed when the reaction is incomplete and the product leaves, which is why δ¹⁵N records the openness of the N cycle: ecosystems that lose a large fraction of their N through nitrification–denitrification or volatilisation become enriched (tropical forests, arid soils, fertilised fields with δ¹⁵N of +5 to +15‰), while closed, N-limited systems that recycle N tightly stay near 0 or negative (boreal forests, tundra, −5 to 0‰). Soil δ¹⁵N increases with depth (1–8‰) as microbial processing enriches residual organic N and as mycorrhizal fungi retain ¹⁵N while passing depleted N to plants (Högberg [17]). Houlton and Bai [16] used the global distribution of soil and plant δ¹⁵N to estimate that gaseous losses, mostly denitrification, account for roughly a third of N losses from natural terrestrial ecosystems, a result otherwise unreachable because denitrification is so hard to measure.

Connections to ecosystem models

Century and DayCent. Century (Parton et al. 1987) couples each of its C pools to an N pool with fixed C:N and computes mineralisation and immobilisation from the C flows (the MIT bookkeeping of the stoichiometry page). DayCent added a daily time step and the N-gas submodel of Parton et al. [15, 18] and Del Grosso et al. [19]: nitrification is a function of NH₄⁺, temperature, WFPS and pH, with a fixed fraction (~2%) emitted as N₂O; denitrification is the minimum of nitrate-, carbon- (heterotrophic respiration as a proxy for electron donors) and WFPS-limited rates; the N₂:N₂O ratio rises with WFPS and with the ratio of NO₃⁻ to respiration; NO is scaled from N₂O by a function of gas diffusivity, i.e. the hole-in-the-pipe in code. DayCent underlies the US national greenhouse-gas inventory for agricultural soils.

CLM5. The soil N cycle (decomposition, mineralisation, immobilisation) follows Century-type pools; nitrification and denitrification are taken from the DayCent formulations above. Plant N acquisition uses FUN (Fixation and Uptake of Nitrogen; Fisher et al. 2010, Shi et al. 2016): the plant spends carbon on whichever route is cheapest among active root uptake, mycorrhizal uptake, retranslocation and symbiotic fixation, so N limitation appears as a carbon cost rather than an abrupt down-regulation. Leaf C:N is flexible and photosynthetic capacity responds to leaf N through LUNA. Losses include leaching proportional to drainage and the NO₃⁻ concentration, and gaseous losses from nitrification and denitrification.

ORCHIDEE-N / -CNP, JULES-CN, JSBACH-CNP, QUINCY. ORCHIDEE-CN (Vuichard et al. 2019) adopted the Century-style soil N with DayCent gas fluxes and a flexible plant stoichiometry; ORCHIDEE-CNP adds P. JULES-CN (Wiltshire et al. 2021) attaches an N cycle to the RothC-based soil model with fixed tissue ratios and BNF scaled by NPP. JSBACH-CNP (Goll et al. 2012) was among the first to show that adding N and P to an ESM land model reduces projected CO₂ uptake. QUINCY (Thum et al. 2019) builds the N and P cycles in from the start with explicit microbial activity and isotope tracers (¹³C, ¹⁴C, ¹⁵N), designed to be tested against δ¹⁵N patterns.

The question that matters. Does N limit the land carbon sink? Zaehle and Dalmonech [20] and Wieder et al. [21] argue yes: when C–N coupling is added, the projected 21st-century land sink shrinks substantially, and Wieder et al. estimated that achieving the CMIP5 land C gains would require N inputs well beyond plausible fixation and deposition. Zaehle et al. (2014) showed at two FACE sites that models disagree mainly in how N limitation develops over a decade. Davies-Barnard et al. (2020) found that CMIP6 models with N cycles still differ by a factor of two in global BNF and in the fate of deposited N. The uncertain pieces are exactly the processes on this page: BNF response to demand, the openness of losses (denitrification and leaching), and the flexibility of plant and soil stoichiometry.

Measurement methods worth knowing

  • Net mineralisation and nitrification: buried-bag or laboratory incubations of sieved soil; change in KCl-extractable NH₄⁺ + NO₃⁻ over weeks. Cheap, integrative, but excludes plant uptake and disturbs structure.
  • Ion-exchange resin bags or capsules: capture mineral N moving through the soil in situ over weeks to months; index of availability, not a rate.
  • ¹⁵N pool dilution: gross mineralisation, nitrification, immobilisation over 1–3 days. ¹⁵N tracer additions (enriched fertiliser or litter) follow the fate of N into plants, soil and losses over years; mass-balance recoveries of 70–90% are typical, with the rest assumed gaseous.
  • Acetylene inhibition: 10 Pa C₂H₂ blocks ammonia monooxygenase (isolates nitrification); 10 kPa blocks N₂O reductase, so N₂O accumulates and total denitrification can be measured. Underestimates in the presence of NO and in soils where acetylene is decomposed; largely replaced by ¹⁵N-gas flux methods and N₂/Ar ratios.
  • Chamber fluxes: static chambers with gas chromatography or laser spectroscopy (QCL) for N₂O and NO; automated chambers and eddy covariance for continuous N₂O. Fluxes are spatially and temporally episodic (hot spots and hot moments), so sampling design dominates uncertainty.
  • Molecular markers: nifH (fixation), amoA (AOA/AOB/comammox), nirK/nirS, nosZ clade I/II, nrfA (DNRA), hydrazine synthase (anammox); abundance and transcript data constrain which pathways are possible, not rates.

Exam-style questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer.

1. Describe the two steps of nitrification, naming the organisms and enzymes, and explain how comammox changed the picture. Which environmental factors control the rate and why?

Step one: ammonia (NH₃, the un-ionised form) is oxidised to hydroxylamine by ammonia monooxygenase (AMO, encoded by amoA), then to nitrite by hydroxylamine oxidoreductase (HAO), in bacteria via NO. This is performed by ammonia-oxidising bacteria (Nitrosomonas, Nitrosospira; β- and γ-proteobacteria) and ammonia-oxidising archaea (Thaumarchaeota such as Nitrososphaera), the archaea having far higher substrate affinity and dominating low-NH₄⁺, acidic and unfertilised soils. Step two: nitrite is oxidised to nitrate by nitrite oxidoreductase (nxr) in nitrite-oxidising bacteria (Nitrobacter, Nitrospira). Both steps are chemolithoautotrophic, with low energy yields and slow growth. Daims et al. (2015) and van Kessel et al. (2015) found Nitrospira strains carrying both AMO and NXR that carry out complete ammonia oxidation (comammox) in one cell, which overturned the assumption that nitrification must be a two-organism process and may explain nitrification in oligotrophic and engineered systems. Controls: O₂ (obligately aerobic; AMO needs O₂ as co-substrate), NH₄⁺/NH₃ supply (substrate; pH dependence through the NH₃/NH₄⁺ equilibrium, with AOA tolerant of low pH), temperature (optimum 25–35 °C), and moisture, with a maximum near 60% WFPS where substrate diffusion in water and O₂ diffusion in air are jointly adequate. N₂O is released as a by-product of HAO chemistry and, under low O₂, by nitrifier denitrification.

2. Explain the hole-in-the-pipe model and use it to predict how NO, N₂O and N₂ emissions change as a dry soil is wetted to saturation.

Firestone and Davidson (1989) represent N flowing through a pipe at a rate set by nitrification plus denitrification (controlled by N availability); gases leak through holes whose sizes depend on soil conditions, chiefly O₂ status set by water-filled pore space (WFPS), plus pH and carbon. Wetting a dry soil: at low WFPS (< 40%) nitrification proceeds where water films allow, O₂ is abundant, and the small gaseous leak is mostly NO, which diffuses out before being consumed. Around 50–60% WFPS nitrification peaks and some N₂O is produced by nitrifiers; as WFPS passes 60–70%, O₂ diffusion falls (gas diffusivity scales with air-filled porosity), anoxic microsites and aggregate interiors appear, denitrification starts on the nitrate the nitrifiers just made, and N₂O production peaks (60–80%) because N₂O reductase is the most O₂-sensitive enzyme and N₂O escapes before being reduced. Above ~85% WFPS the soil is largely anoxic, gas escape is slow, nosZ is fully active and N₂ dominates; total gaseous loss can be large but N₂O flux falls. Hence the classic N₂O pulse after rain on dry fertilised soil and the "Birch-like" burst of mineralisation that accompanies rewetting. DayCent encodes this with WFPS functions for nitrification, denitrification and the N₂:N₂O ratio.

3. Derive the fraction of ammoniacal N present as NH₃ as a function of pH and compute it at pH 6, 8 and 9.5 at 25 °C. Why do urea applications lose more N than ammonium sulphate?

NH₄⁺ ⇌ NH₃ + H⁺ with Ka = [NH₃][H⁺]/[NH₄⁺]. Then [NH₃]/[NH₄⁺] = Ka/[H⁺] = 10pH − pKa, and the fraction f = [NH₃]/([NH₃] + [NH₄⁺]) = 1/(1 + 10pKa − pH). With pKa = 9.25: pH 6 → 1/(1 + 103.25) = 0.056%; pH 8 → 1/(1 + 101.25) = 5.3%; pH 9.5 → 1/(1 + 10−0.25) = 64%. pKa falls with temperature (≈ 0.09 + 2730/T), so warm soils volatilise more. Urea is hydrolysed by urease to NH₃ and CO₂, consuming protons and raising pH in the fertiliser microsite to 8–9 regardless of bulk soil pH, so a large fraction is NH₃ and can escape, especially from the surface of warm, dry or calcareous soils (10–40% loss). Ammonium sulphate is a physiologically acid salt: nitrification and uptake of NH₄⁺ release H⁺, lowering pH and keeping almost all N as NH₄⁺, so losses are small except on calcareous soils. Mitigation: incorporate or inject urea, apply before rain, use urease inhibitors (NBPT) or coated products.

4. What did Schimel and Bennett (2004) change about how we think of N mineralisation, and what are the implications for measuring N availability and for models?

The classical paradigm held that mineralisation (NH₄⁺ production) was the rate-limiting step of plant N supply, that microbes always out-competed plants for mineral N, and that plants used only NH₄⁺ and NO₃⁻ after microbial demand was met. Schimel and Bennett argued that the bottleneck is depolymerisation of N-containing polymers by extracellular enzymes into amino acids and other monomers; that plants, mycorrhizal fungi and free-living microbes compete for these monomers at the microsite scale; and that NH₄⁺ release occurs when microbes are C-limited and have N in excess. They proposed a gradient: in very N-poor systems plants rely on organic N and microsite competition; with increasing N availability microbes become N-saturated, NH₄⁺ accumulates and is nitrified, and the mineral-N view becomes adequate. Implications: net mineralisation incubations underestimate supply and ignore organic N uptake; gross rates (¹⁵N pool dilution) and amino-acid fluxes matter; plant traits such as mycorrhizal type and protease production determine competitive ability; models that supply N to plants only after microbial demand is satisfied and only as NH₄⁺/NO₃⁻ (most land models) misrepresent N-poor systems, which is one motivation for FUN in CLM5 (mycorrhizal and organic N routes) and for microbial-explicit N models.

5. Why does nitrogen limit productivity in most terrestrial ecosystems when the atmosphere is 78% N₂ and some organisms can fix it?

Vitousek and Howarth (1991) gave four groups of reasons. (i) Energetics and constraints on fixers: breaking the N≡N bond costs 16 ATP per N₂ plus the cost of excluding O₂, so fixers are at a competitive disadvantage whenever mineral N is available; they are also limited by P, Mo and Fe, by light (shade in closed forests), by grazing on N-rich tissue, and by cold. (ii) Loss pathways unique to N: denitrification, NH₃ volatilisation, fire, and leaching of the mobile nitrate anion continuously remove N, whereas P is retained by sorption; so ecosystems leak N and must keep fixing to stay even. (iii) Timescales: after disturbance, glaciation or on young substrates, N must accumulate from near zero through fixation (centuries), while P is supplied by weathering of parent material. (iv) Stoichiometric coupling: most N is in organic matter with slow turnover, so supply depends on decomposition. Vitousek and Field (1999) modelled the fixer paradox, showing that fixers are favoured only where N is scarce and other resources (P, light) adequate, explaining their rarity in N-poor temperate forests and abundance in tropical forests. Consequences: added N stimulates growth (fertilisation and deposition experiments), and the long-term CO₂ fertilisation effect depends on whether new N can be acquired.

6. Compare DayCent and CLM5 in how they compute nitrification, denitrification and plant N uptake. Which features control the simulated N₂O response to fertiliser and the N limitation of CO₂ fertilisation?

DayCent: nitrification = f(NH₄⁺) × f(T) × f(WFPS) × f(pH) with a fixed ~2% N₂O yield and NO derived from N₂O via a diffusivity function; denitrification = min of NO₃⁻-, heterotrophic-respiration- (electron donor) and WFPS-limited rates, partitioned into N₂ and N₂O by a ratio that rises with WFPS and with NO₃⁻/CO₂; plant uptake is demand-driven from fixed C:N allocation, taking whatever mineral N is available after microbial immobilisation. CLM5 uses the same DayCent gas formulations for its mineral N pools but Century-style decomposition with vertically resolved soil, and plant acquisition through FUN: N is obtained by active uptake, mycorrhizal uptake, retranslocation or symbiotic fixation, each with a carbon cost depending on root biomass and soil N, with flexible leaf C:N and LUNA linking leaf N to photosynthetic capacity. The N₂O response to fertiliser in both is governed by the WFPS and temperature modifiers, the fixed nitrification yield and the denitrification partitioning; neither reproduces Shcherbak's exponential rate response unless N accumulates beyond uptake. The N limitation of CO₂ fertilisation in CLM5 depends on the FUN cost curves, the BNF option, leaf C:N flexibility, and the leaching and gas loss rates, which together determine how much new N enters relative to the N sequestered in growing biomass and SOM (progressive N limitation).

7. Explain why soil and plant δ¹⁵N increase along gradients of increasing N loss, and how Houlton and Bai used this to constrain global denitrification.

Enzymes discriminate against ¹⁵N, so the products of nitrification, denitrification and volatilisation are depleted and the residual substrate is enriched. Fractionation is expressed only when a reaction is incomplete and the product is removed from the system (Rayleigh distillation): δresidual = δ₀ + ε ln f, where f is the fraction remaining. In a closed, tightly cycling ecosystem, N is taken up and recycled almost completely, so little fractionation is expressed and δ¹⁵N stays close to that of the inputs (fixation ~0‰, deposition −5 to +5‰). In open systems, where a large fraction of mineralised N is nitrified and then lost as NO₃⁻ gas or leachate with low δ¹⁵N, the N retained in soil and plants becomes progressively heavier; the enrichment is greatest when the losses are gaseous (large ε) rather than leaching (small ε for NO₃⁻ leaving, but the nitrate is itself depleted). Houlton and Bai (2009) combined a global map of soil and plant δ¹⁵N with an isotope mass balance of inputs and outputs, solving for the gaseous fraction of losses needed to produce the observed enrichment; they concluded that gaseous losses, mostly denitrification, constitute roughly a third of total N losses from unmanaged terrestrial ecosystems, with the highest fractions in tropical and arid regions. This is an integrative constraint that direct flux measurements cannot provide.

8. A fertilised maize field emits N₂O at 1% of applied N at 150 kg N ha⁻¹. Discuss whether halving the rate halves emissions, and what else changes.

Under the IPCC Tier 1 assumption, direct N₂O is linear in applied N, so halving the rate would halve emissions (from 1.5 to 0.75 kg N₂O-N ha⁻¹). Shcherbak, Millar and Robertson (2014) showed from 78 studies that the response is better described as exponential, with the emission factor itself increasing with N rate, especially above the rate at which crop uptake saturates; emissions from the surplus N (N applied beyond crop demand) are disproportionately large. Halving an above-optimum rate therefore cuts emissions by more than half, while halving a below-optimum rate cuts them by less and reduces yield. Other consequences: lower NO₃⁻ leaching and indirect N₂O (IPCC counts 0.75% of leached N and 1% of volatilised N as indirect N₂O), lower NH₃ volatilisation if urea is used, and possibly lower residue N returning to the soil, which affects the following season's mineralisation. Mechanistically, in hole-in-the-pipe terms the rate reduction shrinks the flow through the pipe; the holes (set by WFPS, temperature, C, pH) are unchanged, so the timing of emissions (after rain, at thaw) stays the same. Management levers that act on the holes include drainage, avoiding application before heavy rain, nitrification inhibitors (DCD, DMPP) and controlled-release sources.
