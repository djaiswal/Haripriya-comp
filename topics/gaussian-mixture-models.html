Gaussian Mixture Models

A density built from a few Gaussians, each with its own weight, centre and covariance, fitted by the expectation–maximisation algorithm. It is k-means with uncertainty, ellipses and a likelihood, and it is the simplest place to learn EM, which reappears in missing-data problems, hidden Markov models and data assimilation.

The one-paragraph version

A Gaussian mixture model (GMM) says that each observation was generated by first picking one of \(K\) components with probabilities \(\pi_k\) and then drawing from that component's Gaussian \(\mathcal N(x\,|\,\mu_k,\Sigma_k)\). The component label \(z\) is a latent variable: never observed, but inferable. The marginal density is \(p(x)=\sum_k\pi_k\,\mathcal N(x|\mu_k,\Sigma_k)\), and the log-likelihood of a sample contains a log of a sum, so setting its gradient to zero gives equations in which the unknowns appear on both sides: no closed-form maximum-likelihood estimate. EM breaks the circularity. The E-step computes responsibilities \(\gamma_{nk}=p(z_n=k\,|\,x_n)\) with the current parameters; the M-step re-estimates \(\pi_k,\mu_k,\Sigma_k\) as responsibility-weighted proportions, means and covariances. Each cycle cannot decrease the likelihood, so EM converges, though only to a local maximum, and it needs a sensible start (usually k-means) and a guard against a component collapsing onto a single point. The number of components and the covariance structure are chosen with BIC or ICL. GMMs give soft clusters, a density estimate for anomaly detection and simulation, and a generative classifier; in ecosystem science they describe multimodal soil and trait distributions, unmix pixels and footprints, and underlie Gaussian mixture filters and Bayesian model averaging.

Examiner's lens

Three things get asked: write the log-likelihood and explain why it cannot be maximised in closed form; derive the EM updates (at least \(\mu_k\) and \(\pi_k\), ideally \(\Sigma_k\)) and say why the likelihood increases; and compare with k-means (soft vs hard, elliptical vs spherical, \(\sigma^2\to0\)). A good answer also mentions singularities, BIC and the fact that EM is a general recipe for latent-variable models, not a GMM-specific trick.

Mixtures as latent-variable models

Let \(z\in\{1,\dots,K\}\) be a categorical latent variable with \(p(z=k)=\pi_k\), \(\pi_k\ge0\), \(\sum_k\pi_k=1\), and let \(p(x\,|\,z=k)=\mathcal N(x\,|\,\mu_k,\Sigma_k)\) with

$$\mathcal N(x\,|\,\mu,\Sigma)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\lvert\Sigma\rvert^{1/2}}\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12\,(x-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\Big).$$

Marginalising the latent variable gives the mixture density

$$p(x\,|\,\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\mathcal N(x\,|\,\mu_k,\Sigma_k),\qquad \theta=\{\pi_k,\mu_k,\Sigma_k\}_{k=1}^K .$$

Three remarks. First, a mixture is a legitimate density for any \(K\) and a universal approximator: with enough components it can approximate any continuous density, which is why GMMs are used for density estimation independently of whether "clusters" exist. Second, the mixture is identifiable only up to label switching: permuting the component indices gives the same density, so there are \(K!\) equivalent maxima, and in Bayesian treatments posterior summaries must respect this. Beyond permutation, finite Gaussian mixtures are identifiable (Yakowitz & Spragins 1968), unlike, say, mixtures of uniforms. Third, mixtures with \(\pi_k=0\) or two identical components are degenerate: \(K\) components can collapse to fewer, which is one reason the likelihood-ratio test for \(K\) vs \(K+1\) does not have its usual \(\chi^2\) distribution and why BIC is used instead.

The incomplete-data log-likelihood and why there is no closed form

For independent observations \(X=\{x_1,\dots,x_N\}\) the (incomplete-data, because \(z\) is missing) log-likelihood is

$$\ell(\theta)=\log p(X\,|\,\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log\Big\{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\mathcal N(x_n\,|\,\mu_k,\Sigma_k)\Big\}.$$

The logarithm of a sum does not simplify. Differentiate with respect to \(\mu_k\):

$$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mu_k}=\sum_n\underbrace{\frac{\pi_k\,\mathcal N(x_n|\mu_k,\Sigma_k)}{\sum_j\pi_j\,\mathcal N(x_n|\mu_j,\Sigma_j)}}_{\gamma_{nk}}\;\Sigma_k^{-1}(x_n-\mu_k)=0\;\Longrightarrow\;\mu_k=\frac{\sum_n\gamma_{nk}\,x_n}{\sum_n\gamma_{nk}} .$$

This looks like a solution, but \(\gamma_{nk}\) depends on \(\mu_k\) (and on every other parameter), so it is a fixed-point equation, not a formula. The same happens for \(\Sigma_k\) and \(\pi_k\). EM is precisely the strategy of iterating this fixed point: evaluate the \(\gamma\)'s at the current parameters, then solve the equations as if the \(\gamma\)'s were constants.

Responsibilities

The quantity that appeared in the derivative is the posterior probability of the latent label given the data point, obtained by Bayes' rule:

$$\gamma_{nk}\equiv p(z_n=k\,|\,x_n,\theta)=\frac{p(z_n=k)\,p(x_n|z_n=k)}{\sum_j p(z_n=j)\,p(x_n|z_n=j)}=\frac{\pi_k\,\mathcal N(x_n|\mu_k,\Sigma_k)}{\sum_{j}\pi_j\,\mathcal N(x_n|\mu_j,\Sigma_j)} .$$

The \(\gamma_{nk}\) are called responsibilities (component \(k\) "takes responsibility" for a share of point \(n\)); they sum to one over \(k\), and \(N_k=\sum_n\gamma_{nk}\) is the effective number of points in component \(k\). Numerically they are computed in log space with the log-sum-exp trick, otherwise the Gaussian densities underflow in more than a few dimensions.

The EM algorithm

Derivation: a lower bound that touches the log-likelihood

Introduce any distribution \(q(Z)\) over the latent labels \(Z=\{z_n\}\). Because \(p(X|\theta)=p(X,Z|\theta)/p(Z|X,\theta)\) for every \(Z\), taking expectations under \(q\) gives the exact decomposition

$$\log p(X|\theta)=\underbrace{\sum_Z q(Z)\log\frac{p(X,Z|\theta)}{q(Z)}}_{\mathcal L(q,\theta)}\;+\;\underbrace{\sum_Z q(Z)\log\frac{q(Z)}{p(Z|X,\theta)}}_{\mathrm{KL}\big(q\,\|\,p(Z|X,\theta)\big)\;\ge\;0}.$$

Since the KL divergence is non-negative, \(\mathcal L(q,\theta)\le\log p(X|\theta)\): \(\mathcal L\) is the evidence lower bound (ELBO). Equivalently, apply Jensen's inequality to the concave logarithm: \(\log\sum_Z q(Z)\frac{p(X,Z|\theta)}{q(Z)}\ge\sum_Z q(Z)\log\frac{p(X,Z|\theta)}{q(Z)}\). EM alternately maximises \(\mathcal L\) over \(q\) and over \(\theta\):

  • E-step. With \(\theta^{\text{old}}\) fixed, \(\mathcal L\) is maximised over \(q\) by making the KL term zero, i.e. \(q(Z)=p(Z|X,\theta^{\text{old}})\). The bound then touches the log-likelihood at \(\theta^{\text{old}}\). For a mixture the posterior factorises over points, and \(q(z_n=k)=\gamma_{nk}\): the E-step is just computing responsibilities.
  • M-step. With \(q\) fixed, \(\mathcal L(q,\theta)=\mathbb E_q[\log p(X,Z|\theta)]+\text{const}\). The first term is the expected complete-data log-likelihood \(Q(\theta,\theta^{\text{old}})\); maximise it over \(\theta\).

Monotonicity follows immediately: \(\log p(X|\theta^{\text{new}})\ge\mathcal L(q,\theta^{\text{new}})\ge\mathcal L(q,\theta^{\text{old}})=\log p(X|\theta^{\text{old}})\). The first inequality is the bound, the second is the M-step maximisation, and the equality is the E-step tightening. The likelihood is non-decreasing, so for a bounded likelihood EM converges in \(\ell\); Wu (1983) gave conditions under which the parameter sequence converges to a stationary point, which may be a local maximum or a saddle. Convergence is linear (first order) and slow when components overlap heavily, because the fraction of missing information is then large (Dempster, Laird & Rubin 1977; Redner & Walker 1984).

The M-step for a Gaussian mixture, derived

The complete-data log-likelihood uses one-hot indicators \(z_{nk}\):

$$\log p(X,Z|\theta)=\sum_n\sum_k z_{nk}\big[\log\pi_k+\log\mathcal N(x_n|\mu_k,\Sigma_k)\big],$$
$$Q(\theta,\theta^{\text{old}})=\mathbb E_{Z|X,\theta^{\text{old}}}\big[\log p(X,Z|\theta)\big]=\sum_n\sum_k\gamma_{nk}\big[\log\pi_k+\log\mathcal N(x_n|\mu_k,\Sigma_k)\big].$$

Taking the expectation replaced \(z_{nk}\) by \(\gamma_{nk}\), and crucially the log now sits inside the sum, so \(Q\) separates into independent, weighted single-Gaussian problems. Writing \(N_k=\sum_n\gamma_{nk}\):

Means. \(\partial Q/\partial\mu_k=\sum_n\gamma_{nk}\Sigma_k^{-1}(x_n-\mu_k)=0\) gives \(\mu_k^{\text{new}}=\frac{1}{N_k}\sum_n\gamma_{nk}x_n\), a weighted mean.

Covariances. Using \(\partial\log|\Sigma|/\partial\Sigma^{-1}=-\Sigma\) and \(\partial\,(x-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)/\partial\Sigma^{-1}=(x-\mu)(x-\mu)^\top\), differentiate \(Q=\sum_n\gamma_{nk}\big[\tfrac12\log|\Sigma_k^{-1}|-\tfrac12(x_n-\mu_k)^\top\Sigma_k^{-1}(x_n-\mu_k)\big]+\dots\) with respect to \(\Sigma_k^{-1}\):

$$\tfrac12 N_k\,\Sigma_k-\tfrac12\sum_n\gamma_{nk}(x_n-\mu_k)(x_n-\mu_k)^\top=0\;\Longrightarrow\;\Sigma_k^{\text{new}}=\frac{1}{N_k}\sum_n\gamma_{nk}\,(x_n-\mu_k^{\text{new}})(x_n-\mu_k^{\text{new}})^\top ,$$

a weighted covariance. Mixing weights. Maximise \(\sum_k N_k\log\pi_k\) subject to \(\sum_k\pi_k=1\) with a Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda\): \(N_k/\pi_k+\lambda=0\Rightarrow\pi_k=-N_k/\lambda\); summing over \(k\) gives \(\lambda=-N\), hence

$$\pi_k^{\text{new}}=\frac{N_k}{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_n\gamma_{nk}.$$

So the M-step is exactly what you would do if the labels were known, with each point split fractionally among components. Put together:

initialise θ (e.g. from k-means: μ_k = centroids, Σ_k = within-cluster cov, π_k = N_k/N) repeat E-step: γ_nk ← π_k N(x_n|μ_k,Σ_k) / Σ_j π_j N(x_n|μ_j,Σ_j) M-step: N_k ← Σ_n γ_nk ; π_k ← N_k/N ; μ_k ← Σ_n γ_nk x_n / N_k Σ_k ← Σ_n γ_nk (x_n−μ_k)(x_n−μ_k)ᵀ / N_k (+ εI if regularising) evaluate ℓ(θ) = Σ_n log Σ_k π_k N(x_n|μ_k,Σ_k) until Δℓ < tolerance

Initialisation, local maxima, singularities

Because EM climbs to the nearest stationary point, the start matters as much as for k-means. Standard practice (mclust, scikit-learn) is to run k-means (itself k-means++ seeded), use its clusters to set \(\pi_k,\mu_k,\Sigma_k\), and then run EM; several random restarts and keeping the highest \(\ell\) is the safeguard. Hierarchical model-based agglomeration is mclust's alternative start.

A more serious pathology is the singularity. Let component \(k\) have \(\mu_k=x_n\) for some data point and \(\Sigma_k=\sigma^2 I\). Its contribution to the likelihood of \(x_n\) is \((2\pi\sigma^2)^{-d/2}\), which tends to infinity as \(\sigma\to0\); because the other components keep the remaining points' likelihood finite, the total log-likelihood is unbounded above. The MLE does not exist in the strict sense: there are infinitely many spikes in the likelihood surface, one per data point, next to the well-behaved local maximum we want. In practice EM wanders into one when a component is responsible for very few points (fewer than \(d+1\) in \(d\) dimensions makes \(\Sigma_k\) singular). Remedies: add a small ridge \(\varepsilon I\) to each covariance; impose a floor on eigenvalues; reset a collapsing component to a random point; or do MAP-EM with a conjugate prior (inverse-Wishart on \(\Sigma_k\), Dirichlet on \(\pi\)) whose M-step is \(\Sigma_k\leftarrow(\sum_n\gamma_{nk}(x_n-\mu_k)(x_n-\mu_k)^\top+\Psi)/(N_k+\nu+d+1)\), which never collapses. A fully Bayesian (variational) treatment removes the problem altogether because the posterior integrates over \(\Sigma\) rather than maximising. Covariance structures and model selection

The covariance matrices carry most of the parameters, and in \(d\) dimensions a full \(\Sigma_k\) has \(d(d+1)/2\) of them per component. Constraining their form trades flexibility for stability, especially when \(N_k\) is small relative to \(d\):

The k-means limit is the spherical model with equal \(\sigma^2\) and equal \(\pi_k\).

Choosing K and the structure

Because \(\ell\) increases monotonically with \(K\) and with covariance flexibility, it must be penalised. The usual criteria are, for \(p\) free parameters and \(N\) observations:

$$\text{AIC}=-2\ell+2p,\qquad \text{BIC}=-2\ell+p\ln N,$$
$$\text{ICL}=\text{BIC}-2\sum_n\sum_k\gamma_{nk}\ln\gamma_{nk}=\text{BIC}+2\times\text{(entropy of the responsibilities)}.$$

Lower is better in all three (mclust reports BIC with the opposite sign). BIC (Schwarz 1978) approximates \(-2\log\) of the marginal likelihood and is consistent for the number of components under regularity conditions that mixtures only partly satisfy, but it works well empirically (Fraley & Raftery 2002) and is the default. AIC penalises less and tends to over-fit \(K\) for large \(N\). ICL (Biernacki, Celeux & Govaert 2000) adds the entropy of the responsibilities, so it penalises solutions whose components overlap: it selects the number of well-separated clusters, whereas BIC selects the number of Gaussians needed to approximate the density. A skewed single population, for instance, may need two or three Gaussians for BIC but counts as one cluster for ICL. Cross-validated likelihood (hold out data, evaluate \(\ell\) on it) is the model-free alternative and the right choice when the goal is density estimation or prediction. In all cases compare over the same data and standardisation, and remember that \(K\) and the covariance structure interact: an elongated cluster is one full-covariance component or two or three spherical ones.

What a fitted mixture is good for

Density estimation. \(p(x)\) itself is the product: smooth, parametric, cheap to evaluate and to sample from. It is the usual parametric alternative to kernel density estimation when \(d\) is more than two or three.

Anomaly detection. Points with low mixture density (\(\log p(x)\) below, say, the 1st percentile of training values) are flagged, which in flux or sensor data catches spikes and instrument drift without defining a rule per variable. Because the score is a likelihood, thresholds have a probabilistic meaning.

Soft clustering. \(\gamma_{nk}\) assigns each site, pixel or species a membership vector; the entropy \(-\sum_k\gamma_{nk}\log\gamma_{nk}\) measures how ambiguous its classification is. Transitional ecotones and intermediate soils get intermediate memberships instead of being forced to one side of a line.

Generative classification. If class labels are known, fitting one Gaussian per class and classifying by posterior \(p(c|x)\propto\pi_c\mathcal N(x|\mu_c,\Sigma_c)\) is quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA); with a tied covariance it is linear discriminant analysis (LDA), whose boundaries are hyperplanes because the quadratic terms cancel. A GMM is therefore QDA/LDA with the labels missing, and EM is how the missing labels are handled. Using several Gaussians per class gives mixture discriminant analysis.

Mixtures of regressions and experts. Replace the component density by a regression, \(y|x\sim\sum_k\pi_k\mathcal N(y|x^\top\beta_k,\sigma_k^2)\), and EM finds several hidden regimes with different slopes (e.g. two respiration–temperature relationships for wet and dry soil). Making the weights depend on the input, \(\pi_k(x)\) (a softmax gate), gives the mixture of experts, a soft piecewise model that is also fitted by EM.

Infinite mixtures. A Dirichlet process mixture (Rasmussen 2000) puts a prior over partitions with an unbounded number of components, using the stick-breaking weights \(\pi_k=v_k\prod_{j<k}(1-v_j)\), \(v_j\sim\text{Beta}(1,\alpha)\); the number of occupied components is inferred from the data rather than chosen by BIC, at the cost of MCMC or variational inference and a concentration parameter \(\alpha\) that influences the answer.

Variational inference versus EM. EM maximises \(\mathcal L(q,\theta)\) with \(q\) exact and \(\theta\) a point estimate. Variational Bayes treats \(\theta\) as latent too, restricts \(q(Z,\theta)\) to a factorised family and maximises the same ELBO; the result is a posterior over the parameters, automatic suppression of unneeded components (their \(\pi_k\to0\)) and no singularities. Blei, Kucukelbir & McAuliffe (2017) review the general machinery; EM is the special case with a delta-function \(q(\theta)\).

EM beyond mixtures. The derivation above never used the Gaussian form. Any model with unobserved variables yields the same recipe: missing covariates in a regression (E-step: expected sufficient statistics given observed values), hidden Markov models (E-step: forward–backward smoothing; M-step: transition and emission re-estimation, the Baum–Welch algorithm), factor analysis and probabilistic PCA (next page), and censored or truncated data. The pattern is always: fill in the missing part in expectation, then estimate as if complete.

Ecological applications

  • Multimodal soil and trait distributions. Soil pH, bulk density or carbon across a landscape with several parent materials or land uses is rarely unimodal; a GMM separates the populations without a prior map, estimates each mode's mean and spread, and gives every sample a membership (Lab B). Leaf trait distributions within a biome (LMA, N) can likewise be decomposed into functional groups, which is a soft, data-driven alternative to fixed PFT bins.
  • Mixed pixels. Clustering pixels in spectral or time-series space with a GMM yields fractional membership of each pixel in each spectral class, an unmixing surrogate. For moderate-resolution imagery over heterogeneous landscapes this is more faithful than hard k-means classes.
  • Flux-tower footprints. A footprint that samples two or three vegetation patches produces half-hourly fluxes drawn from a mixture; clustering footprint-weighted fluxes by wind sector with a GMM (or a mixture of regressions on drivers) recovers patch-specific responses.
  • Mortality and demography. Tree growth or vigour indices are often bimodal, with a slow-growing "at risk" subpopulation; a two-component mixture identifies it and provides a mortality-risk probability per tree that feeds a demographic model.
  • Phenological regimes. Fitting a GMM to phenometrics (start, peak, length of season) classifies pixels into regimes with uncertainty; BIC decides how many regimes the data support.
  • Gap-filling with uncertainty. Conditioning a fitted joint Gaussian mixture on observed variables gives a conditional mixture for the missing ones (each component conditions as a Gaussian, weights re-scaled by the component likelihood of the observed part). The conditional mean fills the gap and the conditional variance is an honest uncertainty, which marginal-distribution-sampling methods do not give.

Connections to ecosystem modelling

  • Gaussian mixture filters. The ensemble Kalman filter assumes a Gaussian forecast distribution; when the forecast is multimodal (snow / no snow, soil wet / dry regimes, a bifurcating phenology state) a Gaussian mixture represents the prior, each component is updated by its own Kalman step, and the mixing weights are updated by each component's likelihood of the observation (\(\pi_k^{+}\propto\pi_k\,p(y|k)\)). Particle filters are the limit of one tiny component per particle; GMMs sit between them and the EnKF.
  • Bayesian model averaging as a mixture. Raftery et al. (2005) write the BMA predictive distribution as \(p(y|\text{data})=\sum_k w_k\,g_k(y|f_k)\), a mixture over ensemble members (or models) with weights equal to posterior model probabilities and per-member dispersion; the weights and variances are estimated by EM on a training period. Multi-model ensembles of carbon-cycle or crop models can be combined the same way, and the mixture gives calibrated predictive intervals rather than a single average.
  • Parameter posteriors and equifinality. MCMC output for process-model parameters is often multimodal; summarising the posterior with a GMM (fitted to the chain) gives interpretable modes, their weights and covariances, a compact proposal distribution for further sampling, and an emulator-friendly prior for the next assimilation cycle.
  • Representing sub-grid heterogeneity. Instead of one mean soil moisture per grid cell, a two- or three-component mixture of soil states can be propagated (each with its own fluxes), which is how some land models represent tiles probabilistically.
  • Error models. Observation errors for eddy-covariance fluxes are heavy-tailed; a two-component Gaussian scale mixture (one narrow, one wide) is a practical likelihood for calibration that down-weights outliers, and is fitted by EM.
  • From GMM to PCA. Probabilistic PCA (next page) is a single Gaussian with low-rank-plus-noise covariance, and a mixture of PPCAs is the natural extension when the data lie on several local low-dimensional sheets; both are fitted by EM.

Exam-style questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer.

1. Write down the log-likelihood of a Gaussian mixture and explain precisely why it has no closed-form maximiser. Then derive the EM update for μk.

\(\ell(\theta)=\sum_n\log\sum_k\pi_k\mathcal N(x_n|\mu_k,\Sigma_k)\). For a single Gaussian the log acts on a product of exponentials and yields a quadratic whose stationary point is the sample mean. With a mixture the log acts on a sum of exponentials, which does not simplify, and differentiating gives \(\partial\ell/\partial\mu_k=\sum_n\gamma_{nk}\Sigma_k^{-1}(x_n-\mu_k)\) with \(\gamma_{nk}=\pi_k\mathcal N_{nk}/\sum_j\pi_j\mathcal N_{nj}\). Setting it to zero gives \(\mu_k=\sum_n\gamma_{nk}x_n/\sum_n\gamma_{nk}\), but \(\gamma_{nk}\) depends on all \(\mu_j,\Sigma_j,\pi_j\) including \(\mu_k\) itself, so this is a coupled nonlinear fixed-point system, not an explicit solution. EM solves it iteratively: in the M-step treat \(\gamma_{nk}\) as fixed numbers computed from the previous parameters (E-step); then \(Q(\theta,\theta^{old})=\sum_n\sum_k\gamma_{nk}[\log\pi_k+\log\mathcal N(x_n|\mu_k,\Sigma_k)]\) is a sum of weighted single-Gaussian log-likelihoods, \(\partial Q/\partial\mu_k=\sum_n\gamma_{nk}\Sigma_k^{-1}(x_n-\mu_k)=0\), and \(\mu_k^{new}=\sum_n\gamma_{nk}x_n/N_k\) is explicit because the \(\gamma\)'s are constants.

2. Prove that each EM iteration cannot decrease the log-likelihood. What does this imply, and not imply, about convergence?

For any \(q(Z)\): \(\log p(X|\theta)=\mathcal L(q,\theta)+\mathrm{KL}(q\|p(Z|X,\theta))\), where \(\mathcal L=\sum_Z q\log[p(X,Z|\theta)/q]\). Proof: write \(\log p(X|\theta)=\log p(X,Z|\theta)-\log p(Z|X,\theta)\), take expectations under \(q\), and add and subtract \(\sum_Z q\log q\). Since KL ≥ 0 (Gibbs' inequality, from Jensen on the concave log), \(\mathcal L\le\log p(X|\theta)\). E-step: set \(q=p(Z|X,\theta^{old})\), making KL = 0 and \(\mathcal L(q,\theta^{old})=\log p(X|\theta^{old})\). M-step: choose \(\theta^{new}=\arg\max_\theta\mathcal L(q,\theta)\), so \(\mathcal L(q,\theta^{new})\ge\mathcal L(q,\theta^{old})\). Then \(\log p(X|\theta^{new})\ge\mathcal L(q,\theta^{new})\ge\mathcal L(q,\theta^{old})=\log p(X|\theta^{old})\). Implications: \(\ell\) is monotone and, if bounded above, converges; under Wu's (1983) regularity conditions the parameters converge to a stationary point of \(\ell\). Not implied: that the point is the global maximum (local maxima and saddles are reachable), that convergence is fast (it is linear, slow with overlapping components), or that the likelihood is bounded at all (GMM singularities make it unbounded, so EM can diverge toward a spike unless regularised).

3. Derive the M-step updates for πk and Σk.

\(Q=\sum_n\sum_k\gamma_{nk}[\log\pi_k-\tfrac{d}{2}\log2\pi-\tfrac12\log|\Sigma_k|-\tfrac12(x_n-\mu_k)^\top\Sigma_k^{-1}(x_n-\mu_k)]\). Weights: maximise \(\sum_kN_k\log\pi_k+\lambda(\sum_k\pi_k-1)\); \(\partial/\partial\pi_k\): \(N_k/\pi_k+\lambda=0\Rightarrow\pi_k=-N_k/\lambda\); the constraint gives \(-\sum_kN_k/\lambda=1\Rightarrow\lambda=-N\), so \(\pi_k=N_k/N\). Covariances: write \(\Lambda_k=\Sigma_k^{-1}\); the relevant terms are \(\tfrac12N_k\log|\Lambda_k|-\tfrac12\sum_n\gamma_{nk}\mathrm{tr}[\Lambda_k(x_n-\mu_k)(x_n-\mu_k)^\top]\). Using \(\partial\log|\Lambda|/\partial\Lambda=\Lambda^{-1}=\Sigma\) and \(\partial\mathrm{tr}(\Lambda S)/\partial\Lambda=S\), the gradient is \(\tfrac12N_k\Sigma_k-\tfrac12\sum_n\gamma_{nk}(x_n-\mu_k)(x_n-\mu_k)^\top=0\), hence \(\Sigma_k=\frac{1}{N_k}\sum_n\gamma_{nk}(x_n-\mu_k)(x_n-\mu_k)^\top\) evaluated at the new \(\mu_k\). Both are the complete-data estimators with fractional counts. With a constraint (spherical: \(\sigma_k^2=\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_k)/d\); tied: \(\Sigma=\sum_k N_k\Sigma_k/N\)) the same algebra gives the pooled versions.

4. What is the singularity problem in maximum-likelihood GMMs, why does it not arise for a single Gaussian, and how do practical implementations avoid it?

Place one component's mean exactly on a data point \(x_n\) with covariance \(\sigma^2I\). Its density at that point is \((2\pi\sigma^2)^{-d/2}\), unbounded as \(\sigma\to0\). The other points' likelihood stays finite because the remaining components cover them, so the total log-likelihood grows without bound: the likelihood surface has a spike at every data point and no finite global maximum exists. For a single Gaussian the same collapse would send the density of every other point to zero (their Mahalanobis distances blow up), so the total likelihood tends to −∞ and the collapse is not favoured; it is the presence of other components that makes the spike affordable. In EM the collapse appears when a component becomes responsible for fewer than \(d+1\) points, making the weighted covariance singular. Remedies: add \(\varepsilon I\) to \(\Sigma_k\) (scikit-learn's reg_covar), floor the eigenvalues, detect a collapsing component and re-seed it, use MAP-EM with an inverse-Wishart prior (mclust's priorControl), or use variational Bayes where \(\Sigma_k\) is integrated out and the problem disappears. Good initialisation from k-means and several restarts also reduce the chance of wandering into a spike.

5. Compare k-means and GMM clustering. Show in what limit they coincide, and give an ecological case where the difference matters.

k-means: hard assignments, one isotropic variance shared by all clusters, equal implicit weights, linear Voronoi boundaries, minimises SSE, \(Kd\) parameters. GMM: soft responsibilities, per-component covariance (ellipses) and weight, quadratic boundaries, maximises a likelihood (so BIC, simulation and outlier scores are available), \((K-1)+Kd+Kd(d+1)/2\) parameters for full covariance. Limit: set \(\pi_k=1/K\), \(\Sigma_k=\sigma^2I\). Then \(\gamma_{nk}=\exp(-\lVert x_n-\mu_k\rVert^2/2\sigma^2)/\sum_j\exp(-\lVert x_n-\mu_j\rVert^2/2\sigma^2)\); as \(\sigma^2\to0\) the nearest centre's term dominates and \(\gamma_{nk}\to r_{nk}\in\{0,1\}\). The expected complete-data log-likelihood becomes \(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_n\sum_kr_{nk}\lVert x_n-\mu_k\rVert^2+\text{const}=-J/2\sigma^2+\text{const}\), so the M-step is the centroid update: EM reduces to Lloyd's algorithm. Ecological case: clustering sites in climate space where one group is a large diffuse continental cluster and another a tight maritime cluster, or trait clusters that are elongated along the leaf economics axis. k-means slices the elongated cluster and lets the tight cluster steal the edge of the diffuse one; a full-covariance GMM fits each shape and reports which sites are genuinely intermediate (\(\gamma\approx0.5\)) instead of silently assigning them.

6. How would you choose the number of components? Contrast BIC and ICL and explain why the likelihood-ratio test is problematic here.

Fit models for \(K=1,\dots,K_{max}\) (and several covariance structures), each from multiple initialisations, and compare penalised likelihoods: BIC \(=-2\ell+p\ln N\) approximates −2 log marginal likelihood and selects the number of Gaussian components needed to represent the density; ICL \(=\) BIC \(+2\sum_n\sum_k(-\gamma_{nk}\ln\gamma_{nk})\) adds the classification entropy, so overlapping components that leave many points ambiguous are penalised, and ICL selects the number of separated clusters. When a single population is skewed or heavy-tailed, BIC may choose 2–3 components to approximate the shape while ICL chooses 1; which is "right" depends on whether the goal is density estimation or classification. Cross-validated held-out log-likelihood is the model-free alternative for density estimation, and domain interpretability remains the final filter. The likelihood-ratio test between \(K\) and \(K+1\) components is problematic because the null model lies on the boundary of the parameter space (\(\pi_{K+1}=0\), or two components identical), so the parameters of the extra component are not identifiable under the null and the regularity conditions for the \(\chi^2\) asymptotics fail; a parametric bootstrap of the LRT distribution is the workable version.

7. A dataset of soil pH from a region contains two parent materials. Describe how you would fit a two-component mixture by EM, what you would report, and how you would initialise and check it.

Model: \(p(x)=\pi\mathcal N(x|\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)+(1-\pi)\mathcal N(x|\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\). Initialise from k-means with \(K=2\) (or from quantiles: means at the 25th and 75th percentiles, common variance, \(\pi=0.5\)). Iterate: E-step \(\gamma_{n}=\pi\mathcal N(x_n|\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)/p(x_n)\); M-step \(\pi=\bar\gamma\), \(\mu_1=\sum\gamma_nx_n/\sum\gamma_n\), \(\sigma_1^2=\sum\gamma_n(x_n-\mu_1)^2/\sum\gamma_n\), and likewise for component 2 with \(1-\gamma_n\); stop when \(\Delta\ell<10^{-6}\). Report \(\hat\pi,\hat\mu_k,\hat\sigma_k\) with bootstrap or observed-information standard errors, the log-likelihood, BIC against the one- and three-component fits, and the classification: each sample's \(\gamma_n\) and the pH threshold where \(\gamma=0.5\) (the Bayes boundary, solvable from a quadratic in \(x\) when variances differ). Checks: multiple starts give the same maximum; no component has \(\sigma\) near zero (singularity) or \(\pi\) near zero (degenerate); the fitted mixture density overlays the histogram and the QQ plot of the probability-integral transform is straight; a parametric bootstrap LRT confirms two components beat one; if parent-material labels exist for a subset, compare \(\gamma\) with them (ARI or a confusion matrix). Finally, note identifiability weakens quickly as \(|\mu_1-\mu_2|/\sigma\) falls below about 2, so standard errors will be large for strongly overlapping populations.

8. Explain how Gaussian mixtures enter ecosystem data assimilation and multi-model combination.

(i) Gaussian mixture filters. When the forecast distribution of a state (soil moisture, snow, phenological stage) is multimodal, a single Gaussian (the EnKF assumption) misplaces the update. Represent the prior as \(\sum_k\pi_k\mathcal N(x|\mu_k,P_k)\); for a linear-Gaussian observation each component is updated by its own Kalman gain, \(\mu_k^+=\mu_k+K_k(y-H\mu_k)\), \(P_k^+=(I-K_kH)P_k\), and the weights are updated by Bayes' rule with the component's marginal likelihood of the observation, \(\pi_k^+\propto\pi_k\,\mathcal N(y|H\mu_k,HP_kH^\top+R)\). Components that explain the observation gain weight; the ensemble version fits a GMM to the ensemble (or uses one kernel per member) before the update. (ii) Bayesian model averaging. Raftery et al. (2005) write the predictive density as a mixture over bias-corrected ensemble members, \(p(y)=\sum_kw_kg_k(y|f_k)\), with \(w_k\) the posterior probability that member \(k\) is best and \(g_k\) a Gaussian centred on its forecast; weights and variances are estimated by EM on a training window, treating "which member is best" as the latent variable. Applied to multi-model carbon or crop ensembles this gives calibrated predictive intervals and down-weights poor models; it is exactly a GMM in which the component means are supplied by the models rather than estimated. (iii) Posterior summaries. A GMM fitted to MCMC samples of process-model parameters captures multimodality (equifinality) compactly and serves as a proposal or prior in later cycles.
