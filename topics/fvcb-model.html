Topic 4 of the SPAC section

The FvCB Model

The Farquhar–von Caemmerer–Berry model in full: how three enzyme-level statements about Rubisco, RuBP regeneration and phosphate recycling become the A–Ci curve, how temperature, light, mesophyll conductance and stomata enter, and how the result is fitted, parameterised and embedded in land-surface models.

The one-paragraph version

FvCB [1] writes net CO₂ assimilation as carboxylation minus photorespiratory release minus mitochondrial respiration, A = Vc − 0.5Vo − Rd, and uses Rubisco kinetics to turn the oxygenation term into a function of chloroplast CO₂: Vo/Vc = 2Γ*/Cc. The carboxylation rate itself is set by whichever of three processes is slowest: the amount and kinetics of Rubisco (Vcmax, Kc, Ko), the regeneration of RuBP by electron transport (J, saturating at Jmax), or the export of triose phosphate and return of inorganic phosphate (Tp). Four leaf parameters (Vcmax, Jmax, Tp, Rd), a set of "universal" kinetic constants with Arrhenius temperature functions, and a light-response function for J therefore predict A for any Cc, irradiance, temperature and O₂. Coupled with CO₂ diffusion through stomata and mesophyll (A = gsc(Ca − Ci) = gm(Ci − Cc)) and a stomatal model, it gives leaf gas exchange; integrated over a canopy it is the photosynthesis scheme of CLM, JULES, ORCHIDEE, BioCro and most other process models.

Examiner's lens

Four things are asked again and again: (i) derive something (the 4 and 8, Γ*, the TPU equation with αg, Topt of the peaked Arrhenius function, the gm quadratic); (ii) name the assumptions behind each rate and say when they fail; (iii) explain what can and cannot be identified from an A–Ci curve; (iv) say exactly how a named land-surface model implements the scheme and where the implementation is weakest. This page is organised around those four.

From Rubisco kinetics to A = Vc − 0.5Vo − Rd

CO₂ and O₂ compete for the same enediol intermediate of RuBP at the Rubisco active site. Each reaction therefore follows Michaelis–Menten kinetics with the other gas as a competitive inhibitor, and both assume RuBP is saturating (which is what "Rubisco-limited" means):

$$V_c = \frac{V_{cmax}\,C_c}{C_c + K_c\left(1 + O/K_o\right)}, \qquad V_o = \frac{V_{omax}\,O}{O + K_o\left(1 + C_c/K_c\right)}$$

Dividing the two rates, the denominators cancel (multiply top and bottom of Vo by Kc/Ko to see it), leaving

$$\frac{V_o}{V_c} = \frac{V_{omax}K_c}{V_{cmax}K_o}\,\frac{O}{C_c} = \frac{1}{S_{c/o}}\,\frac{O}{C_c}, \qquad S_{c/o} = \frac{V_{cmax}K_o}{V_{omax}K_c}$$

Sc/o, the specificity factor, is a property of the enzyme and of temperature, independent of how much Rubisco the leaf holds. Two oxygenations yield two 2-phosphoglycolate, and the photorespiratory pathway converts these to one 3-PGA plus one CO₂ (glycine decarboxylase), so each oxygenation releases 0.5 CO₂. The Cc at which release balances fixation (0.5Vo = Vc) is

$$\Gamma^* = \frac{0.5\,O}{S_{c/o}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{V_o}{V_c} = \frac{2\Gamma^*}{C_c}$$

Substituting into the carbon balance A = Vc − 0.5Vo − Rd gives the single expression every FvCB equation shares:

$$A = V_c\left(1 - \frac{\Gamma^*}{C_c}\right) - R_d = V_c\,\frac{C_c - \Gamma^*}{C_c} - R_d$$

Everything else in the model is a statement about what sets Vc. The assumptions already made are worth listing because examiners probe them: steady state; Rubisco fully activated (carbamylated, activase keeping up); 0.5 CO₂ per oxygenation, i.e. all glycolate carbon except the decarboxylated half returns to the chloroplast (relaxed below with αg); Rd is the non-photorespiratory CO₂ release in the light, not dark respiration; and Cc is the CO₂ partial pressure at the enzyme, which is not Ci. With Bernacchi's constants (Kc = 405 µbar, Ko = 278 mbar, Γ* = 42.75 µbar at 25 °C [8]) Sc/o ≈ 2,450 on a partial-pressure basis, and in air the effective Km for CO₂, Kc(1 + O/Ko), is ≈ 710 µbar, three times Cc: Rubisco is always substrate-limited in a C3 leaf.

Vcmax is kcat times the number of active sites, roughly 60 µmol CO₂ g⁻¹ Rubisco s⁻¹ at 25 °C, which is why it tracks Rubisco content and hence leaf nitrogen (see scaling).

The three limiting rates

Rubisco-limited (Ac)

$$A_c = \frac{V_{cmax}\,(C_c - \Gamma^*)}{C_c + K_c\left(1 + O/K_o\right)} - R_d$$

This is the initial, steeply rising part of the A–Cc curve. Its slope at the compensation point is Vcmax/(Γ* + Km) and its curvature is set entirely by the kinetic constants, so with Kc, Ko and Γ* fixed the only free quantity is Vcmax.

RuBP-regeneration-limited (Aj)

If RuBP is not saturating, carboxylation is paced by how fast the Calvin cycle can regenerate it, which at saturating CO₂ is paced by the supply of NADPH and ATP from electron transport. Reducing the two 3-PGA from one carboxylation costs 2 NADPH = 4 e⁻; the 3-PGA plus phosphoglycolate from one oxygenation cost the same 4 e⁻ once the glycolate is salvaged. So J = 4Vc + 4Vo = 4Vc(1 + 2Γ*/Cc), hence Vc = JCc/(4Cc + 8Γ*) and

$$A_j = \frac{J\,(C_c - \Gamma^*)}{4C_c + 8\Gamma^*} - R_d \quad\text{(NADPH-limited form)}$$
$$A_j = \frac{J\,(C_c - \Gamma^*)}{4.5\,C_c + 10.5\,\Gamma^*} - R_d \quad\text{(ATP-limited form, 3 ATP per } V_c,\ 3.5 \text{ per } V_o\text{)}$$

The ATP form divides by 3 ATP per carboxylation and 3.5 per oxygenation and converts ATP to electrons at 3 ATP per 4 e⁻ (von Caemmerer 2000 [3]). Which form to use depends on whether reductant or ATP limits the thylakoid; the NADPH form is almost universal in land models. J is the potential electron transport rate at the current irradiance, which saturates at Jmax; its light response is the subject of the next section. Note that Aj still rises with Cc, through the Γ* terms: more CO₂ diverts electrons from oxygenation to carboxylation.

Triose-phosphate-utilisation-limited (Ap)

Every three CO₂ fixed export one triose phosphate, whose consumption in sucrose and starch synthesis releases the Pi the cycle needs for ATP regeneration. If export (Tp, µmol triose-P m⁻² s⁻¹) cannot keep up, A is capped at 3Tp − Rd regardless of CO₂. Harley & Sharkey (1991) generalised this to allow a fraction αg of glycolate carbon to leave the cycle as glycine/serine instead of returning as glycerate, which provides an outlet that bypasses the Pi bottleneck:

$$A_p = \frac{3\,T_p\,(C_c - \Gamma^*)}{C_c - (1 + 3\alpha_g)\Gamma^*} - R_d$$
Derivation of the αg form (and why A falls with CO₂ under TPU limitation)

Per oxygenation two carbons leave the cycle as 2-phosphoglycolate; 1.5 return as glycerate and 0.5 is released as CO₂. If a fraction αg of the glycerate carbon does not return, the return is 1.5(1 − αg)Vo and the cycle's carbon balance at steady state is Vc − 2Vo + 1.5(1 − αg)Vo = 3Tp, i.e. Vc − 0.5Vo − 1.5αgVo = 3Tp. The leaf balance is still A + Rd = Vc − 0.5Vo, so A + Rd = 3Tp + 1.5αgVo. Substitute Vo = 2Γ*Vc/Cc and Vc = (A + Rd)Cc/(Cc − Γ*): A + Rd = 3Tp + 3αgΓ*(A + Rd)/(Cc − Γ*). Rearranging gives the equation above. Because the factor (Cc − Γ*)/(Cc − (1 + 3αg)Γ*) decreases towards 1 as Cc rises, Ap declines with CO₂ when αg > 0 and rises when O₂ increases: the "reversed sensitivity" seen in TPU-limited leaves. Busch, Sage & Farquhar (2018) extend the same logic to separate glycine and serine export (αG, αS).

The model in one line

A = min(Ac, Aj, Ap), with Ax = (Cc − Γ*) · ax / (Cc + bx) − Rd

All three rates have the same hyperbolic form with (a, b) = (Vcmax, Kc(1 + O/Ko)) for Rubisco, (J/4, 2Γ*) for RuBP regeneration, and (3Tp, −(1 + 3αg)Γ*) for TPU. This shared form is what makes the mesophyll-conductance quadratic and the coupled stomatal solution below apply to all three limitations with the same algebra.

The light dependence of J

FvCB treats the whole thylakoid as one saturating function of absorbed light. The usual choice is the non-rectangular hyperbola, the smaller root of θJ² − (I₂ + Jmax)J + I₂Jmax = 0:

$$J = \frac{I_2 + J_{max} - \sqrt{(I_2 + J_{max})^2 - 4\theta\,I_2\,J_{max}}}{2\theta}, \qquad I_2 = Q\,\alpha_{abs}\,\frac{1 - f}{2}$$

Q is incident PPFD, αabs ≈ 0.85 is leaf absorptance, f ≈ 0.15 corrects for the spectral quality of light (photons absorbed by non-photosynthetic pigments or lost at the reaction centre) and the ½ shares photons between two photosystems, so the maximum efficiency is 0.5 × 0.85 × 0.85 ≈ 0.36 e⁻ per incident photon, i.e. 0.09 CO₂ per photon in the absence of photorespiration. θ (0 to 1) sets how sharply the curve turns: θ = 0 is a rectangular hyperbola, θ = 1 a Blackman (two straight lines) response; measured values are 0.7–0.9. In the limit of low light J → I₂ (quantum efficiency), at high light J → Jmax. Some models (Collatz 1991 [4], JULES) skip Jmax entirely and write the light-limited rate as αQ(Cc − Γ*)/(Cc + 2Γ*) with α ≈ 0.08 mol CO₂ mol⁻¹ photon, relying on the co-limitation with Rubisco to produce saturation.

Choosing between the rates: minimum versus smoothing

Taking the strict minimum makes A switch limitation at a sharp corner. A real leaf is a population of chloroplasts with different light, CO₂ and Rubisco contents, and the whole-leaf curve is rounded. Kirschbaum & Farquhar (1984) and then Collatz et al. [4] therefore co-limit with a quadratic: the smoothed rate is the smaller root of

$$\theta_{cj}\,A_i^2 - (A_c + A_j)\,A_i + A_c A_j = 0, \qquad \theta_{ip}\,A^2 - (A_i + A_p)\,A + A_i A_p = 0$$

Collatz used θ = 0.98 for the Rubisco–RuBP pair and β = 0.95 for the export pair; CLM4.5/CLM5 use θcj = 0.98 (C3) or 0.80 (C4) and θip = 0.95, applied to the gross rates before Rd is subtracted. The effect is a few percent reduction in A near the transition, which matters for two reasons: at ambient CO₂ a well-acclimated leaf operates at the Ac/Aj transition (co-limitation is what optimal allocation of N between Rubisco and thylakoids predicts), and parameters fitted with a strict minimum are not interchangeable with parameters used in a smoothing model. In curve fitting the minimum is standard; in canopy models smoothing is standard. Know which your numbers came from.

Temperature functions

Every kinetic quantity in the model is temperature dependent. Enzyme constants (Kc, Ko, Γ*) and respiration follow an Arrhenius function, normalised to 25 °C with activation energy Ha (kJ mol⁻¹):

$$k(T) = k_{25}\,\exp\!\left[\frac{H_a\,(T - 298.15)}{298.15\,R\,T}\right] \;=\; \exp\!\left(c - \frac{H_a}{RT}\right)$$

with T in kelvin and R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; Ha = 65 kJ mol⁻¹ corresponds to a Q₁₀ of about 2.4 at 25 °C. Capacities (Vcmax, Jmax, Tp, gm) rise, peak and fall, which the peaked Arrhenius (modified Arrhenius, Johnson–Eyring–Williams) function captures with a deactivation enthalpy Hd and an entropy term ΔS (kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹):

$$k(T) = k_{25}\,\exp\!\left[\frac{H_a(T - 298.15)}{298.15\,R\,T}\right]\;\frac{1 + \exp\!\left(\dfrac{298.15\,\Delta S - H_d}{298.15\,R}\right)}{1 + \exp\!\left(\dfrac{T\,\Delta S - H_d}{R\,T}\right)}$$

Where the peak is

Setting d ln k/dT = 0 gives the optimum temperature (Medlyn et al. 2002 [10]):

$$T_{opt} = \frac{H_d}{\Delta S - R\,\ln\!\left(\dfrac{H_a}{H_d - H_a}\right)}$$

Ha sets how fast the function rises below the optimum, Hd how abruptly it collapses above it, and ΔS shifts the optimum: a 0.01 kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ decrease in ΔS moves Topt up by about 5 °C. Hd and ΔS are strongly correlated in fits, so almost every study fixes Hd = 200 kJ mol⁻¹ and fits ΔS.

Bernacchi et al. 2001 [8] measured, in transgenic tobacco with reduced Rubisco (so A was Rubisco-limited across the range), in vivo Arrhenius functions for Kc (Ha = 79.43), Ko (36.38), Γ* (37.83), Vcmax (65.33) and Rd (46.39 kJ mol⁻¹), with 25 °C values of 404.9 µbar, 278.4 mbar and 42.75 µbar. These are Ci-based (gm assumed infinite). Bernacchi et al. 2002 [9] repeated the analysis with gm measured by fluorescence, giving Cc-based constants (Kc ≈ 272 µbar, Ko ≈ 166 mbar, Γ* ≈ 37.4 µbar) and a peaked function for gm itself. Bernacchi et al. 2003 [11] added peaked functions for Jmax (Ha = 43.9, Hd = 152.3, ΔS = 0.495) and Tp (53.1, 201.8, 0.65), and a temperature dependence of θ. Medlyn et al. 2002 [10] compiled parameters from 19 datasets and standardised on the peaked function with Hd = 200 kJ mol⁻¹; Kattge & Knorr 2007 [12] reanalysed 36 species and found that ΔS, and hence Topt, shifts linearly with growth temperature: ΔSV = 668.39 − 1.07Tg and ΔSJ = 659.70 − 0.75Tg (J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, Tg in °C), with Ha = 71.5 (Vcmax) and 49.9 (Jmax) kJ mol⁻¹, and Jmax,25/Vcmax,25 = 2.59 − 0.035Tg. CLM4.5 and CLM5 use exactly these.

Mesophyll conductance: Cc is not Ci

Between the intercellular air space and the Rubisco active site CO₂ must dissolve, cross the cell wall, plasma membrane, cytosol, chloroplast envelope and stroma. The lumped mesophyll conductance gm (mol m⁻² s⁻¹ bar⁻¹) is of the same order as stomatal conductance (0.1–0.5), so the drawdown Ci − Cc = A/gm is typically 50–100 µbar, a quarter of Ci. Gas exchange measures Ci; the biochemistry responds to Cc. Ethier & Livingston [13] showed that substituting Cc = Ci − A/gm into any of the three rates turns the hyperbola in Cc into a non-rectangular hyperbola in Ci. For the Rubisco-limited rate:

$$A = \frac{-b + \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}, \quad a = -\frac{1}{g_m}, \quad b = \frac{V_{cmax} - R_d}{g_m} + C_i + K_m, \quad c = R_d\,(C_i + K_m) - V_{cmax}\,(C_i - \Gamma^*)$$

with Km = Kc(1 + O/Ko); the Aj form follows by replacing Vcmax with J/4 and Km with 2Γ*, and the TPU form by 3Tp and −(1 + 3αg)Γ*. The consequences for interpretation are large and frequently examined. (i) A Ci-based fit attributes the curvature caused by finite gm to the Michaelis–Menten curvature, so "apparent" Vcmax is 20–50% below the Cc-based value and the apparent Km is inflated. (ii) Because gm has its own peaked temperature response [9], a Ci-based Vcmax(T) is distorted; this is one reason activation energies for Vcmax scatter so widely across studies. (iii) The CO₂ compensation point measured by the Laisk method is in Ci: Ci* = Γ* − Rd/gm, slightly below Γ*. (iv) Constants must match: Bernacchi 2001 constants go with Cc = Ci; Bernacchi 2002 constants go with a finite gm. Mixing them is a common error.

gm is estimated by the variable-J method (fluorescence gives J, which with A fixes Cc; Harley et al. 1992 [7]), by ¹³C discrimination, by curve-fitting the Ethier–Livingston form, or from anatomy. It declines under drought and salinity, rises with temperature to about 35 °C, and is partly regulated by aquaporins and carbonic anhydrase. Most land-surface models still set Cc = Ci (and use Ci-based constants), which is internally consistent but means their Vcmax is the apparent, not the biochemical, value [16, 17].

Respiration in the light

Rd in the model is the rate of mitochondrial, non-photorespiratory CO₂ release while the leaf is illuminated, often written Rday or RL. It is not dark respiration. Light inhibits leaf respiration, typically by 30–50% (sometimes more), as the TCA cycle is partly bypassed and pyruvate dehydrogenase is down-regulated; this is the Kok effect, the break in the slope of the A–Q curve at very low light, first reported by Kok in 1948. Methods to estimate Rd (the Kok, Laisk and Yin methods) are covered on the response-curves page. In models Rd is usually a fixed fraction of Vcmax at 25 °C (0.015 in CLM and Collatz for C3; 0.011 in some others) with its own Arrhenius or Q₁₀ ≈ 2 temperature response, and some models now apply a light-inhibition factor during the day. Because Rd is small (1–2 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) relative to A it is hard to fit from A–Ci data, but it controls the compensation point Γ ≈ Γ* + RdKm/Vcmax and the low-light behaviour, so canopy GPP under dense shade is sensitive to it.

Coupling to diffusion: supply meets demand

FvCB gives the demand for CO₂ as a function of Cc. The supply is Fick's law through the boundary layer and stomata, A = gsc(Ca − Ci) with gsc = gsw/1.6 (and a boundary-layer term gbc = gbw/1.37 in series when the leaf is not in a well-stirred cuvette). The leaf operates where the two agree. In the A–Ci plane the supply function is a straight line from (Ca, 0) with slope −gsc, and the operating point is its intersection with the demand curve. Farquhar & Sharkey (1982) defined the stomatal limitation as l = (A∞ − A)/A∞, where A∞ is the demand-curve value at Ci = Ca, i.e. the assimilation the leaf would have with infinite stomatal conductance.

Solving the system: if gsc is known, substitute Ci = Ca − A/gsc into each rate; the algebra is identical to the gm quadratic with gsc in place of gm (or 1/(1/gsc + 1/gm) when both are finite). If gsc itself depends on A, as in Ball–Berry (gsw = g₀ + g₁ A hs/Cs) or Medlyn 2011 (gsw = g₀ + 1.6(1 + g₁/√D) A/Ca), substitution gives a cubic in A (Baldocchi 1994 derived it analytically; Yin & Struik 2009 did the same with gm included, the form ORCHIDEE uses). Most models instead iterate: guess Ci, compute A from FvCB, compute gs from the stomatal model, update Ci from the diffusion equation, repeat. CLM uses a hybrid Newton–Brent root finder on Ci; BioCro iterates to a tolerance; older schemes use fixed-point iteration, which can oscillate at low light. Iteration must also enclose the leaf energy balance when Tleaf is solved rather than prescribed.

Estimating parameters from A–Ci curves

The model has four leaf parameters (five with gm, six with αg) and a measured A–Ci curve has 10–15 points. Fitting is nonlinear least squares, but it is not a routine regression because each point must first be assigned a limitation state, and the state is itself a function of the parameters being estimated.

  • Sharkey et al. 2007 [14] formalised the standard workflow: convert to Cc with gm and use Cc-based constants [9]; assign points below roughly 300 µbar to Rubisco limitation and higher points to RuBP regeneration, with any flat or declining tail as TPU; minimise the summed squared error over Vcmax, J, Tp, Rd and gm; then check that the fitted Ac is indeed the minimum at the Rubisco-assigned points and vice versa, reassigning if not.
  • Gu et al. 2010 [15] made the assignment part of the estimation: enumerate every admissible partition of the points into the three states (an "admissible" fit is one whose fitted rates are consistent with the assignment), fit each, and keep the best. This avoids the subjective 300 µbar rule and shows how often a curve is consistent with several assignments.
  • Duursma 2015 [16] (plantecophys, R) fits Vcmax, Jmax and optionally Rd, Tp and gm, with a default method that searches for the transition Ci and a fast "bilinear" method that linearises each limitation; it exposes the parameter covariance so the Vcmax–Jmax–Rd correlations can be inspected.

Identifiability is the real difficulty. Kc, Ko and Γ* cannot be estimated from one curve and must be fixed from the literature. Vcmax and gm both control the curvature of the initial region, so they are strongly negatively correlated: a low gm with a high Vcmax fits almost as well as the reverse, and gm from curve fitting alone has wide confidence intervals unless there are many points at low Ci. Vcmax and Rd trade off through the intercept. J and Tp both set the plateau height; with αg = 0 the TPU line is flat and can only be distinguished from Aj if the curve continues to Ci ≈ 1,500 µbar where Aj still rises. Good practice: at least four points in the Rubisco-limited region, points to 1,500 µbar or beyond, Rd or gm measured independently where possible, and parameter uncertainty reported (profile or bootstrap intervals, not just standard errors) when treatments are compared.

Scaling relations and typical values

Because leaves allocate nitrogen between Rubisco and the thylakoid so that neither limits grossly, the parameters are correlated across species, which is what lets models carry fewer numbers. Jmax,25/Vcmax,25 is 1.6–2.0 (Wullschleger's 1993 survey of 109 species gives ≈ 1.64; Medlyn et al. [10] 1.67; Kattge & Knorr [12] 2.59 − 0.035Tg, i.e. 1.7 at 25 °C and 2.1 at 15 °C) and declines with growth temperature. Tp,25 ≈ Vcmax,25/6 (0.167, used in CLM). Rd,25 ≈ 0.01–0.02 Vcmax,25. Vcmax and leaf N: Rubisco holds 15–30% of leaf N, so Vcmax,25 rises roughly linearly with area-based N (Na, g m⁻²). Kattge et al. 2009 [17] fitted Vcmax,25 = i + sNa per plant functional type using the TRY database and found that models of the time used Vcmax values for tropical trees (≈ 65) well above the data (≈ 41 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹). Walker et al. 2014 [18] showed with a global dataset that P modulates the N relation (log Vcmax ≈ 3.7 + 0.65 log N + 0.15 log P with an interaction) and that Jmax scales almost isometrically with Vcmax. CLM uses a mechanistic chain instead: Vcmax,25 = Na × FLNR × FNR × aR25, where FLNR is the fraction of leaf N in Rubisco (PFT parameter), FNR = 7.16 g Rubisco g⁻¹ N and aR25 = 60 µmol CO₂ g⁻¹ Rubisco s⁻¹; CLM5 can alternatively predict Vcmax and Jmax with the LUNA optimality model.

Connections to ecosystem models

CLM5 (and CLM4.5) implements FvCB per sunlit and shaded leaf: Vcmax,25 from leaf N or LUNA; Kattge & Knorr acclimation of ΔS and Jmax/Vcmax to the 10-day mean air temperature (Tg clipped to 11–35 °C); Bernacchi constants; J from the non-rectangular hyperbola with θ = 0.7; Tp = 0.167Vcmax; the two smoothing quadratics (θcj = 0.98, θip = 0.95); Rd = 0.015Vcmax (with light inhibition in CLM5); the Medlyn 2011 stomatal model (Ball–Berry in CLM4.5); Cc = Ci; and a Newton–Brent iteration on Ci nested inside the leaf energy balance. Soil-moisture stress multiplies Vcmax (CLM4.5) or acts through plant hydraulics on gs (CLM5). JULES follows Collatz 1991/1992: Rubisco-, light- and export-limited rates co-limited with θ = 0.83–0.98, Vcmax from leaf N (ne parameter) with a Q₁₀ rise and logistic high- and low-temperature cut-offs (Tupp, Tlow), a light-limited rate αQ without Jmax, and a Jacobs/Cowan-type closure on Ci/Ca and humidity deficit. ORCHIDEE (since the Vuichard et al. 2019 revision) uses the Yin & Struik 2009 analytical solution of FvCB coupled to stomatal and mesophyll conductance, with Kattge & Knorr acclimation. BioCro uses FvCB with Bernacchi temperature functions and Ball–Berry stomata for C3 (solved iteratively for Ci) and the Collatz 1992 C4 model for maize and Miscanthus, integrated through a multilayer canopy at hourly steps. Big-leaf and two-leaf models (SiB2, de Pury & Farquhar 1997) scale Vcmax with canopy N and absorbed light, exploiting the fact that FvCB's Ac is linear in Vcmax and Aj nearly linear in J at low light. The P-model and other optimality schemes do not prescribe Vcmax at all: they assume the coordination hypothesis (Ac = Aj at mean daytime conditions) and solve FvCB for the Vcmax that satisfies it. In every case the FvCB layer is the one that converts CO₂, light and temperature into carbon, so its parameter uncertainty (above all Vcmax,25 and its temperature response) is the largest single source of spread in modelled GPP [19].

Known weaknesses

  • Acclimation. Vcmax,25, Jmax,25 and the temperature parameters are treated as fixed, but leaves adjust them over days to weeks in response to growth temperature, CO₂, light and N. Kattge & Knorr and Kumarathunge et al. (2019) provide empirical corrections; the acclimation page covers them.
  • TPU at low temperature and high CO₂. Phosphate limitation is common below ~20 °C and under elevated CO₂, and when αg > 0 produces A that falls with rising CO₂. Most land models either omit Ap or use a flat 3Tp, so they overestimate the CO₂ response in cool conditions and miss sink limitation entirely.
  • Sink and feedback limitation. Under elevated CO₂ the long-term down-regulation of Vcmax (less Rubisco per unit N, N dilution) is not emergent in FvCB; it must be imposed through an N cycle or empirical factor.
  • Steady state. Photosynthetic induction, Rubisco activation and stomatal lags (minutes) are ignored, which biases GPP under fluctuating light inside canopies.
  • Universal constants. Kc, Ko, Γ* and their activation energies come from one tobacco dataset and are applied to every species; the Rubisco kinetics of cold-adapted and warm-adapted species differ measurably.
  • Mesophyll conductance. Cc = Ci makes Vcmax "apparent" and distorts its temperature response; including gm changes the simulated CO₂ fertilisation and the inferred Vcmax–N relation.
  • High temperature. The decline above ~38 °C is attributed to peaked Arrhenius kinetics, but mechanistically Rubisco activase failure and membrane leakiness dominate, and the recovery (hysteresis) is not represented.

Exam-style questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer.

1. Starting from Michaelis–Menten kinetics with competitive inhibition, derive A = Vc(1 − Γ*/Cc) − Rd and define Γ* in terms of enzyme properties.

Write Vc = VcmaxCc/[Cc + Kc(1 + O/Ko)] and Vo = VomaxO/[O + Ko(1 + Cc/Kc)]. Multiplying numerator and denominator of Vo by Kc/Ko shows both rates share the denominator Cc + Kc + KcO/Ko (up to a factor), so Vo/Vc = (VomaxKc/VcmaxKo)(O/Cc) = O/(Sc/oCc). The photorespiratory pathway releases one CO₂ per two oxygenations, so the net rate is A = Vc − 0.5Vo − Rd. Define Γ* as the Cc at which 0.5Vo = Vc, i.e. Γ* = 0.5O/Sc/o; then Vo/Vc = 2Γ*/Cc and A = Vc(1 − Γ*/Cc) − Rd. Γ* therefore depends only on the specificity factor (a property of the enzyme, falling with temperature) and O₂; it is independent of Rubisco amount, light and RuBP supply, which is why A–Ci curves at different irradiances intersect at the same Cc. Assumptions: steady state, fully activated Rubisco, complete return of glycolate carbon other than the decarboxylated half.

2. Derive the coefficients 4 and 8 in Aj, then the 4.5 and 10.5 of the ATP-limited form. When would you prefer the ATP form?

Each carboxylation yields two 3-PGA, each needing one ATP and one NADPH for reduction to triose phosphate, plus a further ATP in regeneration: 3 ATP and 2 NADPH (4 e⁻) per Vc. Each oxygenation yields one 3-PGA (1 ATP, 1 NADPH) and one phosphoglycolate; recycling the glycolate costs a further ATP (glycerate kinase) and NADPH-equivalent (hydroxypyruvate reduction and NH₃ re-assimilation), with the regenerated RuBP costing 1 more ATP: 3.5 ATP and 2 NADPH (4 e⁻) per Vo in the FvCB accounting. Electron demand J = 4Vc + 4Vo = 4Vc(1 + 2Γ*/Cc), so Vc = JCc/(4Cc + 8Γ*) and Aj = J(Cc − Γ*)/(4Cc + 8Γ*) − Rd. For ATP: demand = 3Vc + 3.5Vo = Vc(3 + 7Γ*/Cc); converting ATP supply to electron equivalents at 4 e⁻ per 3 ATP (linear flow with Q-cycle) gives J(3/4) ≡ ATP, hence Vc = J Cc/(4.5Cc + 10.5Γ*). Use the ATP form when ATP, not reductant, is expected to limit, e.g. when alternative electron sinks (nitrate reduction, Mehler reaction) supply ATP-free reductant demand or when cyclic flow is restricted; most models use the NADPH form because its parameters were fitted that way.

3. Show that under TPU limitation with αg > 0, net assimilation decreases when CO₂ rises. Why does this matter for projecting photosynthesis under elevated CO₂ in cool climates?

Carbon balance of the Calvin cycle under phosphate limitation: Vc − 0.5Vo − 1.5αgVo = 3Tp, where 1.5αgVo is glycolate-derived carbon leaving as amino acids. Leaf balance: A + Rd = Vc − 0.5Vo. Hence A + Rd = 3Tp + 1.5αgVo; with Vo = 2Γ*Vc/Cc and Vc = (A + Rd)Cc/(Cc − Γ*) this rearranges to A = 3Tp(Cc − Γ*)/(Cc − (1 + 3αg)Γ*) − Rd. Differentiating, dA/dCc = −9αgTpΓ*/(Cc − (1 + 3αg)Γ*)² < 0: raising CO₂ suppresses oxygenation, closing the amino-acid outlet, so more carbon must leave as triose phosphate, which is exactly the step that is saturated. The same logic gives a positive response to O₂ ("reversed sensitivity"). TPU limitation is most common at low temperature (Tp has a steep temperature response and sink demand is low), so in cool climates or seasons a model without Ap, or with Ap = 3Tp − Rd, will overestimate the CO₂ fertilisation of GPP.

4. Derive the optimum temperature of the peaked Arrhenius function. Explain the roles of Ha, Hd and ΔS and summarise what Kattge & Knorr (2007) found.

Write ln k = const + Ha(T − 298.15)/(298.15RT) − ln[1 + exp((TΔS − Hd)/RT)]. Differentiating: d ln k/dT = Ha/(RT²) − [exp(x)/(1 + exp(x))]·(Hd/RT²), where x = (TΔS − Hd)/RT (note dx/dT = Hd/RT²). Setting to zero: exp(x)/(1 + exp(x)) = Ha/Hd, so exp(x) = Ha/(Hd − Ha), i.e. (ToptΔS − Hd)/RTopt = ln[Ha/(Hd − Ha)], giving Topt = Hd/[ΔS − R ln(Ha/(Hd − Ha))]. Ha controls the exponential rise (a Q₁₀ of 2.4 at 65 kJ mol⁻¹); Hd controls how sharply activity collapses above the optimum and is poorly identifiable, so it is fixed at 200 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔS shifts the optimum (lower ΔS, higher Topt, about 5 °C per 0.01 kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹). Kattge & Knorr re-fitted 36 species grown at known temperatures and found that Ha was approximately constant but ΔS of both Vcmax and Jmax decreased linearly with growth temperature (ΔSV = 668.39 − 1.07Tg, ΔSJ = 659.70 − 0.75Tg J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹), so Topt rises about 0.4 °C per °C of growth temperature, and that Jmax/Vcmax at 25 °C fell with growth temperature (2.59 − 0.035Tg). These relations are CLM's representation of thermal acclimation.

5. A colleague fits Vcmax from A–Ci data assuming Cc = Ci. Derive the correct expression when gm is finite and explain, with the Laisk intersection as an example, how the assumption biases parameters.

With A = gm(Ci − Cc), substitute Cc = Ci − A/gm into A = Vcmax(Cc − Γ*)/(Cc + Km) − Rd. Multiplying out gives a quadratic in A: (−1/gm)A² + [(Vcmax − Rd)/gm + Ci + Km]A + [Rd(Ci + Km) − Vcmax(Ci − Γ*)] = 0, solved with the root that tends to the gm → ∞ limit (Ethier & Livingston 2004). The Ci-based curve is thus a non-rectangular hyperbola that bends earlier than the Michaelis–Menten curve in Cc. If one ignores gm, the extra curvature is absorbed into the fit as a smaller Vcmax (apparent Vcmax can be 20–50% low) and an apparently larger Km; because gm has its own temperature response, the apparent activation energy of Vcmax is wrong too. The Laisk intersection illustrates the offset directly: at Cc = Γ* the net rate is −Rd irrespective of light, but the corresponding Ci is Γ* − Rd/gm = Ci*, so the intersection sits below Γ* by a few µbar; using Ci* as Γ* biases everything downstream. The remedy is to measure gm independently (variable J, isotopes) or to fit the Ethier–Livingston form with the Cc-based kinetic constants of Bernacchi 2002, remembering that Vcmax and gm are strongly negatively correlated in such fits.

6. Explain how a land-surface model solves for the leaf operating point when FvCB is coupled to a stomatal model, and define stomatal limitation. Sketch the A–Ci diagram.

The diagram has A on the y-axis and Ci on the x-axis. The demand curve is the FvCB A(Ci): negative at Ci = 0, through Γ, rising steeply (Rubisco-limited) then bending to a plateau (RuBP-limited). The supply line comes from A = gsc(Ca − Ci): it starts at (Ca, 0) and rises to the left with slope −gsc. The operating point is their intersection. Stomatal limitation (Farquhar & Sharkey 1982) is l = (A∞ − A)/A∞ with A∞ the demand-curve value at Ci = Ca: the relative loss attributable to stomata. Because gs in Ball–Berry or Medlyn depends on A, the model has three unknowns (A, Ci, gs) and three equations (FvCB, diffusion, stomatal model). Substitution gives a cubic in A (Baldocchi 1994) that can be solved analytically, but most models iterate: guess Ci; compute A from FvCB (co-limited); compute gs from the stomatal model using A and the leaf-surface humidity and CO₂; recompute Ci = Cs − 1.6A/gsw; repeat until Ci converges (CLM uses a hybrid Newton–Brent method on the residual in Ci). The whole loop sits inside the leaf energy balance because Tleaf sets the kinetics and the vapour deficit, and inside the canopy radiative transfer because Q differs for sunlit and shaded leaves.

7. Which FvCB parameters can be identified from a single A–Ci curve, which cannot, and how would you design a measurement campaign to compare Vcmax between two treatments?

Identifiable: Vcmax (from the initial, Rubisco-limited points, provided Kc, Ko, Γ* are fixed), J (from the plateau), and Rd weakly (from the intercept; strongly correlated with Vcmax). Conditionally identifiable: Tp, only if the curve extends to ≥ 1,500 µbar and the plateau is flatter than Aj predicts or declines; gm, only with many low-Ci points and even then with wide intervals because of the Vcmax–gm trade-off. Not identifiable: Kc, Ko, Γ*, θ, αg (fixed from literature or from separate experiments at 2% O₂ and multiple irradiances). Design: measure at a common leaf temperature and saturating light; use ≥ 12 Ci steps with ≥ 4 below 250 µbar and points to 1,500–2,000 µbar; estimate Rd independently (Laisk or Yin method) and fix it; use a consistent fitting procedure (Sharkey 2007 or Gu 2010 state assignment, or plantecophys) with the same constants for both treatments; treat each leaf (not each point) as the replicate; report Vcmax with profile-likelihood or bootstrap confidence intervals; and compare treatments with a mixed model or t-test on leaf-level estimates, checking that the comparison is unchanged when gm is included or excluded. Report whether values are Ci- or Cc-based.

8. Describe how CLM5 implements the FvCB model and give three criticisms of that implementation with the evidence behind them.

CLM5 computes A for sunlit and shaded leaf fractions each time step. Vcmax,25 comes from leaf N via FLNR, FNR and aR25 (or from the LUNA optimality module); Jmax,25 = (2.59 − 0.035Tg)Vcmax,25; Tp,25 = 0.167Vcmax,25; Rd,25 = 0.015Vcmax,25. Temperature functions are peaked Arrhenius with Hd = 200 and Kattge & Knorr ΔS that acclimate to the 10-day running mean air temperature; Kc, Ko, Γ* follow Bernacchi 2001 (Cc = Ci). J is the non-rectangular hyperbola with θ = 0.7 and 0.85 absorptance; the three rates are co-limited by two quadratics (0.98, 0.95). Stomata follow Medlyn et al. 2011 with PFT-specific g₁, and Ci is solved by hybrid Newton–Brent iteration inside the leaf energy balance. Water stress enters through plant hydraulics acting on gs. Criticisms: (i) Vcmax,25 values by PFT were historically too high for tropical forests and too variable within PFTs to be captured by one number (Kattge 2009; Rogers 2017), and the N-based chain assumes a fixed FLNR; (ii) acclimation is only through ΔS and the J/V ratio, so Ha and Rd acclimation (Kumarathunge 2019, Atkin 2015) and CO₂ acclimation are absent or imposed; (iii) Cc = Ci means the model's Vcmax is apparent, and the fixed TPU ratio with αg = 0 misses low-temperature sink limitation and reversed CO₂ sensitivity; more generally the scheme is steady state and applies tobacco kinetic constants globally.
