Maximum-Likelihood Estimation

One principle that generates least squares, logistic regression, the cost functions of data assimilation and the information criteria used to choose between ecosystem models: pick the parameters under which the data you saw were most probable.

The one-paragraph version

A probability model assigns a probability (or density) to data given parameters, \(p(y \mid \theta)\). Read the other way, as a function of θ for the data you actually observed, it is the likelihood \(L(\theta) = p(y_{obs} \mid \theta)\), and the maximum-likelihood estimate (MLE) is the θ that maximises it. Fisher's idea is simple and remarkably productive: under mild regularity conditions the MLE is consistent, asymptotically normal with variance equal to the inverse Fisher information (the Cramér–Rao bound), and invariant to reparametrisation. Least squares is the MLE under Gaussian errors, weighted least squares under heteroscedastic Gaussian errors, logistic and Poisson regression under their respective distributions, and the "cost function" minimised when calibrating a soil-carbon or photosynthesis model to observations is a negative log-likelihood. The curvature of the log-likelihood at its peak gives standard errors; its drop away from the peak gives likelihood-ratio tests and profile intervals; and the maximised value, penalised for parameters, gives AIC and BIC. Maximising likelihood is minimising Kullback–Leibler divergence from the truth, which is why it overfits when models are flexible and why priors (MAP) and penalties exist.

Examiner's lens

You will be asked to derive an MLE from scratch (normal, Poisson, binomial, exponential are the standards), to explain why least squares is maximum likelihood and what happens when that assumption fails, to get a standard error from a Hessian, to compare two models by a likelihood-ratio test or AIC and say which is appropriate, and to recognise a flat or ridged likelihood (Vcmax–gm, soil-pool equifinality) as a statement about the data, not the optimiser.

Likelihood, score and Fisher information

For independent observations y1, …, yn with density f(yi; θ), the likelihood is the joint density viewed as a function of θ:

$$L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n f(y_i;\theta), \qquad \ell(\theta) = \log L(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log f(y_i;\theta).$$

The likelihood is not a probability distribution over θ: it does not integrate to one in θ, and only ratios L(θ1)/L(θ2) carry meaning (the likelihood principle). Working with the log-likelihood ℓ turns products into sums, is numerically stable, and makes the second derivative meaningful. Three derived objects do most of the work:

  • The score \(U(\theta) = \partial\ell/\partial\theta\). The MLE solves the score equation \(U(\hat\theta) = 0\). Under regularity, \(E_\theta[U(\theta)] = 0\) at the true θ, because \(\int \partial_\theta f \, dy = \partial_\theta \int f\,dy = 0\).
  • The expected Fisher information \(I(\theta) = E_\theta\!\left[U(\theta)^2\right] = -E_\theta\!\left[\partial^2\ell/\partial\theta^2\right]\), the variance of the score. The second equality (the information identity) again follows from differentiating \(\int f\,dy = 1\) twice. For n iid observations I(θ) = n i(θ), with i the per-observation information.
  • The observed information \(J(\hat\theta) = -\partial^2\ell/\partial\theta^2\big|_{\hat\theta}\), the curvature of the log-likelihood at its peak. It is what software actually computes (the negative Hessian) and what gives standard errors in practice; Efron and Hinkley argued it is usually the better choice.

Curvature is the key intuition. A sharply peaked log-likelihood means small changes in θ make the data much less probable: the data are informative and the MLE is precise. A flat log-likelihood means many parameter values explain the data about equally well: no optimiser can fix that. The lab shows the log-likelihood of a normal sample as a function of μ and of (μ, σ), and how it sharpens as n grows: because ℓ is a sum of n terms, its curvature scales with n, and the standard error of the MLE with \(1/\sqrt n\).

Properties of the MLE

Under regularity conditions (the parameter is interior to the parameter space, the support of y does not depend on θ, the log-density is three times differentiable with integrable derivatives, and the model is identifiable, meaning θ1 ≠ θ2 implies different distributions) the MLE has four properties that make it the default estimator:

  1. Consistency: \(\hat\theta_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta_0\). The argument: \(\frac1n\ell(\theta) \to E_{\theta_0}[\log f(y;\theta)]\) by the law of large numbers, and this expectation is maximised at θ0 because \(E_{\theta_0}[\log f(y;\theta)] - E_{\theta_0}[\log f(y;\theta_0)] = -\mathrm{KL}(f_{\theta_0}\,\|\,f_\theta) \le 0\) (Jensen's inequality). Maximising likelihood is minimising Kullback–Leibler divergence from the data-generating distribution.
  2. Asymptotic normality: \(\sqrt n(\hat\theta_n - \theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} N\big(0,\, i(\theta_0)^{-1}\big)\). Sketch: expand the score around θ0, \(0 = U(\hat\theta) \approx U(\theta_0) + U'(\theta_0)(\hat\theta - \theta_0)\), so \(\hat\theta - \theta_0 \approx -U(\theta_0)/U'(\theta_0)\). The numerator is a sum of n iid zero-mean terms with variance n i(θ0) (CLT), the denominator converges to −n i(θ0) (LLN), and Slutsky's theorem gives the result. In practice: \(\hat\theta \approx N(\theta_0, J(\hat\theta)^{-1})\).
  3. Efficiency: the Cramér–Rao bound says any unbiased estimator satisfies \(\operatorname{Var}(\tilde\theta) \ge I(\theta)^{-1}\); the MLE attains it asymptotically, so no regular estimator does better in large samples. (In small samples it may be biased, as σ̂² below shows.)
  4. Invariance: if \(\hat\theta\) is the MLE of θ, then \(g(\hat\theta)\) is the MLE of g(θ) for any function g. The MLE of a turnover time τ = 1/k is 1/k̂; the MLE of the Jmax/Vcmax ratio is the ratio of the MLEs. Standard errors do not transfer so simply: use the delta method, \(\operatorname{Var}(g(\hat\theta)) \approx g'(\hat\theta)^2\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)\), or profile the likelihood in the new parameter.

When regularity fails, so do the asymptotics: the uniform example below has a support that depends on θ, and its MLE converges at rate 1/n rather than 1/√n with a non-normal limit. Parameters on a boundary (a variance component estimated at zero; a mixture weight at zero) give likelihood-ratio statistics that are mixtures of χ² distributions rather than a single χ².

Worked derivations

Every derivation follows the same four steps: write ℓ(θ), differentiate, set to zero, check the second derivative is negative. The Fisher information falls out of the same calculation.

Bernoulli / binomial: p

k successes in n independent trials (k germinated seeds out of n; k days of the year with a flux gap): \(\ell(p) = k\log p + (n-k)\log(1-p) + \text{const}\).

$$U(p) = \frac{k}{p} - \frac{n-k}{1-p} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \hat p = \frac{k}{n},$$
$$I(p) = -E\!\left[-\frac{k}{p^2} - \frac{n-k}{(1-p)^2}\right] = \frac{n}{p(1-p)}, \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\hat p) = \sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}}.$$

Poisson: λ

Counts yi (seedlings per quadrat, nodules per root): \(\ell(\lambda) = \sum_i (y_i\log\lambda - \lambda - \log y_i!)\).

$$U(\lambda) = \frac{\sum y_i}{\lambda} - n = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \hat\lambda = \bar y, \qquad I(\lambda) = E\!\left[\frac{\sum y_i}{\lambda^2}\right] = \frac{n}{\lambda}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\hat\lambda) = \frac{\lambda}{n}.$$

Normal: μ and σ²

$$\ell(\mu,\sigma^2) = -\frac n2\log(2\pi) - \frac n2\log\sigma^2 - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i (y_i-\mu)^2.$$
$$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mu} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_i (y_i-\mu) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \hat\mu = \bar y,$$
$$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\sigma^2} = -\frac{n}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{2\sigma^4}\sum_i (y_i-\mu)^2 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \hat\sigma^2 = \frac1n\sum_i (y_i-\bar y)^2.$$

The MLE of σ² divides by n and is biased: because \(\sum(y_i - \bar y)^2 = \sum(y_i-\mu)^2 - n(\bar y - \mu)^2\) and \(E[n(\bar y-\mu)^2] = \sigma^2\), \(E[\hat\sigma^2] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2\). Using ȳ instead of μ "uses up" one degree of freedom; the unbiased s² divides by n − 1. The MLE nevertheless has smaller mean squared error for normal data, a first glimpse of the bias–variance trade-off. The Fisher information matrix is diagonal, \(I(\mu,\sigma^2) = \operatorname{diag}(n/\sigma^2,\; n/(2\sigma^4))\), so μ̂ and σ̂² are asymptotically independent and \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\mu) = \sigma/\sqrt n\).

Exponential: rate λ (or mean 1/λ)

Waiting times or residence times ti (time to decomposition of a litter cohort, if first-order): \(\ell(\lambda) = n\log\lambda - \lambda\sum t_i\), so \(\hat\lambda = 1/\bar t\), and by invariance the MLE of the mean residence time is \(\bar t\). \(I(\lambda) = n/\lambda^2\).

Uniform on [0, θ]: the non-regular case

\(L(\theta) = \theta^{-n}\) for θ ≥ max(yi) and 0 otherwise. The likelihood is decreasing in θ wherever it is positive, so the MLE is \(\hat\theta = y_{(n)}\), the sample maximum, found at a boundary rather than by setting a derivative to zero. It is biased (\(E[y_{(n)}] = \frac{n}{n+1}\theta\)), its error shrinks like 1/n rather than 1/√n, and the Fisher-information machinery does not apply because the support depends on θ.

Least squares is maximum likelihood

Suppose \(y_i = f(x_i;\theta) + \varepsilon_i\) with \(\varepsilon_i \sim N(0,\sigma^2)\) independent. Then

$$\ell(\theta,\sigma^2) = -\frac n2\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\underbrace{\sum_i \big(y_i - f(x_i;\theta)\big)^2}_{RSS(\theta)}.$$

For any σ², maximising ℓ over θ is minimising RSS(θ): ordinary least squares is the MLE under iid Gaussian errors, whether f is linear (\(f = X\beta\), \(\hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y\)) or nonlinear (FvCB, a Michaelis–Menten uptake curve, a two-pool soil-carbon decay). Maximising over σ² afterwards gives \(\hat\sigma^2 = RSS(\hat\theta)/n\) (biased; the usual estimator divides by n − p), and the maximised log-likelihood becomes

$$\ell_{max} = -\frac n2\Big[\log\!\Big(\frac{2\pi\,RSS}{n}\Big) + 1\Big], \qquad \text{so } -2\ell_{max} = n\log(RSS/n) + \text{const},$$

which is why AIC for regression models is often written \(n\log(RSS/n) + 2k\). Three generalisations follow immediately:

  • Weighted least squares. If \(\varepsilon_i \sim N(0,\sigma_i^2)\) with known (or modelled) variances, \(\ell = -\frac12\sum_i\left[\log(2\pi\sigma_i^2) + (y_i - f_i)^2/\sigma_i^2\right]\), and the MLE minimises \(\sum_i (y_i-f_i)^2/\sigma_i^2\): each residual weighted by its inverse variance. Flux data with error proportional to flux magnitude, or A–Ci points measured with different stability, should be fitted this way; ignoring heteroscedasticity leaves the estimate consistent but inefficient and makes the standard errors wrong.
  • Correlated errors. With \(\varepsilon \sim N(0,\Sigma)\), \(-2\ell = \log|\Sigma| + (y-f)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(y-f) + \text{const}\): the generalised least-squares objective and, with Σ the observation-error covariance R, exactly the observation term of the data-assimilation cost function discussed below.
  • Non-Gaussian errors. Laplace (double-exponential) errors give least absolute deviations as the MLE; Student-t errors give a robust M-estimator that down-weights outliers. Richardson and Hollinger's finding that eddy-covariance random error is closer to Laplace than normal is an argument for L1 cost functions in flux-based model calibration.

Generalised linear models

When the response is a count or a proportion the Gaussian likelihood is the wrong model and the MLE changes accordingly. In a GLM the mean \(\mu_i = E[y_i]\) is linked to a linear predictor by \(g(\mu_i) = x_i^\top\beta\) and the likelihood is from an exponential family. For logistic regression (presence/absence of a species; mortality of a tree), \(y_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\pi_i)\), \(\log\frac{\pi_i}{1-\pi_i} = x_i^\top\beta\), and

$$\ell(\beta) = \sum_i \left[y_i\, x_i^\top\beta - \log\!\big(1 + e^{x_i^\top\beta}\big)\right], \qquad U(\beta) = \sum_i (y_i - \pi_i)\,x_i, \qquad I(\beta) = \sum_i \pi_i(1-\pi_i)\,x_i x_i^\top.$$

For Poisson regression with log link, \(\ell(\beta) = \sum_i [y_i x_i^\top\beta - e^{x_i^\top\beta} - \log y_i!]\), \(U = \sum (y_i - \mu_i)x_i\) and \(I = \sum \mu_i x_i x_i^\top\). In both cases the score has the form "residual times covariate" and there is no closed form, so the MLE is found by Fisher scoring, which for GLMs is iteratively reweighted least squares. The logistic-regression page develops this in full.

Finding the maximum numerically

Outside the textbook cases the score equation has no closed-form solution and the MLE is found iteratively. The methods are those of the gradient-descent and Newton–Raphson pages, applied to −ℓ:

  • Newton–Raphson: \(\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - H(\theta_t)^{-1}U(\theta_t)\), with H the Hessian of ℓ. Quadratic convergence near the optimum; needs second derivatives; can diverge from a poor start or on a non-concave surface.
  • Fisher scoring: replace H by its expectation −I(θ). Always negative definite, so the step is always uphill; for GLMs it reduces to iteratively reweighted least squares.
  • Gauss–Newton for least squares: with residuals r = y − f(θ) and Jacobian J = ∂f/∂θ, \(\nabla RSS = -2J^\top r\) and \(H \approx 2J^\top J\) (dropping the term with second derivatives of f, which vanishes at the optimum if the model fits), giving \(\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t + (J^\top J)^{-1}J^\top r\). The Levenberg–Marquardt modification adds λI to JᵀJ to interpolate between Gauss–Newton and gradient descent; it is the algorithm inside nls, scipy.optimize.curve_fit and most A–Ci fitting tools.
  • Gradient and quasi-Newton methods (BFGS, L-BFGS) when the Hessian is too expensive; derivative-free methods (Nelder–Mead) when ℓ is a black-box ecosystem model; stochastic search or MCMC when the surface is multimodal. Always try several starting values: a local maximum of a soil-carbon-model likelihood is easy to mistake for the MLE.
  • EM algorithm (Dempster, Laird & Rubin [9]) for latent variables: if the complete-data likelihood with unobserved z would be easy, alternate an E-step (expected complete log-likelihood given current θ) and an M-step (maximise it). Each iteration increases ℓ. Gaussian mixture models (their page uses it to cluster, for example, flux-tower regimes) and missing-data problems are the standard applications.

Inference from the likelihood

Standard errors and Wald tests

From asymptotic normality, \(\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\theta) = J(\hat\theta)^{-1}\), the inverse of the negative Hessian at the optimum, and SEk is the square root of its k-th diagonal element. The Wald statistic \((\hat\theta_k - \theta_{k,0})/\mathrm{SE}_k\) is approximately N(0, 1), the Wald interval is \(\hat\theta_k \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SE}_k\), and for a vector hypothesis \((\hat\theta - \theta_0)^\top J(\hat\theta)(\hat\theta-\theta_0) \sim \chi^2_p\). Wald inference is cheap (one fit) but trusts the quadratic approximation at the peak and is not invariant to reparametrisation: a Wald interval for k and for log k give different answers after back-transformation.

Likelihood-ratio tests

For nested models, where the reduced model fixes q of the p parameters, Wilks' theorem [8] states that under the reduced model

$$\Lambda = 2\big[\ell(\hat\theta_{full}) - \ell(\hat\theta_{reduced})\big] \;\xrightarrow{d}\; \chi^2_q.$$

The sketch: expand \(\ell(\theta_0)\) around \(\hat\theta\); the first-order term vanishes because \(U(\hat\theta) = 0\), leaving \(2[\ell(\hat\theta) - \ell(\theta_0)] \approx (\hat\theta - \theta_0)^\top J (\hat\theta-\theta_0)\), a quadratic form in an asymptotically normal vector with covariance J⁻¹, hence χ². The LRT is invariant to reparametrisation and more accurate than Wald in small samples, at the cost of two fits. "Does the A–Ci curve need a TPU limitation?" (q = 1, threshold 3.84), "do the two treatments share a Vcmax?" (q = 1), "is a two-pool soil model better than one pool?" (q = 2, threshold 5.99 — but note the second pool's parameters are not identified under the one-pool model, which violates the regularity conditions and makes the χ² reference approximate) are all LRTs. The profile-likelihood interval inverts the LRT: it is the set of θk values with \(2[\ell(\hat\theta) - \ell_p(\theta_k)] \le 3.84\), i.e. where the profile log-likelihood is within 1.92 of its maximum, as in the binomial lab. The score test (Rao) completes the trio, using only the reduced-model fit; Wald, LR and score are asymptotically equivalent.

Model comparison: AIC, BIC and Kullback–Leibler divergence

Non-nested models, or many candidate models, call for an information criterion rather than a test. The target quantity in Akaike's derivation [6] is the expected Kullback–Leibler divergence from the true distribution g to the fitted model fθ̂,

$$\mathrm{KL}(g\,\|\,f_{\hat\theta}) = \int g(y)\log g(y)\,dy - \int g(y)\log f(y;\hat\theta)\,dy = \text{const} - E_g\!\left[\log f(y_{new};\hat\theta)\right],$$

so ranking models by KL is ranking them by expected log-likelihood on new data. The maximised log-likelihood \(\ell(\hat\theta)\) estimates this but is optimistic, because the same data chose θ̂. Akaike showed that, to second order, the optimism equals the number of parameters k, giving

$$\mathrm{AIC} = -2\ell(\hat\theta) + 2k, \qquad \mathrm{AIC}_c = \mathrm{AIC} + \frac{2k(k+1)}{n-k-1}, \qquad \mathrm{BIC} = -2\ell(\hat\theta) + k\log n.$$

The derivation sketch: with \(\hat\theta\) the MLE and θ* the KL-closest parameter, two Taylor expansions give \(E[\ell(\hat\theta)] - E[\ell(\theta^*)] \approx k/2\) (the fitted model gains about k/2 from fitting noise) and \(E[\ell(\theta^*)] - E[\ell_{new}(\hat\theta)] \approx k/2\) (it loses about k/2 on new data), each term being the expectation of a χ²k/2 quadratic form; together the bias is k, doubled for the −2ℓ scale. AICc (Hurvich & Tsai) corrects the small-sample bias and should be used when n/k < 40 [5]. BIC comes from a different question: Schwarz [7] approximated the marginal likelihood \(\int L(\theta)\pi(\theta)d\theta\) by Laplace's method, obtaining \(\log p(y\mid M) \approx \ell(\hat\theta) - \frac k2\log n\), so differences in BIC approximate twice the log Bayes factor. The practical contrast: AIC seeks the model with the best predictions (and keeps adding parameters as n grows, when the truth is complex), BIC seeks the true model among the candidates (consistent for model selection if the truth is in the set) and penalises harder once n > 8. Report differences from the best model (ΔAIC < 2 is indistinguishable, > 10 is decisive) and Akaike weights \(w_m \propto e^{-\Delta_m/2}\), and remember that the criterion compares models fitted to the same data with the same likelihood (a model fitted to log-transformed data cannot be compared by AIC with one fitted to raw data without the Jacobian of the transformation).

Overfitting and penalties

Because ℓ(θ̂) can only rise as parameters are added, maximising likelihood over a family of increasingly flexible models always chooses the most flexible one: the deviance curve in the lab is monotone. Information criteria penalise after the fact; penalised likelihood changes the objective, maximising \(\ell(\theta) - \lambda\,\mathrm{pen}(\theta)\). Ridge (pen = ‖θ‖²), lasso (‖θ‖₁) and smoothness penalties on splines are the common cases, and the next paragraph shows that each is a prior in disguise. The bias–variance page quantifies what the penalty buys.

MLE, MAP and the Bayesian posterior; identifiability

Bayes' theorem gives the posterior \(p(\theta\mid y) \propto L(\theta)\,\pi(\theta)\). The maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate maximises the posterior, i.e. \(\ell(\theta) + \log\pi(\theta)\): the log-prior is a penalty. With a Gaussian prior \(\theta \sim N(0, \tau^2 I)\) and Gaussian errors, \(\log\pi(\theta) = -\|\theta\|^2/2\tau^2 + \text{const}\), so MAP with a Gaussian prior is ridge regression with λ = σ²/τ²; a Laplace prior gives the lasso. The MLE is MAP with a flat prior. The full Bayesian answer does not maximise at all: it reports the whole posterior, and summarises it by its mean (which minimises expected squared error) and credible intervals. For large n the posterior concentrates around the MLE with covariance J⁻¹ (the Bernstein–von Mises theorem), so the three approaches agree; they differ when data are few, parameters many, or prior knowledge is strong, which describes almost every ecosystem-model calibration.

Identifiability is the property that different parameter values produce different likelihoods. It fails structurally when parameters enter only through a product or ratio (the data cannot separate them at all) and practically when the data are too sparse or noisy to separate them well. In FvCB fitting, Vcmax and mesophyll conductance gm trade off: a lower gm with a higher Vcmax produces almost the same A–Ci curve, so the likelihood has a long curved ridge, Wald SEs are huge or nonsensical, and the fitted pair depends on the starting values. In multi-pool soil-carbon models, pool sizes and turnover rates are confounded when only total respiration is observed; many (Cfast, kfast, Cslow, kslow) combinations give the same CO₂ efflux curve. Beven calls this equifinality. The likelihood tells you about it (flat directions in the Hessian, eigenvalues near zero, profile likelihoods that never drop by 1.92); the remedies are more informative data (isotopes, pool fractionation, A–Ci curves at several O₂ levels), fixing or constraining parameters from independent measurements, reparametrising to identifiable combinations, or an informative prior that says which part of the ridge is physically plausible.

Likelihoods for time series and state-space models

When the data are a time series generated by a dynamic model with process noise, observations are not independent and the likelihood must integrate over the unobserved states. For a linear Gaussian state-space model \(x_t = Ax_{t-1} + w_t\), \(y_t = Hx_t + v_t\), the Kalman filter does this recursively: at each step it predicts \(y_t\) with mean \(H\hat x_{t|t-1}\) and innovation covariance \(S_t\), and the log-likelihood is the sum of the innovation log-densities, \(\ell = -\frac12\sum_t\left[\log|2\pi S_t| + e_t^\top S_t^{-1}e_t\right]\), the prediction-error decomposition. Parameters (turnover rates, process-noise variances) are then estimated by maximising this ℓ. Nonlinear models use the extended or ensemble Kalman filter or a particle filter to approximate the same quantity.

Likelihoods in ecosystem data assimilation

Model–data fusion for ecosystem models writes a cost function

$$J(\theta) = \tfrac12\,(y - M(\theta))^\top R^{-1}(y - M(\theta)) + \tfrac12\,(\theta - \theta_b)^\top B^{-1}(\theta - \theta_b),$$

where M(θ) is the model prediction, R the observation-error covariance and B the prior ("background") covariance. The first term is \(-\log L\) for Gaussian observation errors, the second is \(-\log\pi(\theta)\), and minimising J is MAP estimation; the inverse Hessian of J at the minimum is the posterior covariance. Richardson et al. [12] fitted a carbon-cycle model to multiple data streams at Howland Forest and showed that the assumed error structure (and the relative weighting of streams through R) changes the answer; Keenan et al. [13] showed that which data constrain which processes can be read from the posterior covariance, and that adding constraints is more valuable than adding parameters. The Bayesian calibrations of DALEC, BioCro, DayCent or MIMICS by MCMC replace minimisation with sampling, but the "likelihood" they evaluate is the same object, and its assumptions (independent, Gaussian, correctly weighted errors; no model structural error) are the main weakness of the whole enterprise: with autocorrelated flux errors and a biased model, the posterior is overconfident and centred on parameters that compensate for structural error.

Connections to ecosystem modelling

  • Every parameter in a process model that came from data came from a likelihood. Vcmax, Jmax and their temperature responses (nonlinear least squares on A–Ci and A–T curves), the Medlyn g1 (least squares or robust regression on gs vs A/(Ca√D)), Q10 and Ea of respiration, litter decay constants from litterbag mass loss (exponential MLE), soil-pool turnover from incubation CO₂ (multi-exponential, often poorly identified).
  • Model structure choice. Whether to include TPU limitation, a gm term, a second soil pool, or microbial dynamics is an LRT or AIC question when the models are fitted to the same data by likelihood; it is a question about predictive error (cross-validation) when the goal is forecasting. CLM, JULES and ORCHIDEE do not fit their parameters by likelihood globally, but their parameter databases (TRY, Walker et al. Vcmax–N relations) do.
  • Calibration and uncertainty. The cost functions of data assimilation (4D-Var, EnKF, MCMC calibration of DALEC/CARDAMOM, Bayesian calibration in PEcAn) are likelihoods times priors; the posterior covariance, i.e. the inverse Hessian, propagates to prediction uncertainty. Choosing R (observation errors, including gap-filling and partitioning errors) is choosing the likelihood and is as important as choosing the model.
  • Equifinality as a likelihood property. Flat ridges in the likelihood of multi-pool soil models and of FvCB with gm explain why different studies report wildly different pool turnover times and why model intercomparisons disagree on soil-carbon response: the data never constrained them.
  • Remote sensing retrievals. Inverting a radiative-transfer model (PROSAIL) for LAI and chlorophyll is nonlinear MLE or MAP with a prior; the same ill-posedness (LAI–leaf-angle trade-off) appears.
  • Machine-learning bridges. Cross-entropy loss in neural-network classifiers is a Bernoulli/multinomial negative log-likelihood; mean squared error is the Gaussian one; weight decay is a Gaussian prior. The GLM, logistic-regression and GMM pages are all likelihood maximisation under different distributional assumptions.

Exam-style questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer.

1. Define the likelihood function and explain how it differs from a probability distribution over the parameters. State the likelihood principle in one sentence.

Given a probability model p(y | θ) and observed data yobs, the likelihood is L(θ) = p(yobs | θ) regarded as a function of θ with the data fixed. It is not a density in θ: it does not integrate to one over θ, its absolute value is meaningless (multiplying by any constant not depending on θ changes nothing), and only ratios or differences of log-likelihood between parameter values carry information. A probability distribution over θ (a prior or a posterior) requires Bayes' theorem and a prior; the posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior. The likelihood principle states that all the evidence in the data about θ is contained in the likelihood function, so two experiments yielding proportional likelihoods should lead to the same inferences about θ; frequentist p-values violate it (they depend on unobserved outcomes and stopping rules), Bayesian and pure-likelihood inference obey it.

2. Derive the MLEs of μ and σ² for a normal sample, show that σ̂² is biased, and give the Fisher information matrix. Why does the bias not matter asymptotically?

ℓ(μ, σ²) = −(n/2) log(2πσ²) − Σ(yi − μ)²/(2σ²). ∂ℓ/∂μ = Σ(yi − μ)/σ² = 0 gives μ̂ = ȳ. ∂ℓ/∂σ² = −n/(2σ²) + Σ(yi − μ)²/(2σ⁴) = 0 gives σ̂² = Σ(yi − ȳ)²/n after substituting μ̂. Second derivatives are negative at the solution, so it is a maximum. Bias: Σ(yi − ȳ)² = Σ(yi − μ)² − n(ȳ − μ)²; taking expectations, E[Σ(yi − μ)²] = nσ² and E[n(ȳ − μ)²] = n·σ²/n = σ², so E[σ̂²] = (n − 1)σ²/n: the MLE underestimates σ² by the factor (n − 1)/n because the deviations are measured from ȳ, which is closer to the data than μ is. Fisher information: −E[∂²ℓ/∂μ²] = n/σ²; −E[∂²ℓ/∂(σ²)²] = −E[n/(2σ⁴) − Σ(yi − μ)²/σ⁶] = −n/(2σ⁴) + n/σ⁴ = n/(2σ⁴); the cross term −E[Σ(yi − μ)/σ⁴] = 0. So I = diag(n/σ², n/(2σ⁴)), the estimates are asymptotically independent, and SE(μ̂) = σ/√n, SE(σ̂²) = σ²√(2/n). The bias is O(1/n) while the standard error is O(1/√n), so bias/SE → 0: asymptotically the MLE is unbiased, normal and efficient, and in fact its MSE is smaller than that of s² for normal data because the extra variance of s² outweighs the bias of σ̂².

3. Show that ordinary least squares is the maximum-likelihood estimator under independent Gaussian errors. What changes if the errors are heteroscedastic, autocorrelated, or Laplace-distributed?

With yi = f(xi; θ) + εi, εi ~ N(0, σ²) independent, ℓ(θ, σ²) = −(n/2) log(2πσ²) − RSS(θ)/(2σ²) where RSS(θ) = Σ[yi − f(xi; θ)]². For any fixed σ² the only θ-dependence is through −RSS/(2σ²), so maximising ℓ in θ is minimising RSS: the OLS (or nonlinear least squares) estimator is the MLE. Then σ̂² = RSS(θ̂)/n. Heteroscedastic errors εi ~ N(0, σi²): ℓ = −½Σ[log(2πσi²) + (yi − fi)²/σi²], so the MLE is weighted least squares with weights 1/σi²; OLS remains consistent but is inefficient and its standard errors are wrong. Autocorrelated errors ε ~ N(0, Σ): −2ℓ = log|Σ| + (y − f)ᵀΣ⁻¹(y − f) + const, generalised least squares; OLS point estimates are still unbiased but standard errors are typically far too small for positively autocorrelated data (flux time series), and if Σ has unknown parameters they are estimated jointly. Laplace errors with density (1/2b) exp(−|ε|/b): ℓ = −n log 2b − Σ|yi − fi|/b, so the MLE minimises the sum of absolute deviations (L1 regression), which is robust to outliers; Richardson and Hollinger found eddy-covariance random error to be closer to Laplace than Gaussian, which argues for L1 cost functions in flux-based calibration.

4. You fit the FvCB model to an A–Ci curve and obtain the Hessian of the negative log-likelihood at the optimum. Explain how to obtain standard errors and a 95% interval for Vcmax, what assumptions this relies on, and what you would do if the Hessian were nearly singular.

By asymptotic normality, Var(θ̂) ≈ J(θ̂)⁻¹ where J is the observed information, the negative Hessian of ℓ (equivalently the Hessian of −ℓ). For least squares, J ≈ JᵀJ/σ̂² with J the Jacobian of predictions, the Gauss–Newton approximation. Invert the matrix, take the diagonal element for Vcmax, and SE = its square root; the Wald interval is V̂cmax ± tn−p,0.975 SE (or ± 1.96 SE for large n). Assumptions: independent, homoscedastic, approximately Gaussian residuals (or correctly specified weights); the log-likelihood approximately quadratic over the ±2 SE range; n large enough relative to p; and the fitted optimum being the global one. A nearly singular Hessian (a tiny eigenvalue, correlation near ±1 between Vcmax and gm or Tp) means the data do not identify the parameters separately: the likelihood is flat along some direction, the inverse blows up and the Wald SEs are meaningless. Responses: (i) profile the likelihood for Vcmax and report the interval where it drops by 1.92, which will be wide or one-sided and is at least honest; (ii) fix the poorly identified parameter (gm from an independent measurement, or assume infinite gm and report Vcmax as an apparent Ci-based value); (iii) collect data in the region that discriminates (more points at low Ci, curves at 2% O₂, chlorophyll fluorescence to constrain J); (iv) reparametrise or impose a prior and report a posterior. One should also check that the residuals show no structure, since a misspecified transition between limitations biases Vcmax regardless of the SE.

5. State Wilks' theorem and sketch why it holds. Use it to decide whether a two-pool soil-carbon model is justified over a one-pool model given ℓ1 = −142.3 and ℓ2 = −137.1. What subtlety arises in this particular comparison?

Wilks: if the reduced model (with q parameters fixed) is true and regularity conditions hold, Λ = 2[ℓ(θ̂full) − ℓ(θ̂reduced)] converges in distribution to χ²q. Sketch: expand ℓ around the full-model MLE, ℓ(θ0) ≈ ℓ(θ̂) + U(θ̂)ᵀ(θ0 − θ̂) − ½(θ0 − θ̂)ᵀJ(θ0 − θ̂); the score at the MLE is zero, so 2[ℓ(θ̂) − ℓ(θ0)] ≈ (θ̂ − θ0)ᵀJ(θ̂ − θ0). Since √n(θ̂ − θ0) is asymptotically N(0, i⁻¹) and J/n → i, this is a quadratic form of a normal vector with its own inverse covariance, which is χ² with as many degrees of freedom as constrained dimensions. For the soil example Λ = 2(−137.1 + 142.3) = 10.4 with q = 2 (a second pool adds a pool fraction and a rate), threshold χ²2,0.95 = 5.99 and p ≈ 0.006, so the two-pool model is supported. The subtlety: the models are nested only on the boundary of the parameter space (one pool is recovered when the second pool's fraction is zero, and at that point its rate constant is unidentified), which violates Wilks' regularity conditions. The true null distribution is not χ²2 but a mixture of χ² distributions, and the standard test is conservative or anticonservative depending on the parametrisation; a parametric bootstrap of Λ under the one-pool model, or a comparison by AICc, is the safer route. One should also check whether the two-pool parameters are themselves identifiable from the incubation data (profile likelihoods), since a better fit along an equifinal ridge still predicts nothing reliably.

6. Explain what AIC estimates, sketch Akaike's derivation, and contrast it with BIC. A modeller compares four soil-respiration models and reports AIC = 410.2, 408.9, 408.5, 421.0. What should she conclude?

AIC estimates the expected relative Kullback–Leibler divergence between the data-generating distribution and the fitted model, equivalently the expected log-likelihood the fitted model would achieve on new data from the same process, up to a constant common to all models. The maximised log-likelihood ℓ(θ̂) overestimates this quantity because θ̂ was chosen to fit the sample. Akaike's argument: let θ* minimise KL (the "best" parameter in the model). Two second-order Taylor expansions, one of ℓ(θ̂) around θ*, the other of the expected new-data log-likelihood around θ*, show that E[ℓ(θ̂)] exceeds E[ℓ(θ*)] by about k/2 and that E[ℓnew(θ̂)] falls short of E[ℓ(θ*)] by about k/2, each being the expectation of a quadratic form of a χ²k type with the covariance cancelling the information matrix; the total optimism is k, so −2ℓ(θ̂) + 2k is approximately unbiased for −2 × expected new-data log-likelihood. AICc adds 2k(k+1)/(n−k−1) for small n. BIC = −2ℓ + k log n arises instead from a Laplace approximation to the marginal likelihood p(y | M) under a Bayesian model-selection framing; it is consistent for picking the true model when it is in the candidate set and penalises more heavily than AIC for n > 8, whereas AIC targets predictive accuracy and may keep adding parameters as n grows. For the four models: ΔAIC = 1.7, 0.4, 0, 12.5. Models 2 and 3 are essentially indistinguishable (Δ < 2), model 1 has slightly less support, model 4 can be dismissed (Δ > 10). Akaike weights are proportional to exp(−Δ/2): 0.43, 0.82, 1, 0.002, normalised to 0.19, 0.36, 0.44, 0.00. She should not declare model 3 "the best" but report the weights, consider model averaging of predictions, and check that all four were fitted to the same data with the same likelihood (same transformation of the response) and that n/k is large enough that AICc is unnecessary.

7. Show that MAP estimation with a Gaussian prior on regression coefficients is ridge regression, and interpret the ridge parameter. When would you prefer the full posterior to the MAP estimate?

With y = Xβ + ε, ε ~ N(0, σ²I) and prior β ~ N(0, τ²I), the log-posterior is log p(β | y) = ℓ(β) + log π(β) + const = −‖y − Xβ‖²/(2σ²) − ‖β‖²/(2τ²) + const. Multiplying by −2σ² and dropping constants, the MAP estimate minimises ‖y − Xβ‖² + (σ²/τ²)‖β‖², the ridge objective with λ = σ²/τ², whose solution is β̂ = (XᵀX + λI)⁻¹Xᵀy. λ is the ratio of noise variance to prior variance: a vague prior (large τ) gives λ → 0 and OLS; a strong belief that coefficients are small gives large λ and strong shrinkage toward zero. A Laplace prior gives the lasso penalty λ‖β‖₁ and sparsity; a prior centred on θb with covariance B gives the background term of a data-assimilation cost function. The MAP estimate is a point and ignores the shape of the posterior: prefer the full posterior (by MCMC, or its Laplace approximation) when uncertainty must be propagated into predictions, when the posterior is skewed or multimodal (equifinal parameters in a soil-carbon model, Vcmax–gm ridges), when there are hierarchical levels (site- or species-level parameters with partial pooling, where the MAP of variance components can collapse to zero), or when decisions depend on tail probabilities rather than the most probable parameter. The posterior mean minimises expected squared error and can differ substantially from the mode in such cases.

8. Describe the cost function of a typical ecosystem model–data fusion study as a likelihood and prior. What assumptions does it make, which are usually violated with flux-tower data, and what are the consequences?

The standard cost function is J(θ) = ½(y − M(θ))ᵀR⁻¹(y − M(θ)) + ½(θ − θb)ᵀB⁻¹(θ − θb). The first term is −log L(θ) for observation errors that are Gaussian with covariance R (often diagonal, with variances set per data stream); the second is −log π(θ) for a Gaussian prior with mean θb and covariance B. Minimising J is MAP estimation; the inverse Hessian of J at the minimum approximates the posterior covariance, from which prediction uncertainty is propagated. Assumptions: (i) observation errors are independent across time and streams (R diagonal), (ii) Gaussian, (iii) with known variances, (iv) the model is structurally correct so that model-minus-data differences are observation error only, (v) the prior is Gaussian and correctly centred, and (vi) the optimum found is global. With flux-tower data, (i) fails because random flux errors are autocorrelated at sub-daily to seasonal scales and gap-filled values are model output; (ii) fails because the random error is Laplace-like and heteroscedastic, scaling with flux magnitude; (iii) is approximate; and (iv) fails for every ecosystem model. Consequences: the effective number of observations is overstated, so the posterior is far too narrow (overconfidence); the weighting between data streams (NEE vs soil respiration vs biomass) is arbitrary and changes the parameter estimates; parameters absorb structural error and take compensating, physically implausible values that transfer poorly to other sites or future climate; and streams with many observations (half-hourly NEE) dominate streams with few (annual biomass increment) unless R is scaled. Remedies in the literature: thicken R with temporal error correlation or use daily aggregates, use Laplace or heavy-tailed likelihoods, inflate variances per stream to balance them, include a model-discrepancy term, and validate on held-out years or sites rather than trusting the posterior spread (Richardson et al. 2010; Keenan et al. 2012).
