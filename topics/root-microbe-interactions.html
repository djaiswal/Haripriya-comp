Topic 14 of the SPAC section

Root–Microbe Interactions

Plants spend a fifth or more of their fixed carbon below ground on microbes that return nitrogen and phosphorus: mycorrhizal fungi, nitrogen-fixing bacteria and the rhizosphere community. This page treats those exchanges as an economy with costs, prices and markets, which is exactly how the newest land models represent them.

The one-paragraph version

Roots leak. Between 5 and 20% of the carbon a plant fixes is released into the thin cylinder of soil around each root, the rhizosphere, as exudates, mucilage and sloughed cells, and this carbon feeds a microbial community one to two orders of magnitude denser than the bulk soil. Two symbioses dominate the return on that investment. Mycorrhizal fungi colonise the roots of roughly 90% of plant species and trade phosphorus (arbuscular mycorrhiza) or nitrogen, including organic nitrogen (ectomycorrhiza), for 10–20% of the plant's photosynthate. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria, mostly rhizobia in legume nodules, reduce N2 to NH3 at a cost of 6–12 g C per g N, which the plant pays only when soil nitrogen is scarce. Free-living microbes add plant-growth-promoting services such as phosphate solubilisation, siderophore production and hormone signalling, and the carbon that roots release also primes the decomposition of old soil organic matter. Because every one of these processes is a trade of carbon for nutrients, they can be written as costs (g C per g nutrient) and compared, which is the logic of the Fixation and Uptake of Nitrogen (FUN) model in CLM5 and of the mycorrhizal pathways now being added to soil-carbon models.

Examiner's lens

Examiners want you to move from mechanism (what an arbuscule or a nodule does) to ecosystem consequence (why ectomycorrhizal forests store more soil carbon per unit N, why CO2 fertilisation depends on mycorrhizal type, why nitrogen fixers are rare in N-rich soils) and then to model representation (how a cost-based scheme decides between uptake, fixation and mycorrhizae). Keep three numbers at hand: 5–20% of fixed C to rhizodeposition, 10–20% to mycorrhizal fungi, 6–12 g C per g N fixed.

The rhizosphere: where plants feed the soil

Lorenz Hiltner coined the term rhizosphere in 1904 to describe the soil under the influence of roots, where he observed bacterial densities far above the bulk soil. The zone is only a few millimetres wide, yet in a grassland or crop root system with 10–50 km of root per m2 it occupies a substantial share of the topsoil volume. Its chemistry differs from bulk soil in almost every respect: pH can be shifted by 1–2 units by proton or bicarbonate release during cation–anion balance, redox potential is lowered by root and microbial respiration, and the concentrations of nutrients, organic acids and signalling molecules follow steep radial gradients [17].

Rhizodeposition and its components

Rhizodeposition is the sum of all carbon that leaves living roots: low-molecular-weight exudates (sugars, amino acids, organic acids such as citrate, malate and oxalate), high-molecular-weight mucilage secreted by the root cap, border cells released from the cap, sloughed epidermal and cortical cells, root-hair turnover, and gaseous losses as CO2 from root respiration. Reviews of isotope labelling studies put total rhizodeposition at 5–21% of photosynthetically fixed carbon, or roughly 20–40% of the carbon allocated below ground, with the mean around 11% of net fixed C. Exudation is highest just behind the root tip, where young cells have leaky membranes and the cap is active, and is strongly increased by nutrient deficiency, mechanical impedance and, for carboxylates, by P deficiency.

The exuded carbon is consumed within hours. Microbial biomass and activity in the rhizosphere are typically 2–20 times those in bulk soil, and the community is strongly filtered: Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria and Bacteroidetes are enriched relative to the bulk soil, and each plant species, and even genotype, selects a partly specific rhizosphere microbiome from the soil pool [18]. This is the basis of the "two-step selection" picture of rhizosphere assembly: soil type sets the available pool, root exudate chemistry and immune signalling filter it.

Rhizosphere priming

Fresh, labile carbon in the rhizosphere changes the decomposition rate of the old soil organic matter around it. This rhizosphere priming effect (RPE) is measured by growing plants in a 13C-labelled or C3/C4-contrasted system so that root-derived CO2 can be separated from SOM-derived CO2. Kuzyakov's synthesis [15] distinguishes real priming (accelerated SOM mineralisation through co-metabolism, microbial activation and enzyme production) from apparent priming (faster turnover of microbial biomass itself). Cheng et al. [14] compiled RPE values from −50% to +380% of the unplanted control, with a typical stimulation of 20–60%, strongest when microbes are N-limited and roots supply the energy to mine N from SOM. Priming has two consequences for carbon budgets: it couples plant productivity to soil-carbon loss, and it means that extra belowground allocation under elevated CO2 does not translate one-to-one into soil carbon gain.

Plant-growth-promoting rhizobacteria

Many rhizosphere bacteria (Pseudomonas, Bacillus, Azospirillum, Enterobacter) improve plant growth without forming organs. The documented mechanisms are: phosphate solubilisation by organic acid secretion (gluconic, citric) and phosphatase release; siderophore production, which chelates Fe3+ and both supplies iron to the plant and starves pathogens of it; ACC deaminase, which cleaves the ethylene precursor 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate and so lowers stress ethylene that would otherwise inhibit root growth; auxin (IAA) production, which stimulates root branching and root-hair formation and therefore the absorbing surface; and induced systemic resistance. Field responses are inconsistent because inoculants must compete with the resident community, which is why PGPR appear in agronomy rather than in ecosystem models.

Mycorrhizal symbiosis

A mycorrhiza is a structure formed by a fungus and a root in which the fungus delivers soil-derived nutrients to the plant in exchange for carbon. The association is ancient (fossil arbuscules in 400-million-year-old Rhynie chert) and nearly universal. Brundrett & Tedersoo [6] estimate that about 72% of vascular plant species form arbuscular mycorrhizas (AM), about 2% ectomycorrhizas (ECM), 1.5% ericoid mycorrhizas, 10% orchid mycorrhizas, and roughly 8% are non-mycorrhizal (with a further ~7% facultative). Species counts understate ECM importance: the 2% of ECM species include the pines, spruces, firs, oaks, beeches, birches, eucalypts and dipterocarps that dominate most boreal and temperate forests and large tracts of the tropics.

The trade and the market

Mycorrhizal fungi receive 10–20% of the host's photosynthate (up to 30% in some ECM seedlings) as sugars and, in AM, as lipids transferred across the periarbuscular membrane; the fungus cannot make its own fatty acids. In return the plant gains access to the nutrients that hyphae collect from a soil volume the root cannot reach. Because hyphae are 2–10 µm in diameter against 200–1000 µm for fine roots, a gram of carbon invested in hyphae yields 100-fold more length and surface than the same carbon in roots; hyphal length densities of 1–100 m per cm3 soil are typical.

Kiers et al. [5] showed that the exchange is enforced by both partners. Using 13C and 33P labelling of Medicago roots and Rhizophagus species that differ in cooperativeness, they found that plants direct more carbon to root compartments colonised by fungi that deliver more phosphorus, and fungi allocate more phosphorus to roots that provide more carbon. The symbiosis therefore behaves as a biological market with reciprocal rewards, which stabilises cooperation against cheaters without requiring either partner to recognise the other's identity. The same logic appears later on this page as the resistors-in-parallel allocation in FUN: carbon flows preferentially down the cheapest route to nutrients.

Hyphae of a single fungal individual often connect several plants into a common mycorrhizal network. Isotope studies show that carbon and nutrients can move between plants through such networks, and that seedlings establishing in the shade of ECM trees benefit from connection. How much of this transfer is ecologically important, as opposed to measurable, is contested; the fungus, not the "mother tree", controls allocation, and recent reviews caution that popular accounts have run ahead of the evidence.

Phosphorus: why hyphae matter so much

Phosphate diffuses extremely slowly in soil because most of it is sorbed to Fe/Al oxides and clay edges. The effective diffusion coefficient is the solution diffusivity scaled by water content, tortuosity and the buffer power b (the slope of sorbed P versus solution P):

$$D_e = \frac{D_l\,\theta\,f}{b}, \qquad b = \frac{\mathrm{d}C_{\text{total}}}{\mathrm{d}C_l} \approx 10^2\text{–}10^3,$$

so that De is of order 10−13–10−12 m2 s−1, and the depletion zone that develops around an absorbing root after time t, roughly \( \sqrt{D_e t} \), is only about 1 mm after a week. Roots a few millimetres apart exploit nearly all the soil P they can reach within days, while P further away is unavailable. Hyphae extend tens of millimetres beyond the depletion zone, place absorbing surface in pores too small for roots, and also act chemically: they release phosphatases that hydrolyse organic P and, in ECM, organic acids (oxalate) that dissolve Ca-, Fe- and Al-phosphates. Biome gradients: Read's hypothesis and the mycorrhizal nutrient economy

Read [2] observed that mycorrhizal types sort along latitudinal and successional gradients of soil organic matter and nutrient limitation: AM in grasslands, savannas and lowland tropical forests where mineral P limits and litter decomposes fast; ECM in temperate and boreal forests where N is locked in slowly decomposing litter; ericoid mycorrhizas in heathlands and tundra where organic matter is most recalcitrant and acidic. The fungal partner's enzymatic repertoire increases along this gradient: AM fungi have essentially no saprotrophic ability, ECM fungi possess proteases and, in some lineages, peroxidases, and ericoid fungi can degrade phenolic complexes.

Phillips, Brzostek & Midgley [3] turned this into a testable framework for temperate forests, the mycorrhizal-associated nutrient economy (MANE). AM-dominated stands run an inorganic economy: high-quality litter (low C:N, low lignin), fast decomposition, high net N mineralisation and nitrification, nitrate leaching, nutrient cycling through soil microbes rather than plant-controlled fungi. ECM-dominated stands run an organic economy: recalcitrant litter, slow decomposition, high soil C:N, low nitrification, N acquired largely as amino acids and peptides through the fungal partner, and more soil carbon in organic horizons. Because mycorrhizal type is a plant trait that maps onto measurable soil properties, MANE allows forest inventory data to predict soil biogeochemistry, and it motivated the AM/ECM split now used in several land models.

Mycorrhizae and soil carbon

Three lines of evidence connect mycorrhizal type to soil carbon storage. First, Averill, Turner & Finzi [4] analysed soil profiles across biomes and found that ecosystems dominated by ECM and ericoid plants store about 70% more carbon per unit soil nitrogen than AM-dominated ecosystems, independent of climate, clay and NPP. Their interpretation is competition: ECM fungi, with direct access to organic N, out-compete free-living decomposers for nitrogen and so slow decomposition of N-poor substrates, a mechanism first proposed by Gadgil & Gadgil (1971), who showed that trenching to exclude ECM roots accelerated litter decomposition in Pinus radiata stands. This Gadgil effect is now understood as context-dependent; it is strongest where N is scarce and weak or reversed where priming dominates.

Second, Clemmensen et al. [8] used a chronosequence of boreal forested islands and 14C bomb-spike dating to show that 50–70% of stored soil carbon in the organic horizons derives from roots and root-associated fungi rather than from aboveground litter, and that mycorrhizal necromass accumulates in the deeper humus. The classic view of soil organic matter as decomposed leaf litter is, for boreal forests, wrong; the carbon enters the soil from below. Third, elevated-CO2 experiments show that the biomass response to CO2 depends on mycorrhizal type: Terrer et al. [19] found that under low N availability, ECM plants sustained a CO2 fertilisation effect while AM plants did not, because only ECM fungi could mine the additional N required. This is one of the few clear empirical constraints on the N-limitation of the land carbon sink and it feeds directly into how models should represent N acquisition.

The glomalin story illustrates how an attractive mechanism can outrun its evidence. Glomalin, a putative AM-fungal glycoprotein, was proposed in the late 1990s as a major, recalcitrant soil-carbon pool (up to several per cent of soil C) that also stabilised aggregates. The operational measure, "glomalin-related soil protein" extracted by autoclaving in citrate and quantified by a Bradford assay, turned out to be non-specific: it co-extracts humic substances and proteins of any origin and correlates poorly with AM abundance. The consensus now is that AM fungi certainly contribute to aggregation and to necromass inputs, but that GRSP is not a specific marker and glomalin is not a defined compound.

Why the AM/ECM split matters for a carbon model

Both pathways cost the plant about the same carbon, but they put it in different places and with different stoichiometric consequences. AM: carbon mostly respired or turned over quickly in fine hyphae; N cycle inorganic and leaky; little effect on decomposition. ECM: carbon enters the soil as chitinous, melanised necromass with slow turnover; organic N withdrawn by the symbiosis reduces N available to saprotrophs, slowing decomposition (Gadgil effect) and raising C:N of what remains. A model that treats "soil" as one box with first-order decay cannot reproduce the 70% difference in C per N found by Averill et al., which is why mycorrhizal pathways appear explicitly in MIMICS-CN-type and FUN-CORPSE-type models [20].

Biological nitrogen fixation

The N2 triple bond (945 kJ mol−1) is broken biologically by one enzyme complex, nitrogenase, found only in some prokaryotes. The enzyme has two components: the Fe protein (dinitrogenase reductase), which hydrolyses ATP and delivers electrons one at a time, and the MoFe protein (dinitrogenase), whose FeMo-cofactor binds and reduces N2. The minimal stoichiometry is

$$\mathrm{N_2 + 8\,H^+ + 8\,e^- + 16\,ATP \longrightarrow 2\,NH_3 + H_2 + 16\,ADP + 16\,P_i}$$

The obligatory evolution of one H2 per N2 wastes two of the eight electrons; some rhizobia recover part of this with uptake hydrogenases. Sixteen ATP per N2 is the theoretical minimum; measured costs including nodule construction and maintenance are 6–12 g C per g N fixed, compared with roughly 1–3 g C per g N for uptake and assimilation of nitrate or ammonium from soil. Nitrogenase is irreversibly inactivated by O2, yet fixation needs large amounts of ATP, which in aerobes comes from oxidative phosphorylation. Every fixer solves this conflict somehow: cyanobacteria with heterocysts, Azotobacter with extremely high respiration rates, and legume nodules with a cortical diffusion barrier plus leghemoglobin, which keeps free O2 in the infected cells at tens of nanomolar while delivering it fast enough to bacteroid respiration.

Symbiotic fixation

The legume–rhizobia symbiosis (and the Parasponia–rhizobia exception) is initiated by chemical dialogue. Roots release flavonoids; these activate the bacterial regulator NodD, which switches on nod genes producing Nod factors (lipochitooligosaccharides). Nod factors are perceived by LysM receptor kinases on root hairs and trigger calcium spiking, root-hair curling, an infection thread that grows inward, and cortical cell division that forms the nodule. Bacteria are released into cells as bacteroids enclosed in a plant symbiosome membrane, where they fix N and export NH4+ in exchange for dicarboxylates (malate, succinate). Much of this signalling machinery (the "common symbiosis pathway") is shared with AM fungi, from which it was evolutionarily borrowed. Actinorhizal plants (Alnus, Casuarina, Myrica, Ceanothus) form analogous nodules with the actinobacterium Frankia, which protects nitrogenase inside vesicles. Field rates for crop legumes are 50–300 kg N ha−1 yr−1; alder and casuarina stands fix 50–150 kg N ha−1 yr−1.

Free-living and associative fixation

Heterotrophic free-living fixers (Azotobacter, Clostridium) are limited by carbon and typically fix 1–5 kg N ha−1 yr−1. Associative diazotrophs (Azospirillum on grass roots, Gluconacetobacter diazotrophicus inside sugarcane stems, Herbaspirillum) use root exudates and can contribute 10–50 kg N ha−1 yr−1 in some tropical grasses and Brazilian sugarcane, although estimates are debated. Cyanobacteria, autotrophic and so not carbon-limited, are the main free-living fixers in rice paddies (free-living Anabaena, Nostoc, and the Azolla–Anabaena symbiosis, 20–50 kg N ha−1 per crop), in biological soil crusts of drylands, in lichens and in moss-associated communities that supply much of the N input to boreal forests (1–3 kg N ha−1 yr−1, but sustained over centuries).

Regulation and limits

Fixation is expensive, so plants shut it down when cheaper N is available. Nitrate and ammonium inhibit nodule initiation, nodule growth and nitrogenase activity; legumes also operate autoregulation of nodulation, in which early nodules send root-derived CLE peptides to the shoot, where a LRR receptor kinase triggers a shoot-derived signal that suppresses further nodulation. The down-regulation curve of fixation against soil mineral N is approximately sigmoidal with half-inhibition at 1–3 g N m−2 of mineral N in the root zone. Fixation is further limited by phosphorus (nodules are P-rich and ATP-hungry; P addition stimulates fixation in many tropical and old-soil systems), by molybdenum (the FeMo-cofactor; Mo addition raises fixation in Mo-poor tropical forest soils, and some organisms carry vanadium or iron-only alternative nitrogenases), by light (fixers are shade-intolerant because of the carbon cost) and by temperature, which gives the cost function used in FUN its optimum near 25 °C.

Global totals

Estimating terrestrial BNF has been contentious. Cleveland et al. (1999) scaled up plot measurements by biome to 100–290 Tg N yr−1 (central estimate 195) for natural ecosystems. Vitousek et al. [9] argued that this was inconsistent with the pre-industrial N budget, river N exports and isotope mass balance, and proposed ~58 Tg N yr−1 (range 40–100) for natural terrestrial BNF, implying that pre-industrial ecosystems were strongly N-conserving. Davies-Barnard & Friedlingstein [10] compiled a much larger set of field observations and derived ~88 (52–130) Tg N yr−1, below Cleveland's figure and overlapping Vitousek's range, with tropical forests contributing most and large residual uncertainty in free-living rates. Agricultural BNF (legume crops, pastures, rice) adds roughly 50–70 Tg N yr−1, and industrial fixation (Haber–Bosch) ~120 Tg N yr−1: human activity has at least doubled the rate at which N2 is converted to reactive N.

Root traits and acquisition strategies

Roots differ in how much of the acquisition job they outsource. Bergmann et al. [13] analysed traits of ~1800 species and found that the "root economics space" has two independent axes rather than one fast–slow spectrum. The conservation gradient (root tissue density versus root N concentration) is the familiar fast–slow trade-off. Orthogonal to it is a collaboration gradient: thick-rooted species with low specific root length rely on mycorrhizal fungi to explore soil ("outsourcing"), while thin, high-SRL species explore soil themselves ("do-it-yourself"). Thick roots and high mycorrhizal colonisation co-occur because a large cortex houses more arbuscules; thin roots achieve the same soil contact per unit carbon without the fungus. Magnoliids and many tropical trees sit at the outsourcing end; grasses at the do-it-yourself end.

Lambers et al. [16] placed these strategies on a soil-age axis. On young soils N limits and N-fixers are favoured; on intermediate soils mycorrhizal strategies dominate; on very old, severely P-impoverished soils (south-western Australia, the Cape, parts of the tropics) non-mycorrhizal species with cluster roots (Proteaceae) or dauciform roots (Cyperaceae) take over. These ephemeral, densely branched root structures release bursts of carboxylates (citrate, malate) and phosphatases that mobilise sorbed and organic P by ligand exchange and chelation of Fe and Al, a chemically intensive, carbon-expensive strategy that pays only when P is so scarce that the fungal route fails. Carboxylate exudation is also how white lupin and some brassicas obtain P, and it is the mechanism behind the P-mobilising benefit of intercropping legumes with cereals.

Connections to ecosystem models

First-generation carbon–nitrogen land models (CLM4, O-CN, JULES-CN) treated plant N acquisition as a demand-driven draw on a soil mineral N pool, with fixation prescribed as a function of NPP or evapotranspiration (the Cleveland 1999 regression) and no mycorrhizae at all. The Fixation and Uptake of Nitrogen (FUN) model of Fisher et al. [11] replaced this with an optimality scheme: the plant has several routes to N, each with a carbon cost in g C per g N, and it spends carbon on N acquisition until the marginal carbon gained by investing N in leaves equals the marginal carbon spent acquiring it. The costs are

$$C_{\text{active}} = \frac{k_N}{N_{\text{soil}}} + \frac{k_C}{C_{\text{root}}}, \qquad C_{\text{fix}} = -6.25\left[\exp\!\left(-3.62 + 0.27\,T\left(1 - \tfrac{0.5\,T}{25.15}\right)\right) - 2\right], \qquad C_{\text{retrans}} = \frac{k_R}{N_{\text{leaf}}},$$

so active uptake is cheap when soil N is high and roots are abundant, fixation is cheapest near 25 °C (about 7.5 g C g−1 N, rising to 12 at 0 °C), and retranslocation from senescing leaves is cheap but limited in quantity. Rather than sending all carbon down the cheapest route, FUN mixes pathways as resistors in parallel: the N obtained from route i is proportional to 1/Ci, and the effective cost of the mixture is

$$\frac{1}{C_{\text{total}}} = \sum_i \frac{1}{C_i}, \qquad f_i = \frac{1/C_i}{\sum_j 1/C_j}.$$

Brzostek et al. [12] added mycorrhizal pathways (FUN-ECM, later FUN 2.0): AM uptake with a lower root-biomass cost term, and ECM uptake whose cost depends on soil organic as well as mineral N, allowing ECM plants to keep acquiring N where AM plants cannot. This version, with further revisions by Shi et al. (2016), is the N-acquisition scheme in CLM5, where the carbon spent on N acquisition is removed from NPP as an explicit flux and N fixation therefore responds to soil N, temperature and NPP rather than being prescribed. Sulman et al. [20] went further in a global model by coupling FUN-style AM and ECM acquisition to the microbe-explicit CORPSE soil model, so that ECM "mining" of organic N and the resulting slowdown of decomposition emerge from the same scheme; the mycorrhizal distribution altered simulated terrestrial carbon storage by tens of Pg C.

Other model families take different routes. ELM (E3SM Land Model) uses the Equilibrium Chemistry Approximation (ECA) of Zhu et al. (2016), in which plants, microbes and mineral surfaces compete for N and P in proportion to their affinities and abundances, as in a set of simultaneous Michaelis–Menten reactions; mycorrhizae are implicit in the root affinity. ORCHIDEE-CN keeps an ET-based or NPP-based fixation function and demand-driven uptake. MYCOFON (Meyer et al. 2010) is a stand-scale mycorrhiza model that couples fungal biomass to tree C supply and N return and was used to show that mycorrhizal C costs are a significant NPP sink in temperate forests. MIMICS-CN extends the microbial-explicit MIMICS soil model with N, so that microbial N demand controls the fate of litter, and variants with mycorrhizal pathways let plant symbionts compete directly with saprotrophs. The Terrer et al. result [19] that CO2 fertilisation under low N is an ECM phenomenon has become a benchmark these schemes are expected to reproduce.

Exam-style questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer.

1. Define rhizodeposition, list its components, and explain why its magnitude matters for a soil-carbon model.

Rhizodeposition is the total release of organic carbon from living roots into soil: soluble exudates (sugars, amino acids, carboxylates), mucilage from the root cap, border cells, sloughed epidermal and cortical cells and root-hair turnover; root respiration is sometimes counted as rhizodeposited C that is immediately lost. Isotope pulse-labelling gives 5–21% of net fixed C, about 11% on average, concentrated near root tips and increased under nutrient deficiency. It matters for three reasons. (i) It is a flux of fresh, labile carbon that is the primary energy source of the rhizosphere microbiome and so controls microbial biomass, enzyme production and nutrient mineralisation. (ii) It primes decomposition of old SOM (rhizosphere priming, typically +20–60%), so an increase in belowground allocation, e.g. under elevated CO2, can accelerate soil C loss rather than build soil C. (iii) It is a large carbon input bypassing the litter layer: in boreal forests 50–70% of stored C is root- and mycorrhiza-derived. Models that drive soil C only with litterfall and treat decomposition as first-order with fixed rate constants cannot capture (ii) or (iii); microbial-explicit models with root C inputs can.

2. Compare arbuscular and ectomycorrhizal symbioses in structure, taxonomy, host range and nutrient function, and explain how these differences lead to the "mycorrhizal-associated nutrient economy".

AM fungi (Glomeromycota, obligate biotrophs, ~72% of plant species including grasses, herbs, crops and many tropical trees) penetrate cortical cell walls and form intracellular arbuscules surrounded by a plant membrane; they have essentially no capacity to degrade organic matter and deliver mainly inorganic P (also Zn, Cu, some NH4+) by extending the P depletion zone with fine extraradical hyphae. ECM fungi (Basidio- and Ascomycota evolved repeatedly from saprotrophs, ~2% of species but most boreal and temperate trees) form a mantle around the root tip and a Hartig net between epidermal cells, never entering cells; many retain proteases and oxidative enzymes and deliver N, including amino acids from organic matter, as well as P. Phillips et al. (2013) argued that these functional differences propagate to ecosystem biogeochemistry: AM-dominated stands have high-quality litter, fast decomposition, high mineralisation and nitrification, an inorganic and leaky N cycle, and nutrients cycled by free-living microbes; ECM stands have recalcitrant litter, slow decomposition, high soil C:N, little nitrification, N taken up as organic compounds through the symbiont and more carbon stored in organic horizons. The mycorrhizal type of the dominant trees therefore predicts soil properties, allowing inventory data to parameterise soil N and C models.

3. Why do ectomycorrhizal ecosystems store more soil carbon per unit nitrogen than arbuscular ones? Give the evidence and the proposed mechanisms, including the Gadgil effect.

Averill, Turner & Finzi (2014) compared soil profiles across biomes and found ~70% more C per unit N in ECM- and ericoid-dominated systems than in AM systems, after controlling for climate, clay and productivity. Mechanisms: (1) Competition for N. ECM fungi mine organic N with proteases and oxidative enzymes, leaving saprotrophs N-limited and slowing their decomposition of N-poor substrates; this is the Gadgil effect, demonstrated by Gadgil & Gadgil (1971), who found that trenching to exclude ECM roots accelerated litter decomposition. (2) Input quality and location. ECM-associated trees produce recalcitrant litter, and the symbiosis itself delivers chitinous, melanised hyphal necromass directly into the mineral soil and humus (Clemmensen et al. 2013: 50–70% of boreal soil C is root/fungus-derived). (3) Nitrogen-cycle leakiness. AM systems nitrify and leach N, lowering the N available to build and stabilise SOM, while ECM systems retain N in organic forms. Caveats: the Gadgil effect is context-dependent and can be reversed by priming where N is abundant; the AM/ECM contrast partly covaries with climate and soil type; and the glomalin hypothesis for AM-driven C storage has not survived scrutiny because the assay is non-specific.

4. Write the nitrogenase reaction, explain why fixation costs 6–12 g C per g N, and describe how a legume nodule reconciles the enzyme's O₂ sensitivity with its ATP demand.

N2 + 8H+ + 8e− + 16 ATP → 2NH3 + H2 + 16 ADP + 16 Pi. Eight electrons are required although only six reduce N2, because one H2 is evolved obligatorily per N2; sixteen ATP is the minimum, two per electron delivered by the Fe protein to the MoFe protein. In carbon terms, 16 ATP per 28 g N at ~30 ATP per glucose (180 g, 72 g C) is only ~1.4 g C per g N for the reaction itself, but the plant also pays for nodule construction and maintenance, for respiration that protects nitrogenase from O2, for H2 evolution, and for exporting ammonium as amides or ureides; measured totals are 6–12 g C per g N, compared with 1–3 g C per g N for uptake and assimilation of soil N. Nitrogenase is irreversibly oxidised by O2, yet bacteroids generate ATP by aerobic respiration. The nodule solves this with a cortical diffusion barrier that limits O2 entry, high bacteroid respiration that consumes O2 at the periphery, and leghemoglobin, which binds O2 tightly (Kd ~ nM), keeps free O2 in infected cells at ~10–50 nM, and shuttles it to the bacteroid membranes fast enough to sustain oxidative phosphorylation. The same conflict is solved by heterocysts in cyanobacteria and vesicles in Frankia.

5. Explain how the FUN model decides how a plant acquires nitrogen, derive the resistors-in-parallel mixing rule, and state what this changes relative to a demand-driven uptake scheme.

FUN (Fisher et al. 2010) assigns each N pathway a carbon cost in g C g−1 N: active uptake Cact = kN/Nsoil + kC/Croot (cheaper with more soil N and more roots), symbiotic fixation Cfix(T) = −6.25[exp(−3.62 + 0.27T(1 − 0.5T/25.15)) − 2] (Houlton et al. 2008; ~7.5 at 25 °C, ~12 at 0 °C), retranslocation from senescing leaves Cret = kR/Nleaf (cheap but limited to the leaf N pool), and in FUN 2.0/CLM5 mycorrhizal AM and ECM costs with their own k terms, the ECM one depending on soil organic N. The plant spends carbon on N acquisition while the carbon return on the N obtained (through leaf N and photosynthesis) exceeds the cost; the rest of NPP goes to growth. Pathways are combined like electrical resistors in parallel: if each route i has "resistance" Ci and the N flux through it is Ni = Cspent/Ci for a common carbon "potential", total N = CspentΣ(1/Ci), so the effective cost is 1/Ctot = Σ 1/Ci and the fraction from route i is (1/Ci)/Σj(1/Cj). Cheap routes dominate without being exclusive. Relative to demand-driven uptake (plant takes what it needs from the mineral N pool, fixation prescribed from NPP or ET), FUN makes N acquisition cost NPP explicitly, makes fixation emerge where and when soil N is scarce and warm, lets ECM plants exploit organic N, and produces a CO2 fertilisation response that depends on mycorrhizal type, as observed by Terrer et al. (2016).

6. Phosphate is nearly immobile in soil. Starting from the diffusion equation, explain why a root depletes only a thin cylinder of soil and how mycorrhizal hyphae overcome this. What soil properties make the mycorrhizal benefit largest?

In soil the transport equation for a sorbing solute is b ∂Cl/∂t = ∇·(Dlθf ∇Cl) − S, where Cl is solution concentration, θ volumetric water content, f an impedance (tortuosity) factor, and b = dCtotal/dCl the buffer power (102–103 for P). Dividing by b gives an effective diffusivity De = Dlθf/b of order 10−13–10−12 m2 s−1, 103–104 times smaller than for nitrate. With a zero-sink root surface (uptake faster than supply, Nye–Tinker), the depletion front advances as roughly √(Det): about 1 mm in a week, a few mm in a season. P more than a few millimetres from a root is effectively unavailable, so uptake is limited by root length density, not by root uptake kinetics. Hyphae overcome this geometrically and chemically: they are 2–10 µm thick, so a gram of carbon buys ~100× the length of roots and places absorbing surface tens of millimetres beyond the root, outside the depletion zone and in pores too fine for roots; their 1/r decline in density is compensated by branching; they transport P as polyphosphate to the arbuscule; and they release phosphatases (organic P) and, in ECM, oxalate (Ca/Fe/Al-P). The benefit is largest where De is smallest (high buffer power: Fe/Al oxide-rich, acidic or calcareous soils; dry soils with low θ and f) and where root length density is low (thick-rooted species, early growth), and smallest in fertilised or sandy soils, which is why mycorrhizal growth responses decline with soil P and why thick-rooted species sit at the "outsourcing" end of the root economics space.

7. Why are symbiotic nitrogen fixers rare in nitrogen-rich ecosystems and in mature boreal and temperate forests, but common in the tropics and in early succession? Discuss physiological regulation, cost–benefit and other limits.

Physiology: mineral N inhibits nodule initiation and nitrogenase activity, and legumes operate autoregulation of nodulation (root CLE peptides, shoot receptor kinase), so fixation is sigmoidally down-regulated with half-inhibition at ~1–3 g N m−2 of mineral N. Economics: fixation costs 6–12 g C g−1 N versus 1–3 for soil N, so by the FUN logic the crossover N* = kN/Cfix lies at low soil N; above it a fixer spends carbon a non-fixer does not, loses in competition for light, and is excluded. Temperature: Cfix rises from ~7.5 g C g−1 N at 25 °C to ~12 at 0 °C, so fixation is dearer in cold soils; this plus the long-lived N-conserving organic economy of ECM forests (which supply N via the fungal route) explains the scarcity of fixers in boreal and temperate forests, where instead moss–cyanobacteria associations provide small but sustained inputs. Other limits: P and Mo requirements (nodules are P- and Mo-rich; Mo addition raises fixation on tropical soils), shade intolerance because of the carbon cost, and herbivory of N-rich tissue. Early succession (lava flows, glacial forelands, disturbed sites) combines low N, high light and often young P-rich soils, favouring Alnus, Lupinus, Coriaria. Lowland tropical forests are a paradox: legume trees are abundant but mostly do not fix, or fix facultatively in gaps and disturbed patches; the explanation offered by Hedin and Menge is facultative fixation that switches on only where N limits locally, keeping the biome N-rich while maintaining capacity.

8. Describe the root economics space of Bergmann et al. (2020) and explain how mycorrhizal "outsourcing" relates to the resource-acquisition strategies of Lambers et al. (2008) along a soil-age gradient.

Bergmann et al. analysed ~1800 species and found that root traits vary along two independent axes. The conservation gradient runs from fast roots (high N, low tissue density, short lifespan) to slow roots (low N, dense tissue, long lifespan) and mirrors the leaf economics spectrum. The collaboration gradient runs from thin, high-specific-root-length roots that explore soil cheaply themselves ("do-it-yourself") to thick, low-SRL roots with large cortices that house many arbuscules and rely on mycorrhizal fungi for exploration ("outsourcing"); mycorrhizal colonisation increases with root diameter. The two axes are orthogonal, so a species can be any combination of fast/slow and DIY/outsourcing, and whole-plant root economics cannot be collapsed to one axis. Lambers et al. place acquisition strategies along soil age: young soils are N-limited and favour N-fixing symbioses (lots of P, little N); intermediate soils are N- and P-co-limited and favour mycorrhizal strategies (outsourcing, especially AM for P and ECM for N); the oldest, most P-impoverished soils (Western Australian kwongan, Cape fynbos) favour non-mycorrhizal species with cluster or dauciform roots that release carboxylates and phosphatases to mobilise sorbed and organic P, a carbon-expensive DIY chemical strategy that works only when P is so scarce that hyphae find nothing to collect. Both schemes are statements about marginal carbon return per unit nutrient: outsourcing pays when fungi explore soil more cheaply than roots and when the nutrient is diffusion-limited; DIY chemistry pays when the limiting nutrient is chemically locked rather than spatially distant; fixation pays when N, not P, is scarce.
