Topic 8 of the SPAC section

Acclimation and Thermal Response

How photosynthesis and respiration respond to temperature over minutes, how those responses shift when plants live at a different temperature for weeks, and why the difference decides whether a warmer world holds more or less carbon on land.

The one-paragraph version

Every enzyme-catalysed step in a leaf speeds up with temperature (Arrhenius), and most slow down again at high temperature as proteins and membranes lose function (the peaked Arrhenius). The net rate of photosynthesis has a cooler optimum than Vcmax because photorespiration and the Rubisco kinetic constants also rise with temperature. These are instantaneous responses, measured in minutes. Over days to weeks plants acclimate: a leaf grown warm shifts its temperature optimum upward (by about a third of a degree per degree of growth temperature), lowers its Jmax/Vcmax ratio, and reduces its respiration at any given temperature. Over generations populations adapt. Earth-system models that use fixed instantaneous response curves therefore exaggerate both the loss of tropical photosynthesis and the surge in respiration under warming; models that include acclimation (Kattge & Knorr for photosynthesis, Atkin et al. for respiration) project a larger future land carbon sink. The central exam skills are to write the peaked-Arrhenius function and its optimum, to state the empirical acclimation relations, to distinguish type I and type II respiratory acclimation, and to explain why mismatched acclimation of A and R matters for NPP.

Examiner's lens

Expect to be handed a temperature response curve and asked (i) which parameters shape it, (ii) how it would move if the plant were grown 5 °C warmer, (iii) whether a Q10 is adequate, and (iv) what a model that ignores acclimation gets wrong. Be ready to derive Topt from the peaked Arrhenius function and to sketch A, R and A − R versus temperature on the same axes.

Three timescales: response, acclimation, adaptation

Instantaneous response (seconds to minutes): the change in a rate when leaf temperature changes with everything else fixed; it reflects enzyme kinetics, membrane fluidity and gas solubilities, and is what an A–T curve on a gas-exchange system measures. Acclimation (days to weeks): a phenotypic adjustment of the response curve itself after sustained exposure to a new growth temperature, through changed enzyme amounts, isoforms, lipid composition and allocation. Acclimation is reversible and happens within a leaf (for existing leaves) or by producing new leaves with different properties (developmental acclimation, usually stronger). Adaptation (generations): genetic change in the response, visible as differences between populations or species from contrasting climates; "home vs away" comparisons in common gardens separate it from acclimation. Berry & Björkman (1980) [1] established this framework and showed that the photosynthetic optimum of desert and coastal Atriplex and Nerium tracks the growth temperature. For a modeller the distinction maps onto parameters: instantaneous responses are the temperature functions inside the model, acclimation makes their parameters functions of recent climate, and adaptation makes them functions of plant functional type or location.

Arrhenius, peaked Arrhenius, Q10 and Topt

For a parameter k with activation energy Ha (J mol⁻¹), relative to its value at 25 °C (Tref = 298.15 K):

$$\frac{k(T)}{k_{25}} = \exp\!\left[\frac{H_a}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_{ref}} - \frac{1}{T}\right)\right] = \exp\!\left[\frac{H_a\,(T - T_{ref})}{R\,T_{ref}\,T}\right]$$

This never saturates. Enzymes denature and complexes dissociate at high temperature, which Johnson, Eyring & Williams (1942) represented with a thermodynamic deactivation term with enthalpy Hd and entropy ΔS. The peaked Arrhenius (Medlyn et al. 2002 [2], Kattge & Knorr 2007 [3] form) is

$$\frac{k(T)}{k_{25}} = \exp\!\left[\frac{H_a\,(T - T_{ref})}{R\,T_{ref}\,T}\right]\;\frac{1 + \exp\!\left(\dfrac{T_{ref}\,\Delta S - H_d}{R\,T_{ref}}\right)}{1 + \exp\!\left(\dfrac{T\,\Delta S - H_d}{R\,T}\right)}$$

Setting the derivative to zero gives the temperature of the peak,

$$T_{opt} = \frac{H_d}{\Delta S - R\,\ln\!\left(\dfrac{H_a}{H_d - H_a}\right)},$$

which shows that with Hd fixed (200 kJ mol⁻¹ is conventional, because Hd and ΔS are strongly correlated in fits), ΔS alone sets Topt: a lower ΔS means a higher optimum. This is the handle through which acclimation enters models. Ha controls the steepness of the rise below the optimum; Hd the sharpness of the decline. Medlyn et al. (2002) [2] fitted this form to Vcmax and Jmax data from many species and found the peaked form necessary for Jmax (optimum ≈ 30–35 °C) but often not for Vcmax below 40 °C.

Q10 and why it is not enough

The Q10 is the factor by which a rate changes for a 10 °C rise: \(k(T) = k_{ref}\,Q_{10}^{(T - T_{ref})/10}\). It is exactly exponential in temperature, whereas Arrhenius is exponential in 1/T, so an Arrhenius process has a Q10 that declines with temperature: \(Q_{10} = \exp[10 H_a/(R\,T\,(T+10))]\), about 2.4 at 10 °C and 2.0 at 30 °C for Ha = 55 kJ mol⁻¹. Measured respiration shows an even stronger decline of Q10 with temperature (Tjoelker et al. 2001: Q10 ≈ 3.22 − 0.046 T), which is why a single Q10 overpredicts respiration at high temperature and underpredicts it at low temperature. For soil respiration the apparent Q10 also absorbs seasonal covariation of temperature with moisture, substrate supply and root growth (see the last section). Q10 remains in use because it is one parameter and intuitive, but any serious model should treat it as a local tangent, not a law.

Photosynthetic acclimation: Kattge & Knorr and the global synthesis

Kattge & Knorr (2007) [3] re-analysed temperature response data for 36 species grown at 11–35 °C and expressed the peaked-Arrhenius parameters as linear functions of growth temperature Tg (°C, the mean of the preceding ~30 days), with Hd fixed at 200 kJ mol⁻¹, Ha,V = 71.5 and Ha,J = 49.9 kJ mol⁻¹:

$$\Delta S_V = 668.39 - 1.07\,T_g,\qquad \Delta S_J = 659.70 - 0.75\,T_g \quad(\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}),$$
$$r_{JV} = \frac{J_{max,25}}{V_{cmax,25}} = 2.59 - 0.035\,T_g.$$

Because ΔS falls with Tg, Topt rises: roughly 0.44 °C per °C for Vcmax and 0.33 °C per °C for Jmax over the fitted range. The decline of rJV with growth temperature means warm-grown leaves invest relatively less in electron transport and more in Rubisco, consistent with co-limitation being maintained as the temperature optimum of Jmax moves. These relations were adopted by CLM4.5/5, JULES (as an option) and many offline models because they require only a running mean of air temperature.

Kumarathunge et al. (2019) [4] assembled 141 datasets of A–T curves from 83 species spanning Arctic to tropical sites and both growth-chamber and field studies. Main findings: the temperature optimum of net photosynthesis increases with growth temperature by about 0.3–0.4 °C per °C across the global dataset; the shift is achieved mostly through changes in the Jmax/Vcmax ratio and the ΔS of Jmax, with the activation energy of Vcmax also increasing with growth temperature (unlike in Kattge & Knorr, where Ha was fixed); the breadth of the response is also a function of climate; and separating "home" (climate of origin) from "away" (recent growth) temperature shows that both acclimation and adaptation contribute, with acclimation dominant for ΔSJ and adaptation visible in Ha,V. The practical outcome is an updated set of functions that replace Kattge & Knorr in several models; the lesson is that tropical leaves are already near their optimum, so warming reduces their photosynthesis despite acclimation, while boreal leaves gain.

Respiration: instantaneous response, acclimation and the global curve

Leaf dark respiration Rd rises roughly exponentially with temperature over the physiological range, with Q10 ≈ 2 near 20 °C but declining as temperature rises. Heskel et al. (2016) [7] fitted 673 temperature–respiration curves from 231 species across all major biomes and found a globally convergent shape well described by a log-polynomial,

$$\ln R = a + b\,T + c\,T^2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad R(T) = R_{T_{ref}}\exp\!\left[b\,(T - T_{ref}) + c\,(T^2 - T_{ref}^2)\right],$$
$$b = 0.1012\ \mathrm{°C^{-1}},\qquad c = -0.0005\ \mathrm{°C^{-2}},$$

with the same b and c across biomes and plant functional types; only the basal rate differs. The negative c captures the falling Q10 (from ≈ 2.4 at 5 °C to ≈ 1.6 at 35 °C), and the single curve replaces biome-specific Q10 values in models. Atkin et al. (2015) [6] assembled a global database of Rd,25 and showed that, at a standard temperature, leaves from cold sites respire more than leaves from warm sites, and that Rd,25 relates to leaf N, P, LMA and Vcmax with climate as an additional predictor.

Type I and type II acclimation

Atkin & Tjoelker (2003) [5] distinguish two patterns when a plant is moved to a warmer growth temperature. Type I: the Q10 falls (the response curve flattens), so acclimated and non-acclimated leaves respire similarly at low measurement temperatures but differ increasingly at high ones; this reflects changes in substrate supply and adenylate control of existing enzymes and can occur within days in existing leaves. Type II: the basal rate (R at the reference temperature) falls with little change in Q10, shifting the whole curve down; this reflects changed mitochondrial density, protein content and respiratory capacity and generally requires new tissue developed at the new temperature. Full type II acclimation gives homeostasis: the leaf respires at the same rate in its new, warmer environment as it did in the old one. Globally, acclimation of R to warming is substantial (Atkin et al. 2015 and Heskel et al. 2016 suggest that the realised increase in R with a 10 °C warmer growth temperature is far smaller than an instantaneous Q10 of 2 would predict, often of the order of 20–40% rather than 100%), and models that ignore it overstate the respiratory feedback to warming.

Mechanisms and thermal tolerance

Acclimation of photosynthesis to warm temperatures involves (Yamori, Hikosaka & Way 2014 [9]; Way & Yamori 2014 [8]): (i) membrane lipid composition: fewer unsaturated fatty acids in thylakoid membranes raise the temperature at which they become leaky, protecting PSII and electron transport; (ii) Rubisco activase: this ATP-dependent chaperone is the most heat-labile step of the Calvin cycle (deactivating above ~35–40 °C), and warm-grown plants express more thermostable isoforms, which raises the temperature at which Vcmax falls; (iii) nitrogen reallocation between Rubisco and electron-transport components, visible as the fall in Jmax/Vcmax with growth temperature; (iv) heat shock proteins and other chaperones, and increased antioxidant capacity against reactive oxygen species generated when electron sinks are limited. Cold acclimation works the other way (more unsaturated lipids, more Rubisco and cycle enzymes to compensate for low kcat, sink-strength adjustments to avoid TPU limitation).

Thermal tolerance thresholds. Tcrit is the temperature at which minimal chlorophyll fluorescence (F0) begins to rise sharply, indicating the onset of PSII damage, and T50 is where Fv/Fm has fallen by half. O'Sullivan et al. (2017) [12] measured 218 species across a global temperature gradient and found Tcrit of roughly 42–50 °C, increasing with latitude-related mean temperature by only ~0.3 °C per °C of site temperature, so that the thermal safety margin (Tcrit minus maximum leaf temperature) is smallest in the mid-latitudes and the tropics, not at the poles. Leaf temperatures regularly exceed air temperature by 5–10 °C in still, sunny conditions, especially when stomata close under drought, so heatwaves push leaves toward Tcrit in exactly the conditions when transpirational cooling fails. For models, Tcrit provides a hard upper bound that peaked-Arrhenius functions should respect, and an irreversible damage threshold that none of the standard functions represent.

Mismatch, optimality and what models do

Why the mismatch between A and R acclimation matters

NPP is the small difference between GPP and Ra. If respiration acclimates strongly (type II homeostasis) and photosynthesis only partly, warming reduces respiratory losses relative to a fixed-Q10 projection and NPP is more robust; if photosynthesis acclimates and respiration does not, the opposite. Smith & Dukes (2013) [10] reviewed the evidence and concluded that models with fixed responses systematically overestimate the loss of net carbon uptake with warming, because they ignore both forms of acclimation. Lombardozzi et al. (2015) [11] implemented Kattge & Knorr acclimation for Vcmax and Jmax and Atkin-style acclimation of Rd in CLM4.5 and found that acclimation increased simulated land carbon uptake under RCP8.5 relative to the fixed-parameter version, with the respiratory acclimation acting everywhere and the photosynthetic acclimation increasing GPP at high latitudes but slightly decreasing it in the tropics (where leaves are pushed past their optimum regardless). Mercado et al. (2018) [17] showed in JULES that geographical and temporal variation in the thermal response of Vcmax and Jmax, including acclimation, changes the projected land carbon store by amounts comparable to the spread among CMIP5 models, which makes the thermal response one of the most consequential plant-physiological uncertainties in Earth-system projections.

Optimality-based acclimation

Rather than fitting ΔS to growth temperature empirically, optimality models predict the acclimated state from first principles. Wang et al. (2017) [15] combined the least-cost hypothesis (plants minimise the summed costs of maintaining carboxylation and transpiration capacity, predicting Ci/Ca as a function of VPD, temperature and elevation) with the coordination hypothesis (Vcmax acclimates so that Rubisco-limited and electron-transport-limited rates are equal under typical daytime conditions) to form the P-model, which predicts GPP from light, temperature, VPD and CO₂ with essentially no PFT-specific parameters. Smith et al. (2019) [16] used the same eco-evolutionary optimality logic to predict Vcmax,25 globally and showed it captures the observed decline of Vcmax,25 with growth temperature and its increase with light, better than N-based predictors. In these models acclimation of capacity emerges from the environment, which is attractive for projections because the empirical functions were fitted to a limited range of conditions.

Phenology and growing degree days

Temperature also acts through timing. Leaf-out and flowering in temperate species are commonly modelled with growing degree days, \(\mathrm{GDD} = \sum \max(0, T_{mean} - T_{base})\), accumulated from a start date until a threshold is reached, with chilling requirements and photoperiod as additional controls. Warming advances spring and lengthens the growing season, which is a form of acclimation of the season rather than of the leaf, and in many models contributes as much to the warming response of GPP as the leaf-level thermal response. The threshold GDD itself appears to decline when chilling is insufficient, another acclimation-like adjustment that fixed-parameter phenology models miss.

Soil respiration Q10 and its confounders

Soil respiration is routinely fitted with Q10 (typically 1.5–3, median ≈ 2.4 across sites) or the Lloyd & Taylor (1994) function. Davidson & Janssens (2006) [14] separate the intrinsic temperature sensitivity of decomposition (set by activation energy, higher for recalcitrant substrates by Arrhenius kinetics: the "carbon quality–temperature" hypothesis) from the apparent sensitivity observed in the field, which is confounded by substrate supply (root exudation and litterfall peak in the warm season), by moisture (dry summers suppress respiration when it is warmest, lowering the apparent Q10), by physical protection and diffusion limits, and by microbial acclimation and community change. The apparent Q10 of soil respiration is therefore not a transferable parameter, and the long-running debate over whether old soil carbon is more or less temperature-sensitive than young carbon turns on this distinction.

Three points for an exam answer. First, which quantities acclimate in a given model: usually ΔS (hence Topt), rJV and Rd,25, rarely Ha or Tcrit. Second, over what window the growth temperature is averaged (10–30 days), because that sets how fast the model responds to a heatwave versus a climatic trend. Third, the sign of the result: acclimation consistently increases projected land carbon storage relative to fixed responses, with the largest effects in the tropics (where it limits GPP loss) and boreal zone (where it limits respiratory increase).

Exam-style questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer.

1. Write the peaked-Arrhenius function, derive the expression for its optimum temperature, and explain which parameter a model should change to represent thermal acclimation.

k(T) = k25 exp[Ha(T − Tref)/(R Tref T)] × [1 + exp((TrefΔS − Hd)/(R Tref))]/[1 + exp((TΔS − Hd)/(RT))]. Ignoring the constant normalisation, write k ∝ exp(−Ha/RT)/[1 + exp((TΔS − Hd)/RT)]. Differentiate ln k with respect to T: d ln k/dT = Ha/(RT²) − [exp(x) · (Hd/(RT²))]/[1 + exp(x)] where x = (TΔS − Hd)/(RT) and dx/dT = Hd/(RT²). Setting to zero: Ha(1 + ex) = Hd ex, so ex = Ha/(Hd − Ha), i.e. (ToptΔS − Hd)/(RTopt) = ln[Ha/(Hd − Ha)], giving Topt = Hd/[ΔS − R ln(Ha/(Hd − Ha))]. With Hd fixed at 200 kJ mol⁻¹ (because Hd and ΔS are nearly collinear in fits) and Ha fixed, Topt depends only on ΔS: decreasing ΔS raises Topt. Kattge & Knorr therefore make ΔS a declining linear function of growth temperature (ΔSV = 668.39 − 1.07 Tg), which shifts the optimum by about 0.44 °C per °C; they also make Jmax,25/Vcmax,25 decline with Tg. Kumarathunge et al. additionally let Ha,V rise with growth temperature.

2. Why does a single Q10 misrepresent the temperature response of respiration, and what do Heskel et al. (2016) propose instead?

A constant Q10 implies ln R is linear in T. Both theory and data disagree. Theory: an Arrhenius process is exponential in 1/T, so its Q10 = exp[10 Ha/(RT(T + 10))] declines with temperature (2.4 at 10 °C to 2.0 at 30 °C for Ha = 55 kJ mol⁻¹). Data: measured leaf respiration curves bend even more, with Q10 falling from above 3 near 0 °C to about 1.5 at 35 °C, because at high temperatures respiration becomes limited by substrate and adenylate availability rather than enzyme capacity, and at very high temperatures by membrane and enzyme damage. A Q10 fitted at moderate temperatures therefore overpredicts R at high temperature and underpredicts it at low temperature, which biases the simulated respiratory response to warming upward. Heskel et al. fitted 673 curves from 231 species and found a globally convergent second-order log-polynomial, ln R = a + bT + cT² with b = 0.1012 and c = −0.0005 (T in °C), i.e. R(T) = Rref exp[b(T − Tref) + c(T² − Tref²)], with the same curvature across biomes and only the basal rate varying. Implemented in a model this replaces biome-specific Q10 values with one shape plus an acclimating or trait-predicted R25, and reduces the simulated increase in leaf respiration under warming.

3. Distinguish type I and type II acclimation of respiration. Sketch both (in words) and say what each implies for respiration in a warmer climate.

Both describe how the R–T curve changes after a plant is grown at a warmer temperature. Type I: the slope (Q10) decreases, so the acclimated and non-acclimated curves coincide at low measurement temperature and diverge increasingly at high temperature; on a plot of R vs T the warm-grown curve is flatter and lies below the cool-grown one only above the pivot temperature. It is attributed to adjustments in substrate supply, adenylate restriction and enzyme regulation, and can occur in existing leaves within days. Type II: the basal rate Rref falls with little change in Q10, so the whole curve shifts down; it reflects reduced respiratory capacity (mitochondrial number, protein content) and typically needs leaves developed at the new temperature. Full type II acclimation yields homeostasis, the leaf respiring at its new growth temperature at the rate it previously had at the old one. Implications: with type I, warming raises respiration less than Q10 would predict, especially during warm days, but cool nights are unaffected; with type II, respiration at all temperatures is lower, so the annual respiratory flux may hardly change. In both cases models that use a fixed Q10 and fixed R25 overstate the respiratory feedback to warming; CLM5 and JULES now reduce Rd,25 with growth temperature following Atkin et al. (2015), which is a type II representation.

4. A tropical forest leaf and a boreal conifer needle are each warmed 4 °C with full acclimation. Predict the direction of change in net photosynthesis for each and justify with the shape of the A–T curve.

The A–T curve is asymmetric: a gentle rise below Topt, a steep fall above it. Acclimation moves Topt by only ~0.3–0.4 °C per °C of growth temperature, so a 4 °C warming moves the optimum about 1.5 °C while the operating temperature moves 4 °C; leaves end up relatively further up the warm side of their curve. The tropical leaf already operates at or slightly above its optimum (Topt ≈ 28–32 °C, midday leaf temperatures 30–38 °C), so warming pushes it onto the steep descending limb where photorespiration, Rubisco deactivation and high VPD-driven stomatal closure all reduce A; acclimation softens but does not reverse the loss. The boreal needle operates well below its optimum for most of the season (growth temperatures 10–15 °C, Topt ≈ 20–25 °C), so a 4 °C warming moves it up the ascending limb, increases A, and the acclimation (slightly higher optimum, lower rJV) adds further; it also lengthens the growing season. This is the pattern Kumarathunge et al. and Lombardozzi et al. find: photosynthetic acclimation produces gains at high latitudes and smaller losses, but still losses, in the tropics, with the tropical response being the most consequential for global GPP because that is where most of it occurs. Caveat: both answers assume adequate water; drought changes the sign for the boreal needle by raising leaf temperature toward Tcrit.

5. Explain how Lombardozzi et al. (2015) implemented thermal acclimation in a land model and why it changed the projected carbon cycle. What are the limitations of their approach?

They added two empirical acclimation schemes to CLM4.5. For photosynthesis, the ΔS parameters of the peaked-Arrhenius functions for Vcmax and Jmax and the Jmax,25/Vcmax,25 ratio were made linear functions of the 10-day mean air temperature following Kattge & Knorr (2007), so that Topt rises in warm periods. For respiration, leaf Rd,25 was reduced with increasing growth temperature following Atkin and colleagues' type II acclimation. Running RCP8.5 to 2100, respiratory acclimation lowered autotrophic respiration everywhere, and photosynthetic acclimation increased GPP at mid and high latitudes while reducing it slightly in the tropics; net, acclimation increased land carbon storage relative to the fixed-parameter run by a significant fraction of the projected sink. The reason is that fixed functions parameterised at 25 °C place warming leaves on the steep side of their curves and let respiration rise with a constant Q10; acclimation flattens both responses. Limitations: the functions were fitted to a limited set of mostly temperate species and extrapolated to tropical and boreal temperatures; Ha, Tcrit and stomatal parameters were not acclimated; acclimation was instantaneous with the 10-day mean, with no lag for leaf development; respiration acclimation was applied to leaves only, not to stems and roots; and the scheme does not represent damage or mortality above Tcrit. Mercado et al. (2018) showed that alternative plausible thermal-response assumptions change the land carbon store by amounts comparable to inter-model spread, so the uncertainty is large.

6. What is the "apparent" versus "intrinsic" temperature sensitivity of soil respiration, and how does the distinction affect the interpretation of a field-measured Q10?

Intrinsic sensitivity is the response of a decomposition reaction to temperature when substrate, enzyme and moisture are not limiting; by Arrhenius kinetics it should be higher for substrates with higher activation energy (chemically recalcitrant compounds), the carbon quality–temperature hypothesis. Apparent sensitivity is what a seasonal regression of soil CO₂ efflux on soil temperature yields. Davidson & Janssens (2006) list the confounders: substrate supply co-varies with temperature (root exudation and fresh litter are greatest in the growing season), so part of the apparent response is a supply effect; soil moisture limits respiration when soils are hot and dry, lowering the apparent Q10, or when cold and waterlogged; physical protection (aggregates, mineral association) and diffusion limits make the available substrate pool itself temperature-dependent; roots and microbes acclimate and communities shift; and the temperature at the measurement depth does not represent the temperature where most respiration occurs. A field Q10 of 2.4 therefore cannot be transferred to a different site or climate, nor decomposed into root and microbial parts, and models that use it to project warming feedbacks confuse a seasonal correlation with a causal response. Better practice: measure Q10 from short-term manipulations with substrate controlled, separate heterotrophic from autotrophic respiration (trenching, girdling, isotopes), fit moisture jointly with temperature, and treat the long-term response as including acclimation and substrate depletion.

7. Describe how optimality-based models (P-model; Smith et al. 2019) predict acclimated photosynthetic capacity without empirical acclimation functions, and give one strength and one weakness relative to the Kattge & Knorr approach.

Two hypotheses are combined. The least-cost hypothesis states that plants adjust Ci/Ca to minimise the combined unit costs of maintaining carboxylation capacity and water transport, which predicts χ = Ci/Ca as a function of VPD, temperature (through Γ*, K and water viscosity) and elevation, matching the global pattern in leaf δ¹³C. The coordination hypothesis states that Vcmax acclimates over weeks so that the Rubisco-limited rate equals the electron-transport-limited rate under average daytime conditions of light, temperature and Ci; hence Vcmax = φ0 I (Ci + K)/(Ci + 2Γ*) with a correction for Jmax limitation. Given monthly light, temperature, VPD and CO₂ the P-model thus predicts χ, Vcmax, Jmax and GPP; Smith et al. (2019) show the predicted Vcmax,25 reproduces the observed global decline with growth temperature and increase with irradiance. Strength: acclimation emerges from the environment, so the scheme extrapolates to novel climates and CO₂ without the limited calibration range of the Kattge & Knorr regressions, and it removes PFT-specific Vcmax values. Weakness: it predicts only the acclimated steady state, not the instantaneous response or the dynamics of adjustment, it assumes nutrients allow the optimal capacity to be built, and the cost parameters are themselves fitted; the instantaneous temperature functions (Bernacchi kinetics) still have to be supplied.

8. Define Tcrit, explain how it is measured, and discuss why thermal safety margins are smallest at mid latitudes despite Tcrit increasing toward the tropics.

Tcrit is the leaf temperature at which the minimal chlorophyll fluorescence F0 begins to rise steeply during a controlled heating ramp (typically 1 °C min⁻¹ on dark-adapted leaf discs), signalling the onset of irreversible damage to PSII and thylakoid membranes; T50, the temperature at which Fv/Fm falls to half, is a related metric. O'Sullivan et al. (2017) measured 218 species from 19 sites and found Tcrit from about 42 °C in Arctic and cool-temperate plants to about 50 °C in hot-desert and tropical species, increasing with site temperature at roughly 0.3 °C per °C, a weak slope. The thermal safety margin is Tcrit minus the maximum leaf temperature experienced. Maximum leaf temperatures depend on air temperature extremes plus the leaf-to-air difference set by radiation load, wind and transpirational cooling; mid-latitude continental sites have very high summer extremes (heatwaves of 40 °C or more) combined with drought that closes stomata and removes cooling, while tropical sites have higher means but less extreme maxima and more humid air. Because Tcrit rises more slowly than site temperature across latitude, and extremes rise faster than means, the margin narrows toward warm and especially continental mid-latitude sites. For models the point is that peaked-Arrhenius functions decline smoothly and reversibly, whereas real leaves cross a damage threshold; current land models lack this, which matters for heatwave impacts on crops and forests.
