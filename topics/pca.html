Principal Component Analysis

Rotate a cloud of correlated measurements so that the new axes are uncorrelated and ordered by variance, then keep the first few. One theorem (eigenvectors of the covariance matrix) delivers dimension reduction, decorrelation, denoising and the biplot, and it underlies the leaf economics spectrum, EOF analysis and half of the preprocessing in ecosystem data science.

The one-paragraph version

Given \(n\) observations of \(p\) variables, centre each variable and form the \(p\times p\) covariance matrix \(S\). The first principal component is the unit vector \(w_1\) along which the projected data have the largest variance; a Lagrange-multiplier argument shows it is the eigenvector of \(S\) with the largest eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\), and that \(\lambda_1\) is that variance. Subsequent components are the remaining eigenvectors in decreasing order of eigenvalue, mutually orthogonal and uncorrelated. Equivalently (Eckart–Young), the first \(k\) components span the \(k\)-dimensional subspace that reconstructs the data with the smallest squared error, and the error equals the sum of the discarded eigenvalues. Computationally PCA is the singular value decomposition of the centred data matrix: \(\tilde X=U\Sigma V^\top\), with loadings \(V\), scores \(U\Sigma\) and eigenvalues \(\sigma_j^2/(n-1)\). Everything else is practice: standardise when units differ (otherwise the variable with the largest numbers wins), choose how many components with a scree plot, parallel analysis or cross-validation, read the biplot with the right scaling, remember that signs are arbitrary and that PCA sees only linear, variance-shaped structure. In ecosystem science PCA defines the leaf economics spectrum and the global spectrum of plant form and function, summarises climate space, decomposes spatiotemporal fields into EOFs, decorrelates drivers before regression and reduces model ensembles for emulation.

Examiner's lens

Be ready to (i) derive the first PC with a Lagrange multiplier and say what the eigenvalue means; (ii) explain the SVD connection and why you never form \(S\) for a 2000-band spectrum; (iii) argue when to use the correlation rather than the covariance matrix; (iv) interpret a biplot of leaf traits (LMA and lifespan opposite N and Amass); and (v) state the assumptions (linearity, variance = information, sensitivity to outliers and to the horseshoe effect) and name the alternatives.

Setup and the variance-maximising derivation

Let \(X\) be the \(n\times p\) data matrix (rows = sites, species or pixels; columns = variables). Centring subtracts each column mean: \(\tilde X=X-\mathbf 1\bar x^\top\). PCA is about variances and covariances, which are defined around the mean, so centring is not optional: uncentred "PCA" finds the direction of the mean, not of the variance. The sample covariance matrix is

$$S=\frac{1}{n-1}\tilde X^\top\tilde X,\qquad S_{jl}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n\tilde x_{ij}\tilde x_{il},$$

symmetric and positive semi-definite. Consider a linear combination (a projection) \(t=\tilde Xw\) with \(w\in\mathbb R^p\). Its sample variance is \(\frac{1}{n-1}t^\top t=w^\top Sw\). This grows without bound if \(w\) is lengthened, so we constrain \(\lVert w\rVert=1\) and ask for the direction of maximum variance:

$$\max_w\ w^\top Sw\quad\text{subject to}\quad w^\top w=1 .$$

Lagrange multipliers

Form \(\mathcal L(w,\lambda)=w^\top Sw-\lambda(w^\top w-1)\). Using \(\partial(w^\top Sw)/\partial w=2Sw\) (because \(S\) is symmetric) and \(\partial(w^\top w)/\partial w=2w\),

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial w}=2Sw-2\lambda w=0\;\Longrightarrow\;Sw=\lambda w .$$

So any stationary \(w\) is an eigenvector of \(S\) and \(\lambda\) its eigenvalue. Which one? Left-multiplying by \(w^\top\): \(w^\top Sw=\lambda w^\top w=\lambda\). The variance we are maximising equals the eigenvalue, so the maximum is attained at the eigenvector \(v_1\) with the largest eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\). That is the first principal component (loading vector); the projected values \(t_1=\tilde Xv_1\) are the first PC scores, with variance \(\lambda_1\).

For the second component add the constraint \(w^\top v_1=0\) (uncorrelated with, equivalently orthogonal to, the first). The Lagrangian gains a term \(-\phi\,w^\top v_1\); multiplying the stationarity condition \(2Sw-2\lambda w-\phi v_1=0\) by \(v_1^\top\) and using \(v_1^\top S=\lambda_1v_1^\top\) and \(v_1^\top w=0\) gives \(\phi=0\), so again \(Sw=\lambda w\), and the best remaining choice is the eigenvector with the second-largest eigenvalue. By induction the \(k\)-th PC is the \(k\)-th eigenvector. Because \(S\) is symmetric, the spectral theorem guarantees \(p\) real eigenvalues \(\lambda_1\ge\dots\ge\lambda_p\ge0\) and an orthonormal set of eigenvectors \(V=[v_1\dots v_p]\), \(V^\top V=I\), with \(S=V\Lambda V^\top\). The scores \(T=\tilde XV\) have covariance \(V^\top SV=\Lambda\): diagonal, so the PCs are uncorrelated, and the total variance is conserved, \(\sum_j\lambda_j=\mathrm{tr}(S)=\sum_j\mathrm{Var}(x_j)\).

The same answer from reconstruction error (Eckart–Young)

Instead of maximising variance, ask for the \(k\)-dimensional linear subspace that approximates the data best. Project each centred row onto an orthonormal basis \(W\) (\(p\times k\), \(W^\top W=I_k\)) and back: \(\hat{\tilde x}_i=WW^\top\tilde x_i\). The total squared reconstruction error is

$$E(W)=\sum_i\lVert\tilde x_i-WW^\top\tilde x_i\rVert^2=\sum_i\lVert\tilde x_i\rVert^2-\sum_i\lVert W^\top\tilde x_i\rVert^2=(n-1)\Big[\mathrm{tr}(S)-\mathrm{tr}(W^\top SW)\Big],$$

using Pythagoras (the residual is orthogonal to the projection). Minimising \(E\) is maximising \(\mathrm{tr}(W^\top SW)\), the variance captured by the subspace, whose maximum over orthonormal \(W\) is \(\lambda_1+\dots+\lambda_k\) at \(W=V_k\) (Ky Fan's inequality). Hence the minimum error is \((n-1)\sum_{j>k}\lambda_j\): the discarded eigenvalues are exactly the reconstruction error. Eckart & Young (1936) proved the matrix version: the best rank-\(k\) approximation of \(\tilde X\) in Frobenius norm is its truncated SVD. Variance-maximisation and error-minimisation are two descriptions of one subspace, which is why PCA serves both for "finding structure" and for compression.

Eigen-decomposition versus SVD

The thin SVD of the centred matrix is \(\tilde X=U\Sigma V^\top\) with \(U\) (\(n\times r\)) and \(V\) (\(p\times r\)) orthonormal and \(\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1\ge\dots\ge\sigma_r)\), \(r=\min(n-1,p)\). Then

$$S=\frac{1}{n-1}V\Sigma U^\top U\Sigma V^\top=V\,\frac{\Sigma^2}{n-1}\,V^\top ,$$
$$\lambda_j=\frac{\sigma_j^2}{n-1},\qquad\text{loadings}=V,\qquad\text{scores}=\tilde XV=U\Sigma .$$

So the right singular vectors are the loadings, the left singular vectors scaled by the singular values are the scores, and the squared singular values divided by \(n-1\) are the eigenvalues. Numerically the SVD is preferred: it never forms \(\tilde X^\top\tilde X\) (which squares the condition number), and when \(p\gg n\) (2000 spectral bands, 60 leaves) it works with the \(n\times n\) Gram matrix implicitly, giving at most \(n-1\) non-zero components. The labs on this page use the Jacobi eigenvalue algorithm on \(S\) because the matrices are small (6×6 and 50×50) and the code is transparent; for anything large use a library SVD (LAPACK, prcomp in R, sklearn.decomposition.PCA).

Variance explained and scores

The proportion of variance explained by component \(j\) is \(\lambda_j/\sum_l\lambda_l\); the cumulative proportion up to \(k\) is the fraction of total variance (and of squared Frobenius norm) retained. Scores \(t_{ij}=\tilde x_i^\top v_j\) are the coordinates of observation \(i\) on the new axes; loadings \(v_{lj}\) are the weights of original variable \(l\) in component \(j\), and \(v_{lj}\sqrt{\lambda_j}\) is the covariance (correlation, for standardised data) between variable \(l\) and component \(j\). The sign of each \(v_j\) is arbitrary (\(-v_j\) is an equally good eigenvector), so a "negative loading of LMA on PC1" is meaningful only relative to the other loadings on that axis; software picks a sign by convention and two runs can differ.

How many components, and on which matrix?

Covariance or correlation

PCA on \(S\) weights variables by their variance, so the result depends on units: leaf mass per area in g m⁻² (values 30–300) swamps leaf phosphorus in mg g⁻¹ (0.5–3), and merely re-expressing LMA in kg m⁻² changes the components. Standardising each column to unit variance (PCA on the correlation matrix \(R\)) removes the dependence on units and gives every variable the same prior weight; the total variance is then \(p\) and \(\lambda_j>1\) means "more than one variable's worth". Use the correlation matrix whenever variables are in different units or of very different magnitude, which is nearly always for trait or environmental data; use the covariance matrix when the variables share a unit and their relative variance is meaningful, as for the bands of a reflectance spectrum or the grid cells of an NDVI field (where standardising would amplify noisy, low-variance pixels). Log-transform positively skewed variables (most traits, precipitation) before either choice, since PCA is a linear, second-moment method and a few extreme values otherwise define the first axis.

Stopping rules

The scree plot shows \(\lambda_j\) against \(j\); keep components before the curve flattens into the "scree" of noise eigenvalues. Formal rules for correlation PCA:

  • Kaiser–Guttman: keep \(\lambda_j>1\). Simple, widely used, and known to over-retain for large \(p\) and under-retain for small (Jackson 1993).
  • Broken stick: compare \(\lambda_j/\sum\lambda\) with the expected length of the \(j\)-th longest piece of a unit stick broken at random into \(p\) pieces, \(b_j=\frac1p\sum_{i=j}^{p}\frac1i\); keep components exceeding it. Performs well in Jackson's ecological simulations.
  • Parallel analysis (Horn 1965): generate many random datasets of the same \(n\times p\) (independent normals), compute their eigenvalues, and keep observed \(\lambda_j\) that exceed the 95th percentile of the random \(\lambda_j\). This corrects Kaiser's rule for the fact that even noise produces a first eigenvalue above 1 by sampling variation. It is the recommended default (Lab D).
  • Cross-validation: leave out rows (or cells, in the eigenvector cross-validation of Wold), reconstruct them from \(k\) components fitted to the rest, and choose the \(k\) minimising prediction error (PRESS). This is the only rule that measures what reconstruction or prediction actually needs, and the one used in chemometrics/PLS.
  • Proportion thresholds ("keep 90% of variance") depend on noise level and \(p\); they are conventions, not inference.

For EOF analysis North et al. (1982) add a sampling-error rule: eigenvalues closer than \(\delta\lambda_j\approx\lambda_j\sqrt{2/N^*}\) (\(N^*\) effective independent samples) are degenerate and their eigenvectors are arbitrary mixtures, so only well-separated leading modes are interpretable.

Reading and using the results

Biplots and their scalings (Gabriel 1971)

A biplot draws observations and variables on the same pair of axes using the rank-2 factorisation \(\tilde X\approx GH^\top\) with \(G=U_2\Sigma_2^{\alpha}\) (rows) and \(H=V_2\Sigma_2^{1-\alpha}\) (columns). Two conventions matter:

  • Distance biplot (\(\alpha=1\); R's prcomp default, Legendre's "scaling 1"): rows are the PC scores \(U\Sigma\), so distances between points approximate Euclidean distances between observations; variable arrows are the unit loadings \(V\). Arrow length shows how much of a variable lies in the plane; angles between arrows are not correlations.
  • Correlation biplot (\(\alpha=0\); "scaling 2"): columns are \(V\Sigma\) (for standardised data, the correlations of variables with the components), so the cosine of the angle between two arrows approximates their correlation and arrow length their standard deviation in the plane; row points are \(U\), and distances between them are no longer Euclidean. Use this one to read the trait structure (LMA against N), and the distance biplot to compare sites or species.

In either, the projection of a point onto an arrow approximates that observation's (centred) value of the variable, and a sign flip of a PC flips both the points and the arrows along that axis, leaving the picture mirror-imaged but unchanged in meaning.

Rotation, and PCA versus factor analysis

Any orthogonal rotation of the retained loadings, \(V_kR\), spans the same subspace and reconstructs equally well; PCA's choice (maximal variance per axis, in order) is one convention. Varimax chooses \(R\) to maximise the variance of the squared loadings in each column, driving each variable's loading toward 0 or ±1 so that components become "simple" (each variable loads mainly on one axis). The rotated axes are no longer ordered by variance and no longer uncorrelated in the original sense, which trades mathematical optimality for interpretability. Factor analysis is a different model: \(x=\mu+\Lambda f+\varepsilon\) with latent factors \(f\) and variable-specific noise \(\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\Psi)\), \(\Psi\) diagonal, so it explains covariances between variables (common factors) and treats each variable's unique variance separately, estimated by maximum likelihood. PCA explains total variance and has no error model; it is a descriptive rotation, not a latent-variable model. Probabilistic PCA (Tipping & Bishop 1999) sits between: \(x=Wz+\mu+\varepsilon\) with isotropic noise \(\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2I)\), whose maximum-likelihood \(W\) is \(V_k(\Lambda_k-\sigma^2I)^{1/2}R\) and \(\sigma^2\) is the mean of the discarded eigenvalues. It gives PCA a likelihood (so BIC, missing values via EM, and mixtures of PCAs) and shows that ordinary PCA is its \(\sigma^2\to0\) limit.

Outliers and robust PCA

Variance is a second moment, so one extreme site can become PC1 by itself (turn off the log transform in Lab B). Remedies: transform, inspect score and orthogonal-distance plots (the diagnostic plot of Hubert et al.), and if needed use a robust covariance (minimum covariance determinant), projection-pursuit PCA, or the "robust PCA" decomposition of the data into low-rank plus sparse parts (Candès et al.), which is also how gross errors in remote-sensing time series are separated from the smooth seasonal signal.

Compression and denoising

Keeping \(k\) components stores \(nk+pk\) numbers instead of \(np\), and the reconstruction \(\hat X=\bar x+T_kV_k^\top\) discards the low-variance directions, where isotropic noise lives. For a hyperspectral cube this is the minimum-noise-fraction idea; for an ensemble of model outputs it is the basis of emulation. Regression on components, ordination, and nonlinear relatives

Principal component regression and PLS

When predictors are many and collinear (2000 reflectance bands predicting leaf nitrogen from 100 leaves), ordinary least squares is undefined or wildly unstable. Principal component regression (PCR) regresses \(y\) on the first \(k\) PC scores of \(X\); it stabilises the fit but chooses directions by the variance of \(X\) alone, which may be irrelevant to \(y\). Partial least squares (PLS; Wold, Sjöström & Eriksson 2001) instead finds directions that maximise the covariance between \(Xw\) and \(y\),

$$w_1=\arg\max_{\lVert w\rVert=1}\ \mathrm{Cov}(\tilde Xw,\ y)=\arg\max\ w^\top\tilde X^\top y\;\Longrightarrow\;w_1\propto\tilde X^\top y ,$$

then deflates \(X\) and repeats, with \(k\) chosen by cross-validation. PLS is the standard for spectroscopic trait prediction: Serbin et al. (2014) predicted Vcmax, Jmax, LMA and N from leaf reflectance with PLS regression, and the same approach now feeds trait maps into land models. PCA/PCR, PLS and ridge regression are all shrinkage methods that differ in which directions of \(X\) they trust.

The horseshoe effect and why ecological ordination has other methods

Species abundances along an environmental gradient respond unimodally: each species peaks somewhere and is absent far away. Sites far apart on the gradient share no species, so their dissimilarity saturates, and PCA, which models linear responses and Euclidean distance, bends a one-dimensional gradient into an arch or horseshoe in the PC1–PC2 plane, with the ends curling inward so that the most dissimilar sites appear closer than intermediate ones. Correspondence analysis (CA) assumes unimodal responses and reduces but does not remove the arch; detrended CA (DCA) flattens it by brute force (ter Braak & Prentice 1988); non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) on an ecological dissimilarity such as Bray–Curtis only preserves rank order of distances and is the usual choice for community data (Legendre & Legendre 2012). PCA remains right for environmental and trait variables, which respond roughly linearly to one another, and is wrong for abundance matrices with long gradients.

Assumptions of PCA

  • Linearity: structure is captured by linear combinations; curved manifolds are spread across many components.
  • Variance equals information: the interesting directions are the high-variance ones. Untrue when a small-variance variable is the one that matters, or when variance is dominated by measurement noise in one instrument.
  • Second moments suffice (Gaussian-like data): PCA uses only the covariance matrix, so it is blind to higher-order structure and is the maximum-likelihood answer only for Gaussian data (PPCA). Independent component analysis uses higher moments instead.
  • Orthogonality and sensitivity: components must be orthogonal even when the generating processes are not, and loadings are unstable when eigenvalues are close (North's rule).
  • Rows are exchangeable and outlier-free, and variables have been scaled in a way that reflects their intended weight.

Kernel PCA, t-SNE and UMAP

Kernel PCA (Schölkopf, Smola & Müller 1998) performs linear PCA in an implicit feature space defined by a kernel \(k(x,x')\): centre the \(n\times n\) kernel matrix and take its eigenvectors; projections of new points are kernel sums. With a Gaussian kernel it finds curved structure (rings, moons) that linear PCA misses, at the cost of no explicit loadings and a bandwidth to choose. t-SNE (van der Maaten & Hinton 2008) and UMAP (McInnes, Healy & Melville 2018) are nonlinear embeddings for visualisation that preserve neighbourhoods rather than variance or distances: they are excellent for seeing clusters in high-dimensional data (pixels, spectra, sequencing) but their axes have no meaning, distances between clusters and cluster sizes are not interpretable, results depend on perplexity/neighbour settings and random seeds, and they can manufacture apparent clusters from continua. Use them to look, PCA to measure, and report PCA (or a model) for any quantitative claim; running PCA first to 20–50 dimensions is also the standard preprocessing for both.

PCA in ecosystem science

The leaf economics spectrum. Wright et al. (2004) assembled six traits for 2548 species (LMA, leaf lifespan, Nmass, Pmass, Amass, Rmass); after log-transformation, PCA of the correlation matrix put 74% of the variance on a single axis running from "fast" leaves (low LMA, short lifespan, high N, Amass, Rmass) to "slow" leaves (the opposite). That one axis is the LES, and its existence is why land models can parameterise photosynthetic capacity from LMA or leaf N. Díaz et al. (2016) extended this to six whole-plant traits (plant height, stem specific density, leaf area, LMA, leaf N, seed mass) across 46,085 species: two PCs explain 74%, a size axis (height, seed mass, leaf area) and the LES axis, and most of the trait hypervolume is empty, which constrains the functional diversity that models need to represent.

Climate space. Bioclimatic variables (the 19 WorldClim variables, radiation, aridity) are heavily redundant; PCA to 2–4 components yields orthogonal climate axes (energy, water, seasonality) used to define ecoregions, to stratify sampling networks (Metzger et al.'s global stratification clusters PC scores), and to quantify how representative flux-tower networks are of the climate space a model must cover.

EOF analysis of spatiotemporal fields. Arrange a field (NDVI, soil moisture, GPP) as a matrix with time in rows and grid cells in columns and run covariance PCA: the loadings \(v_j\) are spatial patterns, the empirical orthogonal functions, and the scores \(t_j\) are their time series (principal components). The first EOF of NDVI is usually the seasonal cycle unless it is removed first; the next ones capture ENSO-like or drought anomalies. Hannachi, Jolliffe & Stephenson (2007) review the method with its pitfalls: EOFs are orthogonal by construction and need not be physical modes, nearby eigenvalues give unstable patterns, area weighting (multiply by \(\sqrt{\cos\phi}\)) is needed on a latitude–longitude grid, and rotated EOFs (varimax) are often more interpretable regionally.

Decorrelating drivers. Flux-tower analyses regress NEE or GPP on radiation, temperature, VPD and soil moisture, which are strongly collinear (VPD rises with temperature). PCA of the drivers gives uncorrelated composite predictors so that variance can be attributed without inflation, at the cost of interpreting mixtures; PLS or ridge are alternatives when prediction matters more than attribution.

Emulation and sensitivity analysis. A land model run over an ensemble of parameter sets produces outputs of thousands of time steps or grid cells each; PCA of the output ensemble (rows = runs) gives a few score vectors per run, each of which can be emulated with a Gaussian process as a function of the parameters. Global sensitivity analysis is then done on the emulated scores, and reconstruction through the loadings returns full fields. The same reduced-order idea underlies PCA-based sampling of initial conditions and the compression of observations in data assimilation.

Whitening before clustering. Dividing PC scores by \(\sqrt{\lambda_j}\) gives unit-variance, uncorrelated coordinates in which Euclidean distance equals Mahalanobis distance, so k-means and GMMs treat all directions equally and converge faster; it also removes the duplication that redundant variables would otherwise contribute to the distance.

Connections to ecosystem modelling

  • Trait-based parameterisation. Because the LES is one axis, models (and trait databases such as TRY) can predict Vcmax from leaf N or LMA, and trait-based land models can represent a continuum of strategies as positions on one or two PC axes rather than as discrete PFTs.
  • Reduced-order model analysis. PCA/EOF of model output is how ensembles of CLM, JULES or ORCHIDEE runs are compared with each other and with observations (e.g. comparing leading EOFs of simulated and observed soil moisture or NDVI) and how emulators for calibration and sensitivity analysis are built.
  • Data assimilation. Ensemble covariances are rank-deficient; their leading eigenvectors (a PCA of the ensemble perturbations) are the directions in which observations can update the state, and truncating them is a form of localisation/regularisation. Observation operators for hyperspectral data compress radiances onto leading PCs before assimilation.
  • Remote-sensing inputs. Trait maps that drive models come from PLS on reflectance; land-cover and phenology products use PCA of multi-date imagery as a preprocessing step before classification.
  • Diagnosing model behaviour. PCA of a model's parameter posterior (from MCMC) shows which parameter combinations are constrained (large eigenvalues of the precision matrix) and which lie along unidentifiable ridges (equifinality), guiding which parameters to fix.
  • Honest caveats. PCA axes are statistical, not mechanistic; a leading EOF is not necessarily a process, a PC of drivers mixes causes, and reduced-order emulators can only reproduce variability that was in the training ensemble.

Exam-style questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer.

1. Derive the first principal component as the solution of a constrained maximisation and interpret the Lagrange multiplier.

Centre the data and let \(S=\tilde X^\top\tilde X/(n-1)\). The projection \(t=\tilde Xw\) has variance \(w^\top Sw\). Maximise it subject to \(w^\top w=1\) with \(\mathcal L=w^\top Sw-\lambda(w^\top w-1)\). Because \(S\) is symmetric, \(\partial(w^\top Sw)/\partial w=2Sw\); so \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial w=2Sw-2\lambda w=0\Rightarrow Sw=\lambda w\): \(w\) must be an eigenvector and \(\lambda\) the corresponding eigenvalue. Pre-multiplying by \(w^\top\) gives \(w^\top Sw=\lambda\), i.e. the Lagrange multiplier is the variance of the projected data, so the constrained maximum is the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue, \(v_1\), and the maximal variance is \(\lambda_1\). Geometrically the constraint surface is the unit sphere; the gradient of the objective (\(2Sw\)) must be parallel to the gradient of the constraint (\(2w\)) at the optimum, which is exactly the eigenvector condition. Subsequent components add orthogonality constraints whose multipliers vanish, giving the remaining eigenvectors in order; the total variance \(\mathrm{tr}(S)=\sum\lambda_j\) is conserved and the scores are uncorrelated because \(V^\top SV=\Lambda\).

2. Show that the subspace minimising reconstruction error coincides with the variance-maximising subspace, and give the value of the minimum error.

Let \(W\) be \(p\times k\) with orthonormal columns and project \(\tilde x_i\mapsto WW^\top\tilde x_i\). The residual \(\tilde x_i-WW^\top\tilde x_i\) is orthogonal to the column space of \(W\), so by Pythagoras \(\lVert\tilde x_i\rVert^2=\lVert W^\top\tilde x_i\rVert^2+\lVert\tilde x_i-WW^\top\tilde x_i\rVert^2\). Summing over \(i\): \(E(W)=\sum_i\lVert\tilde x_i\rVert^2-\sum_i\lVert W^\top\tilde x_i\rVert^2=(n-1)[\mathrm{tr}(S)-\mathrm{tr}(W^\top SW)]\). The first term is fixed, so minimising \(E\) is maximising \(\mathrm{tr}(W^\top SW)=\sum_{j=1}^k w_j^\top Sw_j\), the total variance captured by the \(k\) directions. For orthonormal \(w_j\) this is at most \(\lambda_1+\dots+\lambda_k\) (Ky Fan: each \(w_j^\top Sw_j\) is a convex combination of eigenvalues, and orthonormality prevents all \(k\) from using \(\lambda_1\)), with equality at \(W=V_k\). Hence \(E_{\min}=(n-1)\sum_{j>k}\lambda_j\): the reconstruction error is the sum of the discarded eigenvalues (times \(n-1\)), and the mean squared error per entry is \(\sum_{j>k}\lambda_j\cdot(n-1)/(np)\). In matrix form this is Eckart–Young: the truncated SVD \(U_k\Sigma_kV_k^\top\) is the best rank-\(k\) approximation in Frobenius norm, with squared error \(\sum_{j>k}\sigma_j^2\).

3. Explain the relationship between PCA and the SVD. Why would you use the SVD rather than the eigen-decomposition of the covariance matrix for a dataset of 80 leaves × 2100 reflectance bands?

With \(\tilde X=U\Sigma V^\top\) (thin SVD, \(U^\top U=V^\top V=I\)), \(S=\tilde X^\top\tilde X/(n-1)=V\Sigma^2V^\top/(n-1)\), which is the eigen-decomposition of \(S\): eigenvectors (loadings) are the right singular vectors \(V\), eigenvalues are \(\lambda_j=\sigma_j^2/(n-1)\), and scores \(\tilde XV=U\Sigma\) are the left singular vectors scaled by the singular values. For 80 × 2100 data, \(S\) would be 2100 × 2100 (4.4 million entries) but of rank at most 79, so forming and diagonalising it is wasteful and ill-conditioned (squaring the condition number). The SVD works directly on the 80 × 2100 matrix, or equivalently on the 80 × 80 Gram matrix \(\tilde X\tilde X^\top=U\Sigma^2U^\top\), returning the 79 non-zero components at a cost of order \(n^2p\) rather than \(p^3\); loadings are then recovered as \(V=\tilde X^\top U\Sigma^{-1}\). This "kernel trick" for PCA (eigenvectors of \(\tilde X\tilde X^\top\)) is also the starting point of kernel PCA. Numerically stable SVD routines (Golub–Kahan) avoid cancellation in \(\tilde X^\top\tilde X\) and are what prcomp and scikit-learn use; princomp in R diagonalises the covariance matrix and fails when \(p>n\).

4. You have LMA (g m⁻²), leaf N (mg g⁻¹), Amass (nmol g⁻¹ s⁻¹), leaf lifespan (months), Rd and P for 300 species. Describe how you would run and interpret a PCA, and what you expect to find.

Log-transform all six (they are right-skewed and vary over one to two orders of magnitude, and the LES relationships are log-linear), check for outliers and missing values (impute or use PPCA/EM), then run PCA on the correlation matrix because the units differ. Examine the scree plot with parallel analysis and the broken stick; expect one dominant eigenvalue (around 4 of 6, ~70% of variance) and perhaps a second near the noise. Inspect the loadings of PC1: LMA and lifespan with one sign, N, Amass, Rd and P with the opposite sign (the sign itself is arbitrary); this is the fast–slow leaf economics axis. Draw a correlation biplot (loadings VΣ, so arrow angles approximate correlations): LMA and lifespan arrows should be nearly parallel and opposite to Amass and N; P may deviate (loading on a second, nutrient axis, as in Wright et al. 2004 where P varied partly independently with soil P). Report eigenvalues, PVE, loadings with bootstrapped stability, and the PC1 score per species as its position on the spectrum. Caveats to state: PCA describes correlations across species, not within-species plasticity; phylogenetic non-independence inflates apparent sample size; the axis is a statistical summary of a trade-off (construction cost vs return time), and model use (predicting Vcmax from LMA) is an application of the correlation, not a causal mechanism.

5. Compare the Kaiser rule, the broken-stick model, parallel analysis and cross-validation for choosing the number of components. Which would you recommend for a 40 × 6 trait matrix and why?

Kaiser (\(\lambda>1\) on the correlation matrix) says "keep components explaining more than one variable's worth"; it ignores sampling variability, so with small \(n\) and many variables the first noise eigenvalue easily exceeds 1 and it over-retains, while with few variables it can under-retain. Broken stick compares each \(\lambda_j/\sum\lambda\) with the expected size of the \(j\)-th largest random fragment; it has no sampling-distribution basis either but is conservative and did well in Jackson's (1993) simulations. Parallel analysis simulates the actual null (independent variables, same \(n\) and \(p\)) and keeps components whose eigenvalue beats the 95th percentile of the simulated \(\lambda_j\); it adapts to \(n\) and \(p\) and is the best general-purpose rule. Cross-validation (leave out rows or cells, reconstruct from \(k\) components, minimise PRESS) answers a different question, how many components predict held-out data, which is the right criterion when PCA is a preprocessing step for regression or compression; it can be noisy for small \(n\). For 40 × 6 trait data: parallel analysis as the primary rule (small \(n\) makes sampling variation large, exactly what it corrects), broken stick as a check, and interpretability as the final arbiter (a component with a coherent loading pattern such as the LES is kept; one that loads on a single variable is probably noise). Kaiser alone would be the weakest choice here.

6. What is the horseshoe (arch) effect, why does PCA produce it for community data, and what should be used instead?

Along a long environmental gradient each species has a unimodal response: present and abundant near its optimum, absent far away. Sites at opposite ends of the gradient therefore share no species, and their Euclidean distance (or 1 − correlation) is the same as that between a site and any other site beyond the species turnover distance: dissimilarity saturates. PCA assumes linear responses and preserves Euclidean distances, so it cannot place the sites along a line; the only way to keep the saturated distances approximately correct in two dimensions is to bend the gradient into an arch, with PC2 a quadratic function of PC1 and, when turnover is complete, the ends curling inward (the horseshoe), so that the most different sites look similar. Correspondence analysis uses chi-square distance and a unimodal model, which reduces the arch but leaves one (the "arch effect"); detrended CA removes it by segment-wise detrending and rescaling (ter Braak & Prentice 1988), at the cost of ad hoc steps. Non-metric multidimensional scaling on Bray–Curtis or Jaccard dissimilarities (which are bounded and ecologically meaningful) preserves only the rank order of dissimilarities and recovers the gradient without the artefact; it is the usual recommendation (Legendre & Legendre). Constrained methods (RDA for linear, CCA for unimodal responses) relate the ordination to measured environmental variables. PCA stays appropriate for environmental and trait matrices, where responses between variables are approximately linear and Euclidean distance is sensible.

7. Explain EOF analysis of a soil-moisture field (10 years of monthly maps on 500 grid cells) as a PCA, including the preprocessing and the interpretation pitfalls.

Build the matrix with time (120 months) as rows and grid cells (500) as columns. Preprocess: remove the climatological seasonal cycle per cell (otherwise EOF1 is the annual cycle), optionally detrend, and weight columns by \(\sqrt{\cos\phi}\) for equal-area contributions on a lat–lon grid; use the covariance (not correlation) matrix if the field's units are shared and you want wet, variable regions to dominate, or the correlation matrix to emphasise pattern over amplitude. Compute the SVD of the 120 × 500 centred matrix (never the 500 × 500 covariance when \(n=120\)): right singular vectors are the EOFs (spatial patterns, one value per cell), left singular vectors times singular values are the PC time series, \(\sigma_j^2/(n-1)\) the explained variance. Interpret leading EOFs by their patterns (a dipole between regions, a monopole) and their PCs by correlating with climate indices or driver fields. Pitfalls: EOFs are mutually orthogonal and PCs uncorrelated by construction, so they need not correspond to physical modes (mixing of modes, domain-shape dependence: Buell patterns); eigenvalues separated by less than North's sampling error \(\lambda\sqrt{2/N^*}\) (with \(N^*\) the number of effectively independent months, far fewer than 120 given autocorrelation) are degenerate and their patterns arbitrary; rotated EOFs can localise patterns; and the sign of each EOF/PC pair is arbitrary. Report the fraction of variance, the North error bars, and compare observed and simulated leading EOFs to evaluate a model's variability, not just its mean.

8. A colleague proposes t-SNE to "find the plant functional types" in a trait database with 20 traits and 5000 species. Advise.

t-SNE is a visualisation tool, not an analysis: it optimises a neighbourhood-preserving embedding in which axes have no meaning, inter-cluster distances and cluster sizes are not interpretable, the layout depends on perplexity and the random seed, and continua are routinely broken into apparent clusters by the method itself. Finding "functional types" with it risks discovering artefacts. Recommended workflow: log-transform and standardise the traits; run PCA (correlation matrix) and examine the scree with parallel analysis; expect a few interpretable axes (size, leaf economics, hydraulics) explaining most of the variance, as in Díaz et al. (2016). Test for discrete structure with methods that have a model and a selection criterion: Gaussian mixtures with BIC/ICL on the leading PC scores (ICL will say whether well-separated clusters exist or whether the data form a continuum), k-means with gap statistic and bootstrap stability, or hierarchical clustering with a dendrogram. If t-SNE or UMAP is used, run it on the first 10–20 PCs, several seeds and perplexities, colour points by the PCA-based cluster labels and by key traits, and treat it as a picture of local neighbourhoods only. The honest conclusion for most trait data is a continuum with soft modes, which argues for trait-based (continuous) parameterisation in land models rather than new discrete PFTs.
