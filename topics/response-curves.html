Topic 5 of the SPAC section

A–Ci, A–Q and A–T Response Curves

How a leaf's CO₂, light and temperature responses are measured with a gas-exchange system, what their shapes mean, which parameters each curve can and cannot give, and how to fit and compare them without fooling yourself.

The one-paragraph version

A response curve holds every environmental driver constant except one and records net assimilation A (and stomatal conductance gs) as that driver is stepped. The A–Ci curve varies CO₂ at saturating light and fixed temperature; its initial slope gives Vcmax, its plateau gives J (hence Jmax), a flat or declining tail gives Tp, the intercept region gives Rd and Γ, and with fluorescence or several irradiances it also gives gm and Γ*. The A–Q curve varies irradiance at fixed CO₂; its initial slope is the apparent quantum yield φ, its x-intercept the light compensation point, its asymptote Asat, its curvature θ, and its low-light break (the Kok effect) a route to Rd in the light. The A–T curve varies leaf temperature at fixed CO₂ and light; its peak gives Topt and Aopt, its width the thermal breadth, and the shift of Topt with CO₂ or growth temperature tells you which biochemical limitation is in control. All three are produced by the same open-path differential infrared gas analyser (IRGA) and interpreted through the same FvCB model, so the previous page is the theory and this page is the measurement.

Examiner's lens

Expect questions of the form "here is a curve, what is wrong with it", "which parameter would this treatment change and why", "write the protocol", and "how would you test whether Vcmax differs between treatments". They test whether you understand that a parameter is only as good as the measurement that produced it: steady state, leaks, matching, Ci versus Cc, and the assignment of points to limitation states.

How an open gas-exchange system measures A and gs

Modern portable systems (LI-6800, LI-6400, CIRAS, GFS-3000) are open, differential systems. Conditioned air of known CO₂ and H₂O mole fraction flows at a measured rate (typically 300–600 µmol s⁻¹) through a chamber that clamps a known leaf area (2–6 cm²; 0.5–1 cm² for conifer or grass chambers); a reference IRGA measures the incoming air and a sample IRGA the outgoing air, and the differences ΔCO₂ and ΔH₂O give the leaf fluxes. The chamber controls light (red/blue or red/blue/far-red LEDs), leaf temperature (Peltier-cooled block, measured by a thermocouple or energy balance), CO₂ (injector from a cartridge, control on reference or sample side) and humidity (desiccant/humidifier columns, or a controlled-humidity module), and a fan keeps the boundary-layer conductance high and known. From von Caemmerer & Farquhar 1981 [1], with F the molar flow (mol s⁻¹), s the leaf area and W, C the water and CO₂ mole fractions entering (r) and leaving (s):

$$E = \frac{F\,(W_s - W_r)}{s\,(1 - W_s)}, \qquad A = \frac{F\,(C_r - C_s)}{s} - E\,C_s$$
$$g_{tw} = \frac{E\,\left(1 - \dfrac{W_l + W_s}{2}\right)}{W_l - W_s}, \qquad \frac{1}{g_{sw}} = \frac{1}{g_{tw}} - \frac{1}{g_{bw}}, \qquad C_i = \frac{\left(g_{tc} - \dfrac{E}{2}\right)C_s - A}{g_{tc} + \dfrac{E}{2}}$$

Wl is the saturation mole fraction at leaf temperature (the leaf interior is assumed saturated); gtc = 1/(1.6/gsw + 1.37/gbw); the E terms correct for the mass flow of water vapour out of the stomata (the "ternary" correction) and the E·Cs term for dilution of CO₂ by transpired water. Everything downstream rests on three practical steps:

  • Matching. The two IRGAs drift relative to each other by a few tenths of a µmol mol⁻¹. Before each point (or at set intervals) the sample IRGA is switched onto the reference air stream and any offset is zeroed; a 0.3 µmol mol⁻¹ error in ΔCO₂ at a flow of 500 µmol s⁻¹ over 6 cm² is 0.25 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ in A, which matters at low A and low Ci.
  • Steady state. After each step, wait until A and gs (and ΔCO₂, ΔH₂O) stop trending: typical stability criteria are slopes of A below ~1 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ per minute and of gsw below ~0.05 mol m⁻² s⁻¹ per minute over a 15–30 s window, with a minimum wait of 1–2 min and a maximum of 3–5 min per CO₂ step. Light steps take longer (2–5 min down, 10–30 min up); temperature steps 10–20 min.
  • Leak correction. CO₂ diffuses through the chamber gaskets whenever chamber CO₂ differs from the surrounding air, in or out. The error scales with the concentration difference and with the gasket perimeter relative to leaf area, so it is largest at Ca = 50 and 1500 µmol mol⁻¹ and for small leaves. Quantify it with an empty chamber or a dead (boiled or photosynthetically inert) leaf across the same CO₂ range and subtract; or sleeve the chamber in a bag flushed with reference air. The LI-6800 applies a built-in correction based on the measured chamber-ambient difference.

Always record leaf temperature, VPD and gs with every point, and keep VPD below ~1.5 kPa so that stomata do not close during the curve; A–Ci interpretation assumes Ci is what you think it is.

The A–Ci curve

Protocol

Acclimate the leaf at ambient CO₂ (400–420 µmol mol⁻¹), saturating light (1,500–2,000 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for sun leaves; use the A–Q curve to choose), 25 °C and VPD ≈ 1 kPa until A and gs are steady (15–40 min). Then step reference CO₂ down first, then return to ambient, then step up:

420 → 300 → 200 → 150 → 100 → 50 → (420, check) → 500 → 600 → 800 → 1000 → 1200 → 1500 → 2000 µmol mol⁻¹

Going down first keeps stomata open (low CO₂ opens them) and keeps Rubisco activated; high CO₂ closes stomata and can deactivate Rubisco, so if you start high the low-CO₂ points are compromised. The return to ambient tests for drift: if A has fallen by more than ~10% the leaf has changed and the curve should be discarded. Twelve to fifteen points, four or more below 250 µbar Ci, and at least one at or above 1,500 µmol mol⁻¹, take 30–45 min. RACiR (rapid A–Ci response; Stinziano et al. 2017 [4]) instead ramps CO₂ continuously, say 20 → 1,500 µmol mol⁻¹ over 5–10 min, exploiting the LI-6800's fast control; the system is not in equilibrium, so the raw ΔCO₂ lags, and an empty-chamber ramp at the same rate is fitted with a polynomial and subtracted. RACiR gives several hundred points per leaf in a few minutes and matches steady-state Vcmax and J to within a few percent, but it cannot resolve TPU reliably, needs the same ramp for leaf and calibration, and is sensitive to the match offset.

Reading the shape and extracting parameters

Plot A against Ci (not Ca: Ci is what the biochemistry sees, and plotting against Ca folds gs into the curve). At Ci = 0 the leaf releases Rd plus photorespired CO₂; the curve crosses zero at Γ (40–60 µbar in air at 25 °C), rises steeply and almost linearly while Rubisco limits, bends between 250 and 450 µbar as RuBP regeneration takes over, and flattens. From the FvCB equations on the previous page:

  • Vcmax from the Rubisco-limited region, with Kc, Ko, Γ* fixed at literature values for the same basis (Ci or Cc) and temperature. The slope at Γ, dA/dC ≈ Vcmax/(Γ* + Km), is a quick check: 0.1–0.15 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ µbar⁻¹ for Vcmax ≈ 80–110.
  • J from the RuBP-limited plateau; it becomes Jmax only if the light was saturating. If the curve was measured at 1,000 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ on a sun leaf, J is not Jmax.
  • Tp only from a plateau that is flatter than Aj predicts, or that declines, at Ci > 800–1,000 µbar; otherwise it is unidentified and should be reported as "not limiting".
  • Rd is weakly constrained by the intercept region and strongly correlated with Vcmax; fix it from an independent measurement where possible.
  • Γ directly from the zero crossing; Γ* (strictly Ci*) from the Laisk intersection of curves at several low irradiances (Lab D), or from the literature.
  • gm from combined gas exchange and chlorophyll fluorescence: ΦPSII × Q × αabs × 0.5 gives Jf; solving Aj = Jf(Cc − Γ*)/(4Cc + 8Γ*) − Rd for Cc and then gm = A/(Ci − Cc) is the variable J method (Harley et al. 1992); calibrating Jf against gas-exchange J under non-photorespiratory conditions (2% O₂, high CO₂) is essential. Isotopic (¹³C discrimination) and curve-fitting (Ethier & Livingston 2004) methods are the alternatives; Pons et al. 2009 [13] compare them.

Most literature Vcmax values are Ci-based (gm assumed infinite) and were fitted with the Bernacchi 2001 constants; Cc-based values use Bernacchi 2002 constants and are 20–50% higher. Both are legitimate; mixing them in one analysis, or feeding a Cc-based Vcmax to a model that sets Cc = Ci, is not.

The A–Q (light response) curve

Protocol

Hold CO₂ at ambient (or at 1,000–1,500 µmol mol⁻¹ if you want the biochemical light response without stomatal interference), 25 °C and VPD ≈ 1 kPa. Induce the leaf at high light until steady (20–40 min: Rubisco activation, stomatal opening and the build-up of Calvin-cycle intermediates all take time), then step light down:

2000 → 1500 → 1000 → 750 → 500 → 300 → 200 → 120 → 80 → 50 → 30 → 15 → 0 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹

Descending steps are fast (2–4 min each) because deactivation lags matter less than activation lags; an ascending curve without full induction at each step underestimates A at every point and produces a spurious low θ. Dense sampling below 100 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ is needed for φ and for the Kok break; dense sampling above 1,000 is needed for Asat. Record Ci at every step: it rises as light falls (A drops faster than gs), which matters for the Kok method.

Shape and parameters

The curve is usually fitted with a non-rectangular hyperbola in gross terms:

$$A = \frac{\varphi Q + A_{sat} - \sqrt{(\varphi Q + A_{sat})^2 - 4\theta\,\varphi Q A_{sat}}}{2\theta} - R_d$$
  • φ, apparent quantum yield (mol CO₂ mol⁻¹ incident photon): the initial slope. In air at 25 °C it is ≈ 0.05–0.06 on an incident basis (0.06–0.07 absorbed); at 2% O₂ it rises to ≈ 0.08–0.09 absorbed, approaching the theoretical 1/8 to 1/10. φ is therefore a sensitive probe of photorespiration and of photodamage; it is also what the FvCB αabs(1 − f)/2 term and Γ* jointly predict.
  • Light compensation point: where A = 0, typically 10–40 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for sun leaves and 2–10 for shade leaves; from the hyperbola, QLCP = Rd(Asat − θRd)/(φ(Asat − Rd)) ≈ Rd/φ.
  • θ, curvature: 0.7–0.9 for healthy leaves. Ögren & Evans 1993 [11] showed it is not a fixed property: it reflects the gradient of photosynthetic capacity through the leaf relative to the light gradient, falling when the light is strongly absorbed near the surface (blue light, high-chlorophyll leaves) and when CO₂ is low.
  • Asat: the light-saturated rate, which at ambient CO₂ is usually Rubisco-limited (so Asat tracks Vcmax) and at high CO₂ is RuBP- or TPU-limited (so it tracks Jmax).
  • Rd in the light (RL): the intercept of the linear region above the Kok break, extrapolated to Q = 0 (the Kok method, Kok 1948 [9]). The break arises because respiration is inhibited by light, so the slope below 20–40 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ (where A is close to −Rdark) is steeper than above. The intercept must be corrected for the change in Ci along the low-light points (Kirschbaum & Farquhar 1987) or measured at constant Ci. The Yin method [10] removes the photorespiratory confounding by plotting A against ¼ΦPSIIQabs (fluorescence-based electron transport) under non-photorespiratory conditions; the intercept is −RL, and RL is typically 50–70% of Rdark.

The A–T (temperature response) curve

Protocol

Hold CO₂ and light constant (ambient 400–420 and saturating light for the "operational" response; 2% O₂ or high CO₂ if you want the J or Rubisco response in isolation) and step the block temperature in 4–5 °C increments from ~10 to ~45 °C, allowing 10–20 min at each step for leaf temperature, gs and the biochemistry to settle. The hard part is humidity: saturation vapour pressure doubles every ~10 °C, so at constant absolute humidity the VPD at 40 °C is enormous and stomata close; the chamber humidifier must raise the dew point as temperature rises, and even so gs and Ci must be reported with every point because part of the apparent decline of A above the optimum is stomatal, not biochemical. Measure ascending, then descend through a few points to test for hysteresis: if A on the way down is lower than on the way up at the same temperature, the leaf was damaged (Rubisco activase, PSII) at the highest step and those points describe injury, not a response. Condensation inside the chamber below the dew point ruins the water channel and hence Ci.

Shape and parametric forms

A rises, peaks at Topt (typically 20–30 °C at ambient CO₂ for temperate C3 leaves, 30–38 °C for tropical C3 and C4 species) and falls. The rise is the Arrhenius increase of Vcmax and Jmax; the peak and fall are the combination of rising Γ* and Km (more photorespiration, more substrate limitation), the peaked functions of Vcmax and Jmax, rising Rd, and stomatal closure. Two empirical forms are common. June, Evans & Farquhar 2004 [12] fitted the temperature response of J with a Gaussian-type function,

$$A(T) = A_{opt}\,\exp\!\left[-\left(\frac{T - T_{opt}}{\Omega}\right)^2\right]$$

in which Ω (°C) is the temperature difference at which the rate falls to e⁻¹ of its maximum (A is at half-maximum at Topt ± Ω√ln 2 ≈ Topt ± 0.83Ω); they found Ω ≈ 15–20 °C and fairly constant while Topt tracked growth temperature. Kumarathunge et al. 2019 [8], in a global synthesis of 141 species, used a parabola for net A,

$$A(T) = A_{opt} - b\,(T - T_{opt})^2$$

whose zero crossings Topt ± √(Aopt/b) give a nominal Tmin and Tmax (breadth), and fitted the peaked Arrhenius parameters of Vcmax and Jmax to the same data. Their main results: Topt of A rises with growth temperature by ~0.3–0.5 °C per °C (acclimation) and across species' home climates (adaptation); ΔS of both Vcmax and Jmax acclimates as Kattge & Knorr found, but Ha of Vcmax also varies with home climate; and Jmax/Vcmax declines with growth temperature. Elevated CO₂ raises Topt by 3–7 °C: at high Cc the photorespiratory penalty that grows with temperature is suppressed and the leaf becomes RuBP-limited, whose optimum is set by Jmax's rather than by Rubisco kinetics in air. Conversely, drought (low Ci) lowers Topt. Which parametric form you use matters less than reporting Topt together with the Ci at which it was obtained.

Fitting and comparing treatments

Nonlinear least squares is the workhorse: minimise Σ(Aobs − Amodel)² over the parameters with a Levenberg–Marquardt or Nelder–Mead optimiser, starting from sensible guesses. For A–Ci data the complication is limitation-state assignment, handled three ways: manually by Ci threshold with a consistency check (Sharkey et al. 2007 [5]), exhaustively (Gu et al. 2010 [6]: every admissible partition is fitted and the best is kept), or by estimating the transition point as an extra parameter (Duursma's plantecophys [7], which also provides a bilinear linearised method that is fast and robust). The fitted curve must satisfy admissibility: at each point the rate assigned must indeed be the minimum of the three. A fit that breaks this is wrong regardless of its residuals, and a common symptom is an Aj curve that passes through Rubisco-limited points because J was pulled down to absorb a leak artefact.

Bayesian fitting treats the parameters as distributions with priors (e.g. Jmax/Vcmax ≈ 1.8 ± 0.4, Rd ≈ 1 ± 0.5) and returns posteriors, which handles the weak identifiability of Rd, Tp and gm honestly and allows hierarchical structure (leaves within plants within treatments). It is the natural framework when many curves must be fitted jointly with shared parameters such as Γ* or gm.

Comparing treatments. The replicate is the leaf (or plant), never the point: fitting one curve to pooled points from several leaves hides between-leaf variance and inflates confidence. Fit each curve, extract Vcmax, Jmax (and their standard errors from the Jacobian), then compare with a t-test, ANOVA or a mixed model with plant as a random effect. For the uncertainty of a single curve's parameters, standard errors from the Jacobian understate the real spread because of the parameter correlations; profile likelihood or bootstrapping (resample the residuals, refit, repeat 500–1,000 times, take the 2.5–97.5 percentiles) gives better intervals and reveals the Vcmax–Rd and Vcmax–gm correlations directly. Always compare parameters on the same basis (Ci or Cc; same kinetic constants; same temperature, or normalised to 25 °C with the same temperature functions) and report which fitting method and constants were used; differences between methods can be as large as differences between treatments. Finally, interpret parameter changes with the biology in view: a treatment that lowers Vcmax and Jmax proportionally has probably reduced leaf N or Rubisco activation, one that lowers Asat but not Vcmax has probably acted on gs or gm, and one that changes θ or φ has acted on the light reactions.

Connections to ecosystem models

Response curves are where model parameters come from. The Vcmax,25 and Jmax,25 values in CLM, JULES, ORCHIDEE, BioCro and the TRY database are A–Ci fits; the PFT means of Kattge et al. 2009 and the N and P relationships of Walker et al. 2014 are compiled from thousands of such curves, and the temperature functions of Bernacchi, Medlyn and Kumarathunge come from A–Ci curves measured at several temperatures (the temperature response of a fitted parameter, not an A–T curve of A itself). Model quantum yield (CLM: 0.85 absorptance, θ = 0.7; JULES and Collatz: α = 0.08 mol CO₂ mol⁻¹ photon; BioCro: fixed θ and α) is calibrated against A–Q curves, and A–T curves at two CO₂ levels are the standard test of whether a model's Topt shifts correctly with CO₂, a test most land models pass only because their Γ* follows Bernacchi. Three cautions follow for a modeller. (i) A Ci-based Vcmax belongs in a model with Cc = Ci; the Cc-based value is not "better" for that model, it is inconsistent. (ii) Parameters fitted with a strict minimum are slightly different from those a model with quadratic co-limitation needs; the difference is a few percent in A at the transition. (iii) Eddy-covariance GPP, the usual model benchmark, is a canopy integral of leaves that are mostly not light-saturated, so canopy GPP constrains φ, θ and Jmax more than Vcmax; data-assimilation studies that calibrate Vcmax against fluxes alone should be read with that in mind. Remote-sensing proxies (leaf N, chlorophyll, solar-induced fluorescence) are now used to map Vcmax seasonally, but every one of them is calibrated against gas-exchange curves.

Exam-style questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer.

1. Write a complete protocol for measuring an A–Ci curve on a sun leaf of a C3 crop, justifying each choice.

Choose a fully expanded, recently matured leaf; note its position and time of day (mid-morning, after natural induction). Set the chamber to 400–420 µmol mol⁻¹ reference CO₂, 1,800 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ PPFD (confirmed saturating from an A–Q curve), leaf temperature 25 °C, flow 500 µmol s⁻¹, VPD ≈ 1 kPa (humidity control on), fan high. Acclimate 20–40 min until A and gs are steady (gs > 0.2 mol m⁻² s⁻¹ for a crop). Match the IRGAs. Step CO₂ down 420 → 300 → 200 → 150 → 100 → 50, waiting for stability (2–4 min per step) and matching at each; return to 420 and verify A is within 10% of the start; then step up 500 → 600 → 800 → 1,000 → 1,200 → 1,500 → 2,000. Downward first keeps stomata open and Rubisco active; the return checks drift; the high points are needed to test for TPU. Record Tleaf, gs, Ci, VPD at each point. Afterwards measure the empty-chamber (or dead-leaf) leak across the same CO₂ range and correct, particularly the 50 and 2,000 points. Fit with Bernacchi constants at 25 °C, state whether Ci- or Cc-based, assign limitation states and check admissibility, and report Vcmax, J, Tp (or "not limiting"), Rd, Γ with confidence intervals. Replicate on 4–6 leaves per treatment.

2. An A–Ci curve measured on a small leaf shows A ≈ +1 at Ci ≈ 10 µbar and a plateau that declines gently above 1,000 µbar. Give two explanations for each feature and say how you would distinguish them.

Positive A near zero CO₂ is physically impossible (A must be ≤ −Rd − photorespiration there), so it is an artefact: either a gasket diffusion leak bringing ambient CO₂ (400) into a chamber at 50, which inflates apparent uptake, or a match offset between IRGAs. Distinguish them by an empty-chamber or dead-leaf test across the CO₂ range (a leak scales with the chamber–ambient difference and with the gasket-to-leaf-area ratio; small leaves are worst) and by re-matching at low CO₂ (an offset is constant). The declining plateau is either genuine TPU limitation with αg > 0 (A falls with CO₂ because glycolate export, an alternative outlet for carbon, is suppressed; the signature is reversed O₂ sensitivity: A rises when O₂ is raised) or an artefact of stomatal closure at high CO₂ combined with a leak outward (chamber at 1,500 against ambient 400 loses CO₂, which makes apparent A too low), or Rubisco deactivation over time. Distinguish them by the O₂ test, by measuring the high-CO₂ points quickly and in different orders, by checking gs and the leak correction, and by lowering the temperature (TPU limitation strengthens in the cold).

3. Explain, with equations, how the Laisk, Kok and Yin methods estimate Rd in the light, and the main confounding factor of each.

Laisk: at Cc = Γ* photorespiratory release equals carboxylation, so A = −Rd independent of irradiance. A–Ci curves at three low irradiances (e.g. 60, 120, 250 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) over Ci = 30–120 µbar are nearly linear; their intersection is (Ci*, −Rd). Confounding: the intersection is in Ci, and Ci* = Γ* − Rd/gm, so Γ* is underestimated unless gm is known; the lines also rarely cross at exactly one point, so a regression of slopes and intercepts is used. Kok: the A–Q curve has a break at 20–40 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ because light inhibits respiration; extrapolating the linear region above the break to Q = 0 gives −RL, and the region below gives −Rdark. Confounding: Ci rises as Q falls, which increases photorespiration-free carboxylation and curves the line, so measure at constant Ci or correct for it (Kirschbaum & Farquhar 1987); the break is also partly an artefact of changing ΦPSII. Yin: measure A and ΦPSII simultaneously under non-photorespiratory conditions (2% O₂, high CO₂) at several low irradiances and regress A on ¼ΦPSIIQabs (electron transport ÷ 4 = gross carboxylation); the intercept is −RL and the slope the calibration between fluorescence and gas-exchange J. Confounding: requires the fluorescence J to be proportional to CO₂-fixing electron flow (no alternative sinks) and a correct absorptance and PSII fraction; sensitive to leak errors at low A.

4. Sketch A–T curves at ambient and elevated CO₂ and explain mechanistically why Topt shifts.

Both curves are humped. The ambient curve peaks near 25 °C for a temperate C3 leaf; the elevated-CO₂ curve is higher at every temperature and peaks 3–7 °C warmer, so the stimulation by CO₂ grows with temperature. Mechanism: A = Vc(1 − Γ*/Cc) − Rd. With temperature, Vcmax and Jmax rise (Arrhenius), but Γ* rises (specificity falls, solubility ratio shifts) and Kc(1 + O/Ko) rises faster than Ko, so at ambient Cc (~250 µbar) the photorespiratory term Γ*/Cc and the substrate limitation both grow and overtake the capacity gains at a modest temperature. At elevated Cc (~600 µbar) Γ*/Cc is smaller and grows more slowly, the substrate term is nearer saturation, and the leaf is RuBP-regeneration-limited, where the relevant optimum is that of Jmax (shifted upward by the Γ* terms in 4Cc + 8Γ*). The decline above the optimum is then set by the peaked functions of Vcmax, Jmax and, in practice, Rubisco activase and stomatal closure under rising VPD. Growth temperature also shifts Topt (0.3–0.5 °C per °C) through ΔS acclimation; this is a separate effect that must not be confused with the CO₂ effect.

5. A colleague reports that Vcmax differs significantly between two nitrogen treatments (p < 0.01) based on one pooled curve fit per treatment using all points from six leaves. Criticise the analysis and propose a correct one.

Pooling points from six leaves and fitting one curve treats the 80-odd points as independent replicates when the experimental unit is the leaf (or plant); between-leaf variance is hidden, the standard error of Vcmax is wildly underestimated and the p-value is meaningless. Pooling also mixes leaves with different Γ, gs and limitation transitions, which distorts the fitted shape. Correct analysis: fit each leaf separately with the same method, constants and basis (state Ci vs Cc), check admissibility of each fit, obtain each leaf's Vcmax and Jmax (with bootstrap or profile intervals so that poorly constrained fits can be identified and down-weighted or excluded), then compare treatment means with a t-test or, if plants contributed several leaves, a mixed model with plant as random effect. Report effect size with a confidence interval, not just p. Also check that the treatments were measured at the same leaf temperature and light, that gs was adequate (low-N leaves often have low gs, and patchiness would bias Ci), and that leaf N was measured so that Vcmax per unit N can be compared; a proportional change in Vcmax and Jmax with N is the expected result, a change in their ratio would be more interesting.

6. What is the RACiR technique, how does it work, and what are its limits?

Rapid A–Ci Response (Stinziano et al. 2017) replaces discrete CO₂ steps with a continuous ramp of reference CO₂ (e.g. 20 → 1,500 µmol mol⁻¹ over 5–10 min) while logging A every second or two. Because the gas in the chamber and tubing cannot equilibrate during a ramp, the measured ΔCO₂ lags the true value; the lag depends on ramp rate, flow and chamber volume but not on the leaf, so a ramp with the empty chamber is fitted with a low-order polynomial and subtracted from the leaf ramp to give corrected A and Ci. The result is hundreds of points per curve in a fraction of the time, which enables large surveys of Vcmax and J (for example along canopy profiles or over seasons) with fitted values typically within a few percent of steady-state ones. Limits: the correction must be made with the same ramp, flow and chamber as the leaf; the leaf itself is not at steady state (stomata and Rubisco activation lag), so low-CO₂ points are least reliable; TPU and Rd are poorly resolved; match offsets and leaks are harder to detect; and it needs an instrument with fast CO₂ control (LI-6800-class).

7. Derive the light compensation point and the light-saturated rate from the non-rectangular hyperbola, and explain what θ represents physiologically.

Write gross assimilation P(Q) as the smaller root of θP² − (φQ + Asat)P + φQAsat = 0, with A = P − Rd. The compensation point satisfies P(QLCP) = Rd; substituting P = Rd into the quadratic and solving for Q gives QLCP = Rd(Asat − θRd)/[φ(Asat − Rd)], which reduces to Rd/φ when Rd ≪ Asat. At Q → ∞ the smaller root tends to Asat (the other root grows as φQ/θ), so the saturated net rate is Asat − Rd; at Q → 0 the root tends to φQ, so the initial slope is φ. θ is the convexity: θ = 0 gives a rectangular hyperbola (Michaelis–Menten shape, reaches only Asat/2 at the intersection of the asymptotes), θ = 1 gives the Blackman limiting-factor response (two straight lines). Physiologically θ expresses how uniformly the leaf's chloroplasts become light-saturated: if photosynthetic capacity were distributed through the leaf exactly in proportion to the light each layer absorbs, every layer would saturate together and θ → 1; the steeper the light gradient relative to the capacity gradient (strongly absorbed blue light, thick leaves, low CO₂ which makes the top layers Rubisco-limited), the lower θ (Ögren & Evans 1993). It is therefore a leaf-level, not a chloroplast-level, parameter and should not be given a fixed universal value in canopy models without thought.

8. Why can the same A–Ci dataset yield Vcmax values that differ by 30% between two laboratories, and which differences are legitimate?

Legitimate differences of definition: Ci-based versus Cc-based (20–50%); different kinetic constants (Bernacchi 2001 vs 2002, or von Caemmerer 2000) and Γ*; different temperature functions used to normalise to 25 °C; whether Rd was fitted or fixed; whether the fit used a strict minimum or quadratic co-limitation; and the limitation-state assignment (how many points were given to Ac), especially when the transition is ambiguous. Illegitimate differences: leaks and match errors not corrected, points at non-steady state, inadmissible fits, and fitting with J fixed to a value that was not saturating. The remedy is to publish the raw data with the fitting code and constants, report both Ci- and Cc-based values where gm is available, and give confidence intervals; comparisons across studies should then be made on a common basis, which is precisely what compilations such as Kattge et al. 2009 and Kumarathunge et al. 2019 had to do by refitting the original curves.
